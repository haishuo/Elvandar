# The Government of Uratha

## Government and Political Structure

Uratha's entire society is structured like a massive university, complete with departments, faculties, and a rigid hierarchical system that determines every citizen's place.

### The Council of Provosts

The ruling body of Uratha consists of the heads of each Tower — the great academic institutions that form both the physical and intellectual spine of Urathan civilization. Each Provost holds dual authority as both head of their Tower's scholarly discipline and as a provincial governor in all but name:

- **The High Provost** — Currently Elaris Venn, head of the Amethyst Tower (Theoretical Magic), who serves as first among equals on the Council
- **The War Provost** — Kandor Belus, head of the Ruby Tower, responsible for defense and martial training; often seen as the most practical-minded member
- **The History Provost** — Iyanna Mereth, head of the Sapphire Tower, keeper of historical records and traditional knowledge
- **The Sciences Provost** — Tolrin Fax, head of the Topaz Tower, overseeing applied sciences and technological development
- **The Agriculture Provost** — Daven Harth, head of the Emerald Tower, managing food production, distribution, and natural sciences
- **The Medicine Provost** — Nessa Lind, head of the Pearl Tower, overseeing health services and medical research
- **The Foreign Affairs Provost** — Galvin Tarus, head of the Onyx Tower, handling diplomatic relations — and rather more besides
- **The Scholar-King** — Presides over all from the Ivory Tower, the administrative heart of Uratha

Each of the seven Towers elects its own Provost. Every Full, Senior, Master, and Grand Scholar on that Tower's faculty has one vote. The faculty's choice takes the Tower's seat on the Council. [Scholarly ranks](Uratha_%20The%20Land%20of%20Scholars.md#the-scholarly-hierarchy)

A Provost serves a renewable five-year term. The Tower's voting faculty may recall its Provost early by a two-thirds vote. The Scholar-King or the Council may send the faculty a formal recommendation for removal with reasons. The decision belongs to the faculty, which may retain its Provost.

Where continued access to sensitive orders poses an immediate danger, the Council may suspend that access. A credible accusation that a Provost has passed military orders to an enemy is grounds for such a suspension. The suspension triggers an emergency session of the Tower's voting faculty to consider recall. A conviction for treason vacates the seat. The Tower elects the replacement whenever a seat falls vacant.

The Council meets weekly in the Central Chamber of the Ivory Tower. Their deliberations follow strict academic protocols, with position papers submitted in advance, formal presentations, and ritualized debate. The Council handles ordinary business by consensus. Any Provost may bring a motion. If another Provost seconds it, the chair must put it to a recorded vote.

On formal motions in which the Scholar-King participates, each of the seven Tower Provosts casts one vote and the Scholar-King casts an eighth. In a tie, the Scholar-King's vote counts twice and the side the Scholar-King supported prevails. A motion of [no confidence](#loss-of-confidence) is decided by the seven Provosts alone.

**The Scholar's Crown.** A permanent transfer of custody requires a Council vote. Draskin carried the Book 3 motion on his casting vote. His mandate required exclusive foreign scholarly access to Hauren. He could negotiate its extent, including the exclusion of the sealed chamber. The [account of the Crown vote](../../Offstage.md#the-vote-on-the-scholars-crown) gives the division and each Tower's reasons.

### The Scholar-King

Elected from among the Council of Provosts, the Scholar-King represents the highest authority in Uratha:

- The current Scholar-King, Leowin Draskin, was elected five years ago from the Department of Cosmological Studies
- The Scholar-King traditionally wears the Scholar's Crown at official functions
- With the Crown's silence, elaborate ceremonies now involve the Crown being briefly displayed but not worn
- The Scholar-King maintains the fiction that they consult privately with the Crown on matters of importance

Leowin is regarded as a competent administrator but uninspiring leader, lacking the visionary qualities of earlier Scholar-Kings. His election represented a compromise between factions on the Council, and his rule has been characterized by maintenance of the status quo rather than innovation.

#### Diplomatic and wartime authority

The Scholar-King represents Uratha abroad and can settle terms within the mandate the Council has granted. In the Hauren negotiations the Provosts had already authorized the exchange of the Crown for exclusive access. Draskin could agree to exclude a chamber from the compact at the table.

During a military emergency the Scholar-King may issue temporary orders governing military service on the authority of the office. Commanders can act on receipt of the order, without waiting for a Council vote. In the Compact War this power allowed Draskin to admit *bakarn* to integrated units for the duration of the emergency. The High Exam continued to determine civilian status. A permanent change to classification requires the Council's assent. #### Election and the return to scholarship

The seven sitting Tower Provosts elect both the Scholar-King and the High Provost in a single ranked ballot. All seven stand, and each ranks the other six. At the end of a term the sitting Scholar-King may also enter the election. If the incumbent stands, each Provost ranks the incumbent alongside the other six candidates. Only the seven Tower seats carry a vote. Every candidate for Scholar-King needs four of those seven votes, on first preferences or through transfers.

The count begins with first preferences. The candidate with the fewest votes drops out, and each ballot counted for that candidate passes to its highest-ranked remaining candidate. For a tie at the bottom, the tellers work backward through earlier rounds. At each count they retain only the candidates tied for the fewest votes among those still being compared. A sole lowest candidate drops out. If the tie persists through the first count, a public drawing of lots decides which of those candidates drops out.

In an open succession, counting continues until two candidates remain, even if one has already won a majority. The finalist with more votes becomes Scholar-King and moves to Ivory. The faculty of the departing Provost's Tower elects a successor to its seat. The runner-up becomes High Provost and keeps their Tower seat.

The Scholar-King serves a ten-year term. The Council expects the scholar it chooses to accept with some reluctance. A decade in the Ivory Tower costs a scholar time with students and can leave an inquiry unfinished for someone else to pursue. Many Provosts feel that sacrifice keenly.

At the end of the term, the customary course is to step down and resume scholarly work. Several Provosts may approach the incumbent privately and ask them to stand again. Taking up that request is honorable. The incumbent is free to enter on their own initiative, though doing so unasked is considered vulgar. Colleagues who approved of a Scholar-King's first term may rank another candidate higher if they think the incumbent too eager to keep the office. An invitation is a sign of support. The election determines whether that support amounts to a majority. Second terms have grown rare in recent centuries.

Once the incumbent wins a majority, the tellers count the original ballots again for High Provost. All seven Tower Provosts start this count. The tellers skip the reelected Scholar-King on every ballot and credit each vote to the highest-ranked Provost listed on it. They apply the same transfer and tie rules, using only rounds from this count for countback. The first Provost to receive four votes becomes High Provost.

#### Loss of confidence

The Council may remove a sitting Scholar-King through a vote of no confidence. Each of the seven Provosts has one vote, including the High Provost. The Scholar-King has none on the motion. Removal requires two-thirds of the full seven seats, or five affirmative votes. An absence or abstention leaves that threshold at five.

Removal takes effect when the motion carries.

A Scholar-King who learns privately that five colleagues will vote for removal has good reason to step down before the session. A public announcement of a return to scholarship gives the incumbent a dignified departure and can spare the Provosts a formal breach with a colleague.

#### Vacancy during a term

Upon the Scholar-King's death, resignation, or removal, the sitting High Provost immediately becomes interim Scholar-King. The vacancy itself calls the seven Provosts into emergency session seven days later to elect both officers. The High Provost serves until the election and stands on the same terms as the other six Provosts.

## Local administration and petitions

Local service officials can spend the funds already appropriated to their service. If a canal bank fails in Agraria, the superintendent of the Irrigation Control Center can close the gates, call out a crew, and pay for repairs from the irrigation budget. The Agriculture Provost answers to the Council for Emerald's irrigation spending across Uratha. If the repair requires funds appropriated to another Tower, the Provost must take the transfer to the Council. [Agraria](The%20Physical%20Geography%20of%20Uratha.md#agraria)

Sapphire-trained magistrates hear petitions concerning the application of existing law. A scholar held by the Ilhamori for unlicensed research can produce the license in question. If it covers the work, the magistrate can order the scholar's release.

The law gives *bakarn* few rights. A worker assigned to a labor detail under another person's name can petition a magistrate directly. The magistrate can order the clerk to correct the roll. The assignment center still decides where the petitioner works, and the curfew applies on the way home. Changing the rules of classification requires the Council's assent.

## The High Exam and *Bakarn* Status

The cornerstone of Urathan society is the High Exam, a comprehensive test that every citizen must take at age sixteen, with profound consequences for the entirety of their life thereafter.

- Those who pass join the scholarly class and can pursue academic advancement
- Those who fail become *bakarn*, or "beast-kin" in the Urathan tongue
- The *bakarn* are considered intellectually inferior and relegated to manual labor and service positions
- The exam is notoriously difficult, with a pass rate of approximately 30%
- Preparation begins in early childhood, with families investing significant resources in tutoring

### Preparation and examination

Early aptitude testing already divides children into school tracks. Children from scholarly households have better-equipped classrooms and can receive private tutoring. Children in the practical track spend those years learning skills that carry little weight in the examination hall. Examiners can also mark an intelligible answer down for imprecision when a child uses a form unfamiliar in Collegiate Urathan. [Primary schools and examination grounds](The%20Physical%20Geography%20of%20Uratha.md#educational-facilities) · [Language and the High Exam](../../Languages/Urathan.md#bakarn-varieties)

The major cities hold the exam annually, with lodging for candidates from outlying settlements. At the same guarded complex, successful candidates find recruiters for the academic departments. Candidates who fail go to a separate processing area for assignment to labor training.

### The Meritocracy Argument

Uratha's official position is that the High Exam is fair. Every citizen sits the same test. Performance reflects capacity. The system rewards merit and only merit. Those who fail had the same opportunity as those who passed; their failure reflects their limitations, not the system's.

This argument is made in complete sincerity by the people who benefit from it, which is part of what makes it so effective and so insidious.

The exam does not test raw intellect. It tests *prepared* intellect — which is to say, it tests how much investment a child's parents were able to make in their education. A scholar's child grows up surrounded by books, educated conversation, and people who model intellectual life as the natural state of existence. A bakarn's child grows up with none of this. The exam then measures both children by the same standard and declares the outcome a reflection of their innate capacity.

Children of scholars pass at dramatically higher rates than children of bakarn. The official explanation is that intelligence is heritable and scholars are intelligent, so of course their children perform better — that's the meritocracy functioning correctly. The circularity of this reasoning is invisible to those inside it because the circularity *is* the ideology. You cannot falsify it from within the framework.

This is precisely the kind of statistical analysis that the Emerald Tower has occasionally attempted to publish. The papers do not clear peer review. Reviewers — who are, by definition, scholars, meaning they passed the exam — consistently identify methodological concerns. The sample sizes. The controls. The framing. There is always something. A junior scholar who attempts this research three times learns, without being told explicitly, what the field rewards and what it does not. They redirect their work. They tell themselves they will return to it when they have more seniority. They rarely return to it.

*We're scholars here, not politicians* — which is the biggest lie in Uratha, delivered with complete conviction by people who have never examined whether their scholarship might itself be a political act.

### The Token

There is always the token.

The token is a bakarn-born scholar — genuinely exceptional, genuinely brilliant, elevated through the system by the identification and investment of a sympathetic faculty member at the right moment. The token is real. Their achievement is real. And they are required, implicitly and permanently, to serve as proof that the system works.

Every time an uncomfortable question is raised about bakarn pipeline disparities, their name comes up. They are no longer a person. They are an argument. Some of them internalize this entirely — it is easier to believe you were simply exceptional enough to clear a fair system than to conclude that everything you suffered was in service of a lie. Others know exactly what they are and live with that knowledge quietly, because the door is behind them now and they didn't build it.

The token almost always ends up elevated to a high-prestige tower. Amethyst. Sapphire. Ivory. Somewhere visible, where their presence can say *see, the heights are open to all.* Not Emerald, where their presence would simply raise further questions about everyone they left behind.

The Emerald Tower has the fewest tokens. This is not an accident.

### The *Bakarn* Experience

For those who fail the High Exam, life follows a dramatically different path:

- Immediate reassignment to labor training programs
- Required to wear identifying markers denoting their status: undyed or coarse grey fabric, with no tower color and no cuff markings — the visual antithesis of the scholar's robe. Every tower color is vivid and named; bakarn wear the color of nothing
- Housed in separate quarters, often in the outskirts of academic centers
- Limited access to certain areas of cities, particularly academic buildings
- Strict curfews and movement restrictions
- Minimal educational opportunities beyond practical skills training

Despite these restrictions, the *bakarn* have developed vibrant subcultures in each major Urathan city. Their folk traditions, practical crafts, and oral histories represent a parallel knowledge system entirely unrecognized by the academic establishment. Some have developed significant practical expertise that scholars depend upon but rarely acknowledge. The Emerald Tower depends on bakarn agricultural labor more directly than any other institution, which is one reason its scholars are better positioned than most to notice when familiar faces disappear without adequate explanation.

In the service quarters, labor assignment centers organize work details and distribution points issue rations. The Council's [Ilhamori](#the-ilhamori-enforcers-of-academic-protocol) pursue escaped *bakarn*. Residents also maintain their own workshops, teaching spaces, and networks for sharing supplies. [The bakarn quarters](The%20Physical%20Geography%20of%20Uratha.md#the-bakarn-quarters)

### Testimony, family, and personal property

The murder of a *bakarn* is prosecuted as murder. A *bakarn* may report a crime and testify against a scholar. A magistrate who believes a *bakarn* witness may convict on that testimony alone. The magistrate judges credibility, and some give a scholar's account more weight because of the speaker's rank.

Urathan law recognizes *bakarn* marriages and parenthood. Parents may raise their children, though a labor reassignment can separate a family even when both spouses object. A scholar and a *bakarn* may marry. Such marriages are socially unusual. Classification attaches to the individual, and the marriage confers none of the scholar's privileges on the *bakarn* spouse. The usual work assignments, curfew, and restrictions on access to academic buildings still apply.

A *bakarn* may own personal belongings, including tools, and leave them to a child or another heir. Taking those belongings is theft under the same law that protects a scholar's possessions.

### The *Bakarn* Question
- The growing bakarn population shows signs of discontent
- Underground networks have developed for sharing information and resources
- Some bakarn communities have developed practical innovations that scholars unknowingly depend upon
- A few radical scholars have begun questioning the ethical foundations of the system — concentrated, unsurprisingly, in the Emerald Tower
- The system is structurally dependent on the people it has systematically degraded, which is a vulnerability no one in power wants to examine

### Military service during the Compact War

After the bombardment of the Ivory Tower, the Scholar-King suspended *bakarn* classification for military service and authorized integrated units. Some who fought beside scholars received commendations. The military exception lapsed after the war. The High Exam continued, and the committees considering reform left the division between scholar and *bakarn* substantially intact. ## The Ilhamori: Enforcers of Academic Protocol

To maintain Uratha's system of regulated free inquiry, the nation employs **the Ilhamori**, formally **Vela Theraris, Rava Eshais va Ilhamori Kethae** — “The Constituted Commission for Licensed Inquiry, Civic Order, and Extraordinary Mandates.” The short formal title is **Ilhamori Kethae**, “Commission for Extraordinary Mandates”; ordinary speech drops the generic commission-word and calls the service **the Ilhamori**. The name is pronounced **il-ha-MO-ri**; `Languages/Urathan.md` holds its linguistic analysis. This elite force serves as magical police and investigative bureau combined:

- They enforce the rulings of the Council of Provosts
- They hunt those who practice unsanctioned magic or conduct unregistered research
- Their authority extends throughout Urathan territory
- They report directly to the Council of Provosts, bypassing normal departmental hierarchies
- Their training combines magical combat, investigation techniques, and academic knowledge
- They are recruited exclusively from the scholarly class, with preference for those with Onyx Tower backgrounds or multi-disciplinary training
- When a scholar joins the Ilhamori, they shed their tower robe entirely; institutional identity replaces disciplinary identity. Their livery is a deep ink-blue — darker and more uniform than Sapphire Tower's blue — with no tower-color body and no cuff rank bands. A silver service pin at the collar marks their commission. Most Urathans read the deep blue as an extension of law and order, which is the Sapphire Tower's domain; that Ilhamori recruits come predominantly from the Onyx Tower is not publicly documented, and the color misdirection is not accidental

Ilhamori officers are feared and respected in equal measure. While they do not prohibit any field of study outright, they are merciless in punishing those who circumvent Uratha's bureaucratic requirements for research. Their presence ensures that while knowledge may be free, access to it remains tightly controlled by the academic hierarchy.

The organization has several specialized divisions:

- **The Archivists** — Specialize in monitoring library access and preventing unauthorized duplication of restricted texts
- **The Questioners** — Focus on interrogation and information extraction, particularly skilled in mind-reading magics
- **The Trackers** — Experts in locating fugitive scholars and escaped *bakarn*
- **The Nullifiers** — Trained to contain and neutralize dangerous magical experiments
- **The Auditors** — Review research proposals and conduct surprise inspections of active projects

Several of the highest-ranking Ilhamori officers are secretly aware of the Globehall's existence and serve as its guardians, though most members of the organization remain ignorant of this abomination. The pipeline from the Onyx Tower to the Ilhamori means that the people best equipped to uncover the Globehall's existence are precisely the people most institutionally committed to concealing it.
