The Mistress came up at dawn.

Elara sent for her before the sky had finished changing, which meant somebody had to go down into the Warrens in the dark with a message and a lamp and find a woman who did not advertise where she slept, and it was done inside two hours, and Xion never learned how.

He had assumed, when Elara said send for her, that they would go down. It was what he would have advised. An empress descending into the tunnels the morning after would have been a gesture the whole city could read and half the city would have approved of.

"No," Elara said. "I'm not paying her in theatre. I said she would hear it from me. She'll hear it from me sitting down."

So she came up.

Xion stood in the corner of a small room in a merchant's house near the wall and watched a woman he had first seen in a chamber four hundred metres under the city walk through a door in the low city at the sixth hour of the morning, and understood, from nothing more than the way she came through it, that she had not been above ground in a very long time.

She did not look around. That was the thing. Everyone looks around in a room they have not been in. She came in, found the chair, sat in it, and put her hands on her knees, and the only concession she made to the whole of it was that she did not take the seat with its back to the window.

"Empress."

"Mistress."

"You've had a night."

"I've had several," Elara said. "This one was worse than the others."

Then she told her.

It took a quarter of an hour, and Xion sat through all of it with his hands flat on his thighs and did not say one word, because his function in that room was to be a second person who had been there and could be looked at if the account was doubted, and the account was not doubted.

She did not soften it. That was what he took away, afterward, and kept: that a woman who had been awake for a day and a half, who had been on her hands and knees on the base of a machine at the bottom of the world, sat in a borrowed chair and gave a criminal the truth in order, at the pace it could be taken in, and did not once reach for the version that would have been easier to say.

Lathion was real, and it was a city, and it had been a city for five thousand years.

It had not been abandoned. It had been emptied, and it had emptied itself, and it had done so twenty years ago.

The Rending had not stopped by a miracle. It had been stopped, from underneath, deliberately, by the people whose city that was, using a machine they had built in the last four months of their lives for that one purpose, and the machine had used them to do it.

The brass door the Long Knives had held for generations was the back door of a tomb, and the people in it had gone into it walking.

The Mistress did not interrupt. Her face did not do anything at all. Xion, who had spent eight years learning to read what a body does when it is being told something it cannot afford to react to, watched her hands and saw nothing in them either, and revised his estimate of her upward, which he had already done twice.

When it was finished she was quiet for long enough that Elara let the quiet stand.

"Two questions," the Mistress said.

"Ask them."

"Did they suffer."

Elara did not answer straight away, and Xion knew what the pause was, because it was the same pause she had come to a stop at in the room upstairs when he had asked her almost the same thing in different words.

"No," she said. "I've thought about whether I'm telling you that because it's true or because it's what people say. It's true. It was not done to them. They did it."

The Mistress nodded once.

"Second. The door we've kept." Her voice did not change. "Was there ever anything on the other side of it that wanted keeping out?"

"No."

"Right," the Mistress said, and something went out of her shoulders that Xion suspected had been there since before he was born.

That was all she asked.

She did not ask what was in the Repository, or what it was worth, or what it would do to the price of anything, and Xion — who had spent a season with people whose first question was always some version of that — found he had to sit still for a moment and let it pass.

And she did not ask the other thing.

He was certain she had seen the shape of it. Elara had given a complete account of a city and had said nothing whatsoever about how she came to be standing at the centre of that account, and there was a hole in it the size of a person, and the Mistress had been listening to people leave things out of stories for longer than either of them had been alive.

She looked at it. Xion watched her look at it.

Then she put her hands on her knees again and stood up.

"You've kept your side," she said. "Note it down somewhere, if you keep that kind of book. I don't."

---

She was gone by the seventh hour and it did not matter, because the seventh hour was already an hour later than the morning could afford.

Draskin's delegation had been assembled since the sixth. The reception was called for noon and everything before noon was a chain of small things that had to happen in order — the household, the protocol officers, two hours of briefing Xion had been promised and got fifty minutes of, the attendants, the clothes — and the quarter of an hour with the Mistress had come out of the front of it and pushed everything back against the wall.

They started late.

Not by much. Not by anything that would appear in a record, and the Urathans were shown into the reception chamber with the courtesies performed correctly and nobody said anything at all. But it was fourteen minutes, and Xion, coming in at Elara's shoulder, watched one of their own advisors do the arithmetic across the room — a man with a good face for a table and a bad one for a corridor — and could see him arriving at the question and failing to find any answer to it.

There was nothing in Kaha'an that outranked the Scholar-King of Uratha at noon. The man knew that. He had run the list and come up short and was now looking at his Empress with an expression Xion recognised from his father's rooms, which was the expression of a professional discovering that he does not have all the information and being unable to decide whether that is an insult or a warning.

Nobody was going to tell him.

Xion had thought about that, briefly, while the attendants were doing the last of the fastenings: that Elara had made a head of state wait, at a cost she would carry and could never claim, in order to keep a promise made in a tunnel to a woman who had no standing anywhere in the world and no way at all to enforce it.

He put it where he put things. The pile was extremely large by now, and this one went near the top.

---

He had slept two hours.

He established this in the dressing room, in front of a glass, while two attendants worked on him with the deep unhurried competence of people who have dressed better men for worse mornings, and what he was actually doing was the calculation he did before every room that mattered, which was how much of himself was available.

The answer was enough.

It was not a good answer. It was the answer, and he had learned in council rooms and in the Warrens and in a chamber under the ground that the difference between enough and plenty is generally invisible from the other side of a table.

He had two hours of sleep, eleven days of Lathion, and the whole of a night in a borrowed room listening to a woman describe watching her parents die from underneath.

He was what was available.

---

The room was Elara's choice and she made it in the four minutes between the Mistress going out and the attendants coming in, which was, Xion thought, about as much deliberation as it needed.

Not the throne room. The throne room was for declaring things.

They took the long reception chamber off the east colonnade instead — big enough for two delegations, small enough that everybody could hear a sentence spoken at ordinary volume, and furnished with nothing that decided anything in advance. No dais. No single chair higher than the others. A table you could put paper on.

Draskin would read it. That was the point of it.

Elara came out of her chambers at ten minutes to the hour, and Xion looked at her once and then made a point of not doing it again.

She was functioning.

That was the whole of the word and there was not a better one. The face was on: the specific stillness she had learned somewhere in the first six weeks of her reign and had by now made into an instrument, and it was convincing, and it would be convincing to Draskin, who was very good.

Underneath it was a woman who twelve hours ago had said I'm sorry it took us so long to an empty chamber and had meant it, and who was not going to be able to feel anything that was not that chamber for some while yet.

He could see both. He would have preferred, on the whole, not to be able to.

She caught his eye at the door. She did not smile. There was no reason to smile and neither of them had ever needed the gesture.

They went in.

---

Leowin Draskin came into the chamber before protocol required it.

It was a small thing and Xion caught it and turned it over twice while the greetings were being performed. The Scholar-King of Uratha does not enter a foreign reception chamber ahead of his own delegation's ceremonial mark; it is not done, not because it is forbidden but because there is no reason to. Draskin had come through the door at the front of his people like a man arriving at a meeting he had been waiting three days for.

He had not aged in the season since the Ivory Tower. He had the same containment, the same absence of anything courtly, the same stillness of a man holding a large idea steady in his head.

His eyes went to Elara, and Xion — watching for it, because watching was his function — saw the small correction.

Draskin had been told the expedition came up in the night. He had built a picture of what would walk into the room, and what walked in did not match it, and he adjusted without any part of his face admitting that an adjustment had taken place.

He expected to be given an advantage this morning, Xion thought, and he has just been told he does not have one.

At Draskin's right hand was a woman Xion had not met.

"High Provost Venn," Draskin said. "Who will hold the academic side of anything we agree."

She was perhaps fifty. She had the flat courtesy of somebody who has been introduced a great many times and does not find it interesting, and she did the correct thing with her hands, and then she looked at the room.

Not at the people. At the room — the length of it, the doors, the table, the light through the colonnade windows, the way the chairs had been set. Her attention went along it the way a quartermaster's goes along a manifest, and it did not stop anywhere, and it did not come back.

Xion filed her under monitor before she had said a word to him.

---

He had spent two weeks building for the wrong meeting.

He understood that inside ninety seconds, which was fast, and it was still not fast enough to be useful.

Everything on the Balishan side of that table was braced for a fight about access. That was the meeting the dispatches had bought: Uratha had read Ormuth's careful measured report and then whatever came after it, had grasped the size of the thing, and was arriving to demand a great deal more than the spring treaty had granted. Scholars, numbers, jurisdiction, credentialing, the right to bring their own people and their own rules. Xion had drafted for it. He had a document, and three fallback positions, and a rank order of what Balisha could stand to lose.

Draskin set all of it aside without appearing to notice that it was there.

"Before we come to the city," he said, "I want to close something else, because I do not want it standing behind this conversation while we have it."

And then he named the Scholar's Crown.

---

He did it without any ceremony whatever, which was itself the ceremony.

Three hundred and some years of it. The Separation, and what went north when the Separation happened, and the fact — he used the word fact, not dispute — that every Balishan sovereign since had asked for the Crown back and every Scholar-King had declined. He gave the history in about forty seconds and he did not make Balisha's side of it sound unreasonable, which no Urathan had ever bothered to do.

Then he said: "I would like to resolve it. Today."

Xion was aware of the room going very slightly wrong around him, and it took him a moment to identify why, and when he identified it he had to work not to look at Elara.

She gave it up.

Not a predecessor. Not a century ago. Her, in the spring, in the Ivory Tower, in a sealed instrument with her own name at the bottom of it: the demand withdrawn, formally and permanently, with an acknowledgment on the record that Uratha held the Crown legitimately and that no future Balishan sovereign would revive the claim. It had been the price of the expedition. Xion had built the thing himself and had watched her put it on the table without decorating it, and had been proud of her for it, and would have advised it again.

Which meant that the Empress of Balisha could not now open her mouth about the Scholar's Crown.

She had no standing. There was nothing left to ask for; the asking had been signed away with her own hand at Draskin's insistence, and the instrument was sitting in a case in this building.

So Draskin had to raise it. Only Draskin could raise it.

He had, and he had done it in the first minute, before the city, before terms, before anybody could be accused of trading one for the other.

"Uratha will return the Scholar's Crown," Draskin said. "Permanent transfer of custody, full ceremony, on the record, and I will say plainly in that ceremony that it was Uratha's to give and that we gave it. In exchange, I want expanded and exclusive Urathan scholarly access to Lathion, formalised in a compact whose terms we can settle in this room today."

He let that sit for exactly as long as it needed.

"The Crown," he said, "for the city."

---

Nobody said anything.

Xion had been at a great many tables by now and had never before been in a silence with that particular texture. It was not shock; the Balishan advisors were too well trained for shock. It was eight people simultaneously discovering that the document they had all read on the way in was about something else.

He made himself go through it slowly, because the size of a thing is exactly when a man stops thinking.

The Crown had been gone longer than the Kemvimore house had existed. It had been the standing grievance of the Balishan court for three centuries — not a live wound, nothing so useful as that, but the low permanent ache of a state that had lost an argument by having its opponent decline to have it. Every sovereign had asked. Every sovereign had been refused, courteously, in writing, on grounds that never varied and never had to.

And in the spring, Elara had done the only intelligent thing anybody in that line had ever done with it, which was to notice that the demand had never once been enforceable and to sell it for something she could actually use.

Now the man who had required that sale was offering the thing back.

Unasked. Having crossed a continent in person, which he did not do. Having then sat in the diplomatic quarter for three days waiting for a woman to come up out of the ground.

Xion looked at the shape of it as a lawyer would, because that was the nearest instrument to hand.

The spring instrument barred Balisha from demanding. It said nothing whatsoever about Uratha giving. There was no claim left to settle, and no negotiation to be had over a claim, because the claim was dead and buried by Balisha's own signature.

Which meant that what was happening in this room could not be a settlement.

It could only be a gift.

He got that far and stopped, because Draskin was waiting and there was work to do, and because the next thought after that one had not arrived yet and would not for some hours.

---

Elara let it stand a moment longer than comfort and not long enough to be a tactic.

"Thank you for your candour," she said, and the word did the work it was chosen for. "I would like the Counselor to take the terms."

It was choreography and Draskin read it correctly, because he read everything correctly: the First Counselor would do the granular work, the Empress held the authority, and nothing that happened in the next two hours was final until she said it was.

Xion also knew that she had just bought herself thirty seconds inside her own face.

He took them.

"Then let me say back what I think you have offered," he said, "before we agree to any part of it."

---

He restated it slowly.

It is the oldest move there is and it works for two reasons, and one of them is that it confirms both sides are discussing the same object, and the other is that it gives the man doing the restating a minute in which nobody expects him to be clever.

Draskin listened without a flicker. He had used the same move himself in the spring.

Venn listened differently.

When Xion said expanded and exclusive, something in her attention came up half a degree — not at the words, he thought, but at the joint underneath them, the way a carpenter's eye goes to a joint while a room is being praised. It was gone as fast as it arrived.

He filed it and kept talking.

---

The first term was sovereignty and it was not negotiable and he said so.

Balisha's administrative sovereignty over Lathion, stated in the compact, early, in plain words, not implied and not left to interpretation. The city was Balishan ground. Access was a permission, and a permission is a thing that has an owner.

"Yes," Draskin said.

Xion waited for the rest of it.

There was no rest of it.

He had built four arguments for that clause and had expected to spend forty minutes and one of the fallbacks getting it. Draskin had conceded the single most consequential structural term in the document in under two seconds, in a tone he might have used to agree about the weather, and had then sat waiting, politely, for the next item.

A concession given without resistance is either honest or worthless.

He did not know which this was. He wrote the clause down and went on to artifacts.

---

Artifacts took longer, and Venn came into it.

Anything found in Lathion — object, text, material, mechanism — remained under Balishan jurisdiction. Urathan scholars could study, catalogue, copy, publish. They could remove nothing from the city without the Empress's written approval, given case by case.

Venn wanted to push. Xion saw the whole of it arrive in her and saw her decline to let it out — the small settling of a person putting something back down — and what she said instead was a technical question about the classification of copies, which he answered, and which she accepted.

That restraint told him more than anything she had said. She was working under instruction and the instruction was: do not lose this over the small things.

He went on to exclusivity braced for the hardest part of the morning.

---

It was not the hardest part, but it should have been, and the reason it was not is a thing nobody in the room said out loud.

Draskin wanted exclusive Urathan scholarly access. No other foreign academic body, no third-party study, no arrangement with anybody else while the compact ran.

Xion opened his mouth to be cautious about it, because exclusive arrangements have a way of settling in and becoming furniture, and because it was the sort of promise a state makes cheaply in the spring and cannot deliver by the autumn.

Except that Balisha could deliver it.

The crown did not rent a right of way to the Brass Door. Xion had bought the north four shafts and the third tier off Ronas Dermir in the summer, for four months, in writing, in terms that survived the man who signed them — and he had bought them closed. That clause was in the instrument. It was the only part of that entire filthy afternoon he had gone back and read twice.

An exclusive promise over a site reached by a road anybody could hire is not worth the paper. A scholar-state's negotiators would establish that inside a week, and then the compact would be worth nothing, and the next conversation would be much worse than this one.

He said none of it. There was no version of saying it that did not amount to explaining to a foreign power exactly how narrow the crown's grip on its own approach was.

He was still holding the caution in his mouth, arranging it, when Elara said, from across the table:

"Yes."

He looked at her.

She did not elaborate and she did not look back at him, and after a moment he understood the arithmetic and found no fault in it. One party with structured access was better than three competing for it. Gunastran was not in the picture and would very much like to be. Exclusivity granted to a known quantity beat an auction among unknown ones, and an auction was the only other thing on offer.

It was the right call and she had made it in a second and a half.

It was also, he noted, a statement to Draskin about whose negotiation this was.

He adjusted, and went on.

---

The framework took two hours.

Numbers on site at any one time. Credentialing, and who held it, and what happened when it was withdrawn. Supervision. Reporting. Publication, and the lag between a finding and its publication, which took twenty minutes on its own. Dispute resolution. Renewal, and on what cadence, and what constituted cause not to renew.

It was the part that nobody outside a room like that one has ever cared about and the part that decides whether an agreement holds or becomes the grievance somebody's grandchildren inherit. Xion enjoyed it, in the specific way he had learned to enjoy this kind of work, which was not enjoyment so much as the absence for two hours of anything else.

Draskin did it himself. He did not delegate to Venn and he did not defer to her, and he was very good at it — precise, unhurried, entirely without vanity, conceding small things quickly and holding the two or three that mattered until he had something for them.

Venn came in twice, both times on academic classification, both times in language so exact and so undramatic that Xion granted her what she asked before he had finished noticing that she had asked for anything.

He thought about that afterward. At the time he only registered that she knew precisely what she wanted and described it in terms that made it easy to give her, which is a thing you learn by wanting it for a very long time.

---

Then he raised the chamber, because Draskin had not.

That was the tell and it was not subtle to a man who had been listening for two hours. Draskin had not asked what the Empress had sealed. He had not asked why the expedition came up weeks early. He had built an entire access framework without once enquiring about the one thing in Lathion that the compact would need to be silent about, and a man does not fail to ask a question that size by accident.

He was leaving it implicit. He intended the access terms to be broad enough that the sealed door could be raised later, in a smaller room, on a quieter day, by somebody other than himself.

Xion closed it.

"One exclusion," he said. "The Empress has sealed a chamber in Lathion by imperial command. It sits outside this agreement entirely. No Urathan scholar approaches it, attempts entry, surveys or measures its exterior, or refers to its existence in published work, without written authority from the Empress herself." He set his pen down. "That is not a term I am able to trade against anything else in this document."

The room got very quiet.

Draskin did not flinch and did not look at Venn, and Xion watched him price the thing in real time, which took perhaps three seconds and was the most interesting three seconds of the morning.

"Accepted," Draskin said.

No counter. No request for the exclusion's boundary to be defined in metres, which was the obvious move and the one Xion had prepared for. No enquiry as to what was in it.

There was a disturbance in him. It was very small and Xion caught it only because he had spent the whole morning waiting for something to catch: the particular flatness of a man who came to a table expecting to have something to bargain with and has discovered the line was drawn before he arrived in the country.

It did not stop him. It changed the temperature of the room by about a degree, and the degree stayed changed.

And beside him, Venn's shoulders came down.

Not much. Not relief that the chamber had been excluded — Xion had been ready to read that and it was not what he read. It was the release of somebody who has been braced for a bill and has just been handed a smaller one.

She was expecting the Empress to close off something larger.

He had no idea what. He wrote the clause in and did not look at her again.

---

Draskin was gracious at the end, and the warmth in it was real.

"Your predecessors," he said to Elara, "asked correctly, and at the correct intervals, for three hundred years." He was assembling the sentence as he went, which Xion had not seen him do before. "I have read the whole correspondence. It is a great deal of very good letter-writing." A pause. "I think we had been waiting for somebody we could give it to."

He did not say waiting for somebody capable. He came within a word of it and stopped, and let the shape stand there.

Elara received it in the same body that had received the vision, and her face did not move.

"Thank you," she said.

---

The signing was preliminary text only.

The full compact would be drawn properly and executed at ceremony, and the Crown would not move until then — Draskin would send word north tonight, and the Ivory Tower would prepare it, and it would come south under Urathan escort at whatever pace the escort of a thing like that requires, and be given to Elara in Kaha'an when it arrived.

He had not brought it. Xion had wondered, and thought about it, and concluded that a man who brought Uratha's most sacred object across a continent in advance of an agreement would be a man announcing that he expected to be told yes, and Draskin had never in his life told anybody what he expected.

He had brought himself instead. Which had said the same thing and cost him more.

The pen came out of its case — lacquered wood, worn pale at the grip, the nib reset once by somebody good — and there was the small correct pause while everybody understood they were being shown something.

Then Draskin put the nib to the parchment and signed.

Xion was watching his hands, because he had been watching his hands for two hours and because he had promised himself in the spring that he would watch them the next time.

There was nothing.

No hesitation. Not the two seconds he had caught in the Ivory Tower and checked afterward with two separate men who had both told him nothing at all had happened. The nib went down and the signature came out precise and unhurried and there was no cost anywhere in it.

In the spring, a man committed to something that hurt him had paused before signing a treaty that gave up nothing.

This morning, the same man had given back the Scholar's Crown of Uratha and his hand had not changed pace.

---

The Urathans withdrew with the courtesies done properly, and word would go north before dark, and the advisors found reasons to drift toward the doors in the way that senior people do when they can tell that the room is finished with them.

Elara stood looking at the preliminary terms lying on the table with the ink still drying.

"That should feel like a victory," she said.

"It is one."

"I know." She did not move. "I'd like to be told when it starts."

---

There was a message in the anteroom, and it had been there since before noon, and Xion read it standing up.

The Factor-Principal of House Greystone requested an audience with the crown at the crown's convenience, at whatever hour suited, on any day in the coming fortnight; and the consortium on whose behalf he wrote would welcome an early opportunity to discuss terms.

Xion read it twice.

In the spring he had sat at the long table in the south gallery and explained to the Empress of Balisha, in front of her council, why the money in this city would not lend to her. Three reasons, in the order a lender would set them down. No record on a six-week-old court. A sum that was a rebuild and not a bridge. And a street that had watched the largest private fortune in Kaha'an leave a house in a single night with the crown's blessing and no court ever asked whether it was lawful.

None of that had changed. Not one figure in the accounts was different. The water still held nine months and not fourteen.

What had changed was that the crown now had something to pledge, and the street had heard what it was.

He put the message in the folder for the afternoon and did not mention it to anybody, and could not have said, if pressed, why the thing sitting in his chest was not relief.

The house that would not see him in the spring wanted an appointment on the strength of a city full of dead people.

---

They walked back through the east colonnade with the sun in the south and the day still with hours in it.

"Tell me the bad part," Elara said. "You've been carrying it since the sovereignty clause."

"The deal is good." He wanted that established first because it was true. "The terms are clean, the framework will hold, the exclusion will hold, and I would not change a line of it."

"But."

He took a while.

"In the spring," he said, "when you withdrew the Crown demand — I was watching him, because that was what I was there for. And something in him let go. Shoulders and hands. The way a man goes when you finally give him something for a pain he has stopped noticing he was in." He looked down the colonnade. "I filed it. I've had it since Uratha. I could not tell you what it meant then and I can't now."

"And?"

"And this morning that same man crossed a continent to give it back."

Elara did not say anything.

"Those two things do not fit inside one person," Xion said. "I have been trying to make them fit since he opened his mouth and they will not go. A man does not go slack with relief when a claim on a thing is finally dead, and then travel a thousand miles to hand over the thing." He heard his own voice and made it flatter. "There are three explanations. He is simply that disciplined, which he might be. Or the Crown is worth a great deal less to Uratha than it has been worth to us for three hundred years, which would be an extraordinary thing to be true and would explain everything. Or there is a third one and I am not standing anywhere I could see it from."

"Which do you think?"

"I think," he said, "that I am the only man alive who saw both. Every other Balishan in that chamber missed the pen. I checked afterwards. I asked two of them separately and neither of them had seen anything at all."

They walked.

"There's a second thing," he said. "Smaller. The Provost."

"Venn."

"She wants Lathion the way a nation of scholars would want it, and that reading covers everything she did today, and I believe it." He turned it over as he said it, checking the join. "But I could not find the bottom of her. With most people who want something badly, you get there eventually — you can see the whole of what they want and roughly what they would pay, and after that they're legible. I never got there with her. It kept going back."

"Back into what?"

"I don't know. That's the difficulty. It isn't that she was hiding it. It's that it went further than academic appetite goes, and I don't have anywhere to put an observation like that."

He had the two of them side by side now, the easiness and the Provost, and no folder to put them in and no name for the folder.

"So we watch," Elara said.

"So we watch."
