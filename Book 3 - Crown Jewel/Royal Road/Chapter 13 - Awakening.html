It was the fourth item of eleven, and it took four minutes.

The Gunastran envoy had come through the ordinary channels, with the ordinary notice, and had been given the ordinary room — the small audience chamber off the north corridor where the crown heard things that did not require the hall. No state visit. No delegation. One man with good boots and a folder, lodging a working request on behalf of the Industrial Consortium.

He put it well. Xion, standing at Elara's left with the morning's schedule in his hand and eleven items on it, noted that the man put it very well indeed: scholarly and technical access to Lathion, framed in the language of shared advancement, with two specific proposals about metallurgical survey that were plainly the work of somebody who had read everything published on the subject in the last two months and had understood most of it.

He was able. He was well briefed. He had come a long way to ask a reasonable question in a reasonable manner.

"I'm sorry," Elara said. "I can't."

She did not decorate it either.

"We concluded an instrument with Uratha five weeks ago. It grants exclusive scholarly access and it excludes third foreign bodies by name. It isn't a preference and I'm not able to make an exception for you; there is no clause I could reach for. If it helps, you may say in your report that I did not consider it and did not have the latitude to."

The envoy took it exactly as it was given. He asked one clarifying question about whether the exclusivity ran to the compact's renewal or beyond it, and Xion answered, because Xion had written the clause, and the answer was to renewal, and the man wrote it down.

Then he thanked her for her time, which he meant, and withdrew.

Somebody put a cup of something at Xion's elbow while the room reset for the fifth item, and he drank about half of it, and did not think about the fourth item again.

The ceremony was in four hours.

---

By the third hour the hall had been filling for some while, and Xion stood where his position put him — near the front and to the side, close enough to see everything and far enough that nobody had to account for him — and read the room the way he read a room.

Everyone had come.

That was the first fact and it was worth standing still for. Every cartel. Every allied house. The Water bench and the Iron bench and the two houses that had spent the summer very carefully not being in the same district as each other, standing eleven feet apart and pretending the distance was to do with the light. Men who had sent regrets to the coronation had sent nobody's regrets today.

The second fact was in the geometry, and the geometry had shifted since the spring. He could have drawn the alliances of six months ago from memory and the drawing would have been wrong now in four places, and three of the four were wrong in the same direction.

The third fact was the charge on it. A hall of people who have spent their lives reading political weather can tell when the weather is about to do something, and none of them knew what, and all of them had dressed for it.

The Urathan delegation stood apart, as foreign observers always stand apart, with the particular self-consciousness of people who know they are being watched watching.

Draskin was not among them. He had gone north within the week, which Xion had expected and had thought about at some length; a Scholar-King does not sit four weeks in a foreign capital waiting on a case to be carried down a mountain, and a Scholar-King who had already got what he came for had less reason than most.

High Provost Venn had stayed.

She stood at the front of her people in academic dress with her hands folded, and she was doing what she had done in the reception chamber, which was to look at the room rather than at anybody in it. Xion watched her attention travel the hall — the doors, the height of the ceiling, the arrangement of the benches, the light — and go all the way along it and not come back.

He had not been able to find the bottom of that in five weeks of not looking for it.

He put it away. There was a great deal to track today and none of it was her.

---

Elara came in and the hall adjusted.

It did it the way it always did now, which was without any instruction and without anybody appearing to move: a settling, a turning, a change in the average direction of four hundred faces. She had learned this. That was the thing Xion could never quite get past — that there had been a woman in a brown cloak at the edge of a market crowd a year ago who did not know how to hold a wine glass, and that the way she crossed a room now had been assembled, piece by piece, out of a year of being looked at.

She was not performing. That was the whole of why it worked.

Behind her, on a cushion carried by an attendant who had plainly been practising, came the Scholar's Crown.

It was smaller than the histories made it. That surprised him and he let it. It was a band of something pale and greyish that was not silver and was not brass, worked in a way that made no sense at any distance he was permitted to stand at — no stones, no ornament, none of the aggressive craftsmanship the Balishan regalia went in for. It looked like an instrument. It looked, in fact, a great deal like the objects on the shelves of a room four hundred metres under the floor of this building, and Xion had known that it would and was not prepared for how hard it landed anyway.

Lathion had made this.

Lathion had made this, and given it to the Valanar, and then sealed itself away for two thousand years, and then died in a chamber to save the world it had given it to. And the thing had gone north three hundred years ago in a crate and had sat in a tower ever since, and not one person who had ever fought over it had known what it was or who had made it or what those makers had done at the end.

He knew. There were perhaps thirty people alive who knew, and most of them were scholars from Uratha, and two of them were in this room.

Even inert, on a cushion, at forty feet, it drew the eye. Everything else in the hall was gold.

---

The liturgy was old, or built to sound it.

Xion attended to it properly, which he had learned to do somewhere in the last year — ritual has functions that have nothing to do with beauty, and a man who dismisses it because it is not argument is a man who will be surprised by things he should have seen coming. The words were in the older register, the one that survives in liturgy after it has gone out of speech, and they had the specific weight that comes of having been said in this order over other heads in other centuries.

They were also, he noticed, describing a transaction that had not happened in three hundred years, in the present tense, without embarrassment.

The court watched. The Urathans watched. The attendant carried the Crown forward, and the celebrant took it, and Elara bowed her head — which was the only moment in the entire ceremony when she was required to look at the floor, and she did it without any hesitation at all.

The Crown went onto her head.

And nothing happened.

The liturgy provides for this. There is a pause written into it, a space of some seconds in which the assembly is instructed to be still, and the instruction is three centuries old and had presumably been put there by somebody who understood the difference between a ritual that has room in it and a ritual that does not.

The hall was extremely quiet.

Xion, who had spent eight years in rooms where a thing either worked or did not, recognised the quality of that quiet and felt his own chest go tight with it. It was the silence of people watching to see whether something takes. It was the silence outside a door.

Four hundred people waited.

Then the Crown lit.

---

It did not brighten. That was the thing he came back to afterward and could never make anybody understand who had not been standing in it.

Brightening is what a lamp does. This was ignition — a blue that arrived at full strength in no time at all, that did not come up from nothing but simply commenced, as though it had been there the entire time at a pitch the eye had no access to and had now been permitted through. It came out of the band and out of the air just above it and it went up and out and filled the hall to the corners, and the gold on four hundred people turned the colour of deep water, and every shadow in that room moved at once.

There was no heat in it. There was no sound.

Xion had watched the wards answer her. He had stood in the chamber when the throne took her and had felt the floor of his own certainty come up under him. He had been assembling the argument for this woman for a year, out of evidence, in front of people who did not want to hear it, and he had thought — genuinely, right up until the moment the light arrived — that he had already had the largest version of this feeling that was available.

He had not.

The court broke. Four hundred of the most guarded people in the world lost their composure simultaneously and completely, and it lasted about three seconds, and Xion would remember the sound of it for the rest of his life — not a shout, nothing so coherent; the sound of a great many people inhaling in a room with a high ceiling.

Then, being what they were, they got it back. The recovery moved across the hall like weather across a field. Faces reassembled. Postures corrected. Within five seconds every man in that room was standing the way a man stands at a ceremony.

But it had happened, and everybody in the hall had seen everybody else in the hall let it happen.

Whatever doubt had still been alive in the corners of this city — and it had been; Xion could have named the four rooms it lived in and the particular careful phrasing it used in official correspondence — whatever was left of it had just had a very bad afternoon.

---

What went through him was not vindication.

He waited for it, in the way you wait for a familiar thing, and it did not come, and what came instead was so simple that it took him a moment to identify.

He was glad.

Not relieved. He had been relieved before and knew the taste of it. This had no relief in it at all, because relief requires that you have been afraid of the other outcome, and he had not been; that was the whole trouble with the last year, that the certainty had never wavered and had never once been worth anything to anybody who did not already share it.

He had carried this conviction through the Coterie's table and his father's house and eleven days under the ground. He had said she is who I say she is to men who smiled at him. He had never doubted it and had never been able to prove it, and the two of those together are a specific kind of lonely that he had stopped noticing he was in.

The Crown was alight on her head and every person in this hall now knew what he had known in a market square with his hands shaking.

There.

That was the whole of the thought and it was not a very impressive one, and he stood in blue light with his eyes stinging and let it be the thought.

---

The light held.

That mattered more than the ignition, and he understood why while it was still happening. A flash is an event. A flash can be argued with — men had spent three centuries arguing with things that lasted a second. This did not stop. It stayed, at that impossible level, while the celebrant found his place again and went on, and while the recognitions proceeded, and while the great houses came forward one at a time in the order the protocol demanded and made their obeisance.

Every one of them did it in the light.

The whole remainder of the ceremony happened inside it. Which meant that no man in that room would ever again be able to remember the day Balisha's court formally recognised Elara Valanar without also remembering the colour it happened in, and Xion thought — watching the Iron cartel's head straighten up out of a bow he had almost certainly rehearsed as a formality — that whoever had built this ceremony three hundred years ago had understood exactly what they were doing, and had built it for precisely this, and had then not needed it for three centuries.

---

He read the room, because it was his function and because his hands needed something to do.

The court was easy. Four faces openly wet. A dozen radiant. Most of them somewhere in the middle of a rapid and entirely visible recalculation of their own position, which is a specific expression and one he had seen a great deal of lately, and which he did not hold against a single one of them.

He let his attention go out to the edges, where he kept it.

And caught it.

---

It was very fast.

The Urathan delegation had been standing through the whole of it in the correct formation with the correct attitude, and at some point in the three seconds when the hall broke, they had broken too — but not with it, and not like it.

The court's faces had done astonishment. He knew astonishment; he had watched four hundred examples of it a moment ago and could have sorted them by degree.

The Urathans had not done astonishment.

He got perhaps a quarter of a second of it, at forty feet, through blue light, and he was aware even at the time that he had no business being confident about anything he thought he had seen. Two of the men behind Venn had gone the colour of the cushion. One had turned his head sharply toward another, which is not a thing a trained delegate does in a foreign hall, and had been looking at him for help.

And Venn had put one hand flat on the rail in front of her.

That was all. She did not sway and her face did nothing. She put her hand down on the rail the way a person does when the floor has stopped being reliable, and she left it there for about as long as it takes to breathe out, and then she took it off again and folded it back into the other one.

By the time Xion had properly turned his head, all three of them were composed. They were, in fact, the most correctly composed people in the hall, which was itself a thing worth noticing, because everybody else in that room had spent the last minute visibly recovering and the Urathans had nothing left to recover from.

---

He turned it over.

Not for long — the ceremony was moving and there were eleven people he was supposed to be watching — but he had the habit, and the habit ran whether he authorised it or not.

Why would that surprise them.

The Crown was made in Lathion. The Valanar are Lathion's line; that was the whole theological argument, it was in every history, it was the reason three hundred years of Balishan ambassadors had been able to stand in the Ivory Tower and say that is ours without embarrassment. An artifact of Lathionese make answering a Valanar sovereign was not a surprise. It was the single most predicted event in the room. It was what the entire liturgy had been written on the assumption of.

Every Urathan in this hall had known that better than he did. They were scholars; it was their own doctrine; Venn's people had been maintaining the Crown for three centuries and had presumably had a view about what it would do.

You are not shocked when a thing does what your own scholarship says it will do.

Unless you had been expecting something else. And if you had been expecting something else, then you had a reason, and the reason would have to be something you knew and had not said.

He got that far.

He did not get further, and he did not try, because Elara was receiving the obeisance of House Larannas and there were things to track — who bowed low and who bowed correctly, who came forward in the second rank who should have been in the fourth, which of the two estranged houses had contrived to be presented immediately after the other and whether that was an approach or an insult.

He filed the Urathans and went back to work.

---

The ceremony ended and the light did not.

It came down — settled, rather; the blaze eased back to something a man could look at directly, the way a fire that has been fed hard comes down to its working heat. It did not go out. It sat on her brow at a level that said, unmistakably, this is what I am like when nothing in particular is happening.

And Elara stood under it.

She was not performing serenity. He had watched her perform a great many things in a year and had got good at telling; there is always something in the shoulders. There was nothing in the shoulders. She stood in a hall of four hundred people with a divine artifact burning quietly above her eyes and received what was owed to her, and what was in her face was not triumph and not relief and not even, particularly, surprise.

It was recognition.

Not the room's, of her. Hers, of something. She had the expression of a woman who has been handed a thing she was not aware of having been owed, and who is discovering, while four hundred people watch, that some part of her had known it was coming.

Xion looked at her for a long moment and thought about a hand laid flat on the side of a brass form in a gallery nobody had catalogued, and about a woman saying they knew to a room she could not explain.

He did not know what the Crown had given her. He was fairly certain, watching her, that she did not either — that it had opened and she had no idea what to ask it, and that she was standing there with something enormous made available to her and no instrument to take hold of it with.

That was a thought for another day. He put it with the others.

---

Afterward, in the seam between the ritual and the reception, when the hall had stopped being one thing and had not yet become the other, he passed near the Urathan delegation.

Not close. Close enough to see and not close enough to require a conversation.

They were faultless. Gracious with the two Balishan scholars who had come over to them, appropriately moved, saying the correct things about a historic day in the correct register. One of them laughed at something. Venn was speaking with Ormuth, who had come up from the camp for the ceremony and who was talking with his hands.

There was no trace of it anywhere. Not in the posture, not in the faces, not in the way any of them held a glass.

Xion went past without stopping.

He put it away deeper this time, in the place where he kept the pen that had not hesitated and a woman whose wanting had no bottom to it, and he was aware as he did that the folder had three things in it now and still did not have a name.
