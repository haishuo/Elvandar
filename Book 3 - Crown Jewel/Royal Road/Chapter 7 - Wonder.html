The Urathans arrived on a grey morning and immediately forgot the weather.

That was Xion's first real impression of them as a group: the moment the scholars stepped onto the passage floor and saw the Brass Door, the rest of the world ceased to exist. Not rudely — they were not rude people — but completely. One of the junior scholars stopped walking mid-step and had to be guided around a crate by a colleague who was also staring at the Door and navigating by some secondary sense. Their baggage was substantial: manuscripts in glass-fronted cases, portable reference stands that unfolded with articulated brass joints, instruments Xion had no names for. They had come prepared to work. They had not come prepared for the scale of it.

Nobody was, on first sight. The Door was five meters tall and three wide, faced in an alloy that had survived five thousand years without marking or tarnishing, inscribed across its entire surface with script and geometric patterns so precise they made the eye feel uncertain about what it was looking at. The chamber at the end of Dead Man's Passage did it no favors — forty meters of dark overhead that the camp's lamps never reached, blade-marks worked into the walls by hand over centuries, plank flooring laid across stone nobody had walked on in generations — and the Door sat in the far wall like something that had no business being there, like an object made in the wrong century that declined to apologize for it.

The lead scholar stood six feet from the Door and looked at it the way a surgeon looks at a patient before the first incision. With total, professional hunger. The junior scholars spread along the wall. One of them touched the surface very briefly with the tips of two fingers, then pulled back and looked at her hand. Whatever internal frictions existed within the contingent — tower rivalries, methodological disagreements, the ordinary politics of scholars who had not all chosen each other — none of it was visible now. These people were here because they could not not be here. Xion understood the feeling. It was not unlike medicine.

Farleen had been in the camp since before the Urathans arrived. She moved through it the way she moved through everything: quietly, methodically, making herself unremarkable by being consistently present. She was there because Xion was there. That was the whole of her reason, and she had never needed a second one.

Opening the Door was not fast.

Xion had known intellectually that it wouldn't be a matter of days, but he hadn't fully felt the timescale until he was living inside it. Weeks of scholars bent over the inscriptions with rubbing paper and magnification instruments, arguing in the precise, fond way of people who disagreed about things they cared about deeply: cross-referencing sequences against manuscripts that had survived through increasingly degraded copies and increasingly uncertain transmission chains, debating a translation made four centuries ago by someone who may or may not have seen the original. The scholarly apparatus was formidable. The Door was formidable. They spent weeks in careful conflict.

Elara worked alongside them, and she was strange to watch.

Where the scholars read the Door as a text — deciphering glyphs, proposing interpretations, arguing over whether the geometric sequences were numerical or phonetic or both — she read it differently. Not through scholarship. Through something Xion didn't have a word for. She would stand in front of a section of the surface for a long time without speaking, and then she would say something that hadn't come from any manuscript they had, and it would be right, and no one, including her, could say where it had come from. The knowledge arrived the way a word arrives on the tip of the tongue: present before she could account for it. She couldn't tell the scholars where it came from. She didn't try. The scholars found this professionally maddening and practically indispensable, and over the weeks they had reached an uneasy accommodation with that contradiction.

The disagreement came on the fourteenth day. The lead scholar had a translation, a documented sequence, a textual argument. Elara had a sense that the sequence started in the wrong place — the way a song that starts in the middle sounds like music but never resolves. Neither could immediately prove the other wrong. They held their positions for two days, working around the impasse. Xion watched them and understood something that hadn't been obvious before: this was what combined expertise actually looked like. It was not two authorities agreeing. It was two authorities making each other more precise.

The correct sequence emerged from the friction. The scholar's text and Elara's sense triangulated on something neither would have reached alone. The moment it clicked was quiet: her hand against a specific section of the Door, the scholar looking at his manuscript, a beat of silence, and then both of them turning to look at the same point simultaneously. Xion didn't entirely understand what had happened. He didn't need to.

The morning they tried came without ceremony.

People checked their lamps. They confirmed positions. They went through the ordinary preparations of readiness, not because the preparations were necessary but because having something to do with your hands helped. The expedition had been working toward this moment for weeks. Now that it was here, nobody rushed it. Xion stood back and watched with the feeling he remembered from operating rooms: the instruments laid out, everything prepared, nothing left to do but the thing itself. You had either prepared well or you hadn't. The scholars had their notebooks open. Farleen had positioned herself at the passage entrance in a way that was not obviously strategic. Elara stood at the Door. She looked at it for a long moment. Then she began.

Xion watched her work and could not have described what she did — not in any way that would mean something to someone who hadn't been present. She touched specific points on the surface. She spoke: briefly, quietly, in something that was not any language he recognized and that she had not, when he'd asked, been able to tell him she knew. She performed the sequence the scholars had assembled across weeks of careful work, and she did it from memory, without checking the notes, without hesitation, without the particular quality of attention that accompanies performing something learned. She did it the way you do something you have always known. The Empress of Balisha, who had grown up in an Arol Batae compound and spent years seeking out things that no one had told her to seek, opened the Door.

The pulse changed before anything moved.

It had been there so long — weeks of the expedition living next to it, sleeping through it, eventually forgetting to hear it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat — that the shift was what registered, not the pulse itself. It didn't get louder. It got more present. More close. As if something far below had registered what had just happened and was, in whatever way something made of brass and five thousand years old could, paying attention. The scholars went very still. Everyone waited.

Then the Door began to move.

Not opening. That was the first understanding, arriving all at once: it was not opening. It was rotating. The entire circular surface began to turn, slowly, without mechanical complaint, without the grinding resistance you would expect from anything sealed for five thousand years. The only sound it made was a single deep tone — like a bell struck at the bottom of a well, like something enormous and patient clearing its throat once. The inscription and geometric patterns covering the Door's face moved with it, and in moving became what they had always been: not decoration, not inscription. Components. The Door was a gear, and it had just been operated for the first time in fifty centuries.

The gears in the surrounding wall engaged. They were each the size of a house. Xion had been living next to this Door for weeks and had understood, at some level, that it was large. The gears recontextualized that understanding completely. This was not a door in a wall. This was a wall that was a door. The sound it made was geological — deep enough to feel in the soles of his feet before it reached his ears, the sound of something built to last ten thousand years being asked, for the first time in five millennia, to do what it was built to do.

A sliver of light appeared at the edge as the gear rotated clear. Blue. Perfectly steady. Not flickering — not the quality of any flame or lamp, nothing that consumed itself to produce light. Something that had been making this color in a sealed chamber since before any living person's grandparents were born, and it didn't know it should have gone out. As the gap widened, the light spilled into the passage, and it was wrong for underground and wrong for night and wrong for anything in Xion's vocabulary. The scholars were completely silent. So was everyone else.

The Door finished its rotation.

What it revealed was a room. Eight meters in diameter. Perfectly circular. Walls so smooth they seemed to have no material, no grain, no seam. No dust. No debris. No webbing, no mineral deposit, no organic decay of any kind. Clean — as clean as a room cleaned this morning — and it was five thousand years old, and the cleaning systems had been running this entire time.

It was also small.

The silence that followed had a specific quality. Xion knew the difference between silences, and this one was the silence of recalibration: people who had built something very large in their minds being asked to fit it into something much smaller. He felt it himself — the mental reordering, the scramble for the right frame. One of the junior scholars said, very quietly: It was a broom closet after all. A few people almost laughed. It wasn't unkind. It was exactly what everyone was thinking.

Then the pulse reached them.

Louder inside the antechamber. Not dramatically — perceptibly, undeniably louder. One beat every three seconds, felt in the chest before heard. The junior scholar who had made the joke was paying very close attention to the floor now. The pulse was coming from somewhere below this room. It was large, and regular, and it had been running without interruption for as long as the Door had been sealed. Whatever was producing it had not stopped.

The lead scholar stepped to the threshold — just the threshold — and reached out a hand into the chamber, palm up, the way you reach into the dark to check for rain. He stood like that for a moment. Then he turned and looked at Elara.

"The air is fresh," he said.

Not stale. Not sour. Not the compressed stillness of a sealed underground space. Moving air, circulating, drawn from somewhere — the ventilation was still running, something had been drawing air from the surface through this place for the entire time the city had been locked. Xion formed the thought carefully, the way he formed thoughts that needed to be gotten right: We are not standing at the entrance to a ruin.

"It's still running," he said, to no one in particular.

Past the antechamber, a passage descended. Five meters wide. Well-lit in the same steady blue. The walls had changed — still smooth, still seamless, but the proportions were different, more considered, as if whoever had designed this passage had thought about what it would feel like to walk through it every day. Not only how to construct it. And there was a conveyor system. Xion looked at it for a moment to be certain of what he was seeing. A mechanical transport, functional, currently running — the platform moving at a steady pace, built for carrying people efficiently between depths. Not a rope arrangement. Not stairs. A system, engineered at scale for regular use, because the people who needed it had made this journey often enough that climbing was an inconvenience worth solving.

It was still running. It had been running for five thousand years. It was waiting for passengers.

"The antechamber," he said, thinking it aloud, "was never the destination." The lead scholar, who had come to stand beside him, looked at the conveyor and at the passage descending into blue light. His professional certainty had been reorganizing itself for the last twenty minutes. He looked like a man who had been very confident about the dimensions of a room and had just been shown a door he hadn't known existed.

"It was a waiting room," the scholar said.

"Yes."

Someone stepped onto the conveyor. The decision wasn't ceremonial — the platform was moving, someone had to go first, and the decision was made the way practical decisions are made when they need to be. The platform took the weight without complaint. It moved. The expedition followed.

The descent took time. The passage ran wide and lit, shallow enough that it didn't feel like dropping — more like walking steeply downward, consistent grade. Nothing about it hesitated. The pulse grew as they descended. Not faster — never faster, always that same three-second interval — but deeper, more physical, felt now in the sternum before it reached anything else. Something was running below them, and it was large, and the closer they got to it the truer that became.

The passage stayed utilitarian throughout — well-made, clean, functional. Not built to be seen. Built to be used. That shift would come later.

The pulse grew. The light held steady. They descended.

The conveyor slowed as it approached the bottom. They passed through a transitional space, and Xion recognized the function immediately: the utilitarian passage ended here. Vaulted ceilings where there had been none. The first carved ornamentation they had seen since the Door's surface — five thousand years of functional construction and then suddenly, here, decoration: deliberate, restrained, beautiful. Brass fixtures catching the blue light and holding it differently, warmer, more considered. Whatever this space was called by the people who built it, its purpose was clear — it was a threshold, a place designed to mark the crossing from one world into another, to say: you are about to arrive somewhere. The scholars had been quiet for the entire descent. They were quieter now.

The space opened outward.

Xion stood at the threshold.

The first reflex was automatic: you enter a room, you find its edges. He looked for the far wall and couldn't find it. There was something in the distance, but it wasn't a wall. It was a building. And between here and there — more city. More city than his eye could account for. Streets wide enough to swallow the entire Noble Quarter, buildings whose rooflines rose beyond easy reckoning, and it went on, and it curved — gently, impossibly — and he still couldn't find the edge.

No shadows.

The light was everywhere. Sourceless. The same blue-white they'd followed since the Door, but here it came from all directions at once, distributed through the space so completely that nothing cast shade. He looked at his own hands. Nothing. Underground, sealed for five thousand years, and lit like open sky at noon.

Then the scholar beside him said, very quietly: "Those are plants."

A terrace, close enough to see clearly. Green. Something growing — actually growing, reaching toward a ceiling that was not a sky, under light that had no sun behind it, alive when it had every reason not to be. Five thousand years and something here was still alive.

He looked back at the large building he'd been using to anchor himself — the one whose proportions communicated, in a language he didn't speak but understood anyway, that it had mattered enormously. He looked at its windows.

The lights were on.

Warm light, different from the blue-white everywhere else, coming from inside. From this distance the building looked like somewhere people had just stepped out of. Like they'd be back.

Then he noticed that the corner of the building had moved.

He'd been looking at it long enough. He hadn't noticed the movement begin — you couldn't, it was too slow — but it had been at one position, and now it was at another. He fixed his eye on it and held. The corner drifted. Slowly. Patiently. The whole level was rotating.

At the center: a shaft. Open through the floor, cutting straight down through everything below, dropping into depth he couldn't measure. He looked at it and felt, for the first time since the Door, something other than the horizontal scale of the city. Whatever was below — however many levels, however far — the shaft went all the way. And at the bottom, past the light, past where the eye could follow: a warm deep glow. The color of the earth's own heat. That was what had been beating. He understood that now — felt it in his chest, in the floor, in his teeth. It had been running since before anyone alive was born.

It was running now.

Nobody moved. Nobody spoke.

The lead scholar sat down. Slowly, on the nearest surface. Not dramatically. Just because his legs had made a decision.

Farleen found his hand. She didn't say anything. He didn't either. They stood like that, looking at the shaft, at the slow impossible drift of the city turning below them.

The moment held. A city, lit from within. Still turning. Five thousand years old, and still running. No name for it. No word any of them knew that fit what they were looking at. That was all. That was everything.

Someone — Xion didn't know who, possibly himself — whispered it.

"Lathion."
