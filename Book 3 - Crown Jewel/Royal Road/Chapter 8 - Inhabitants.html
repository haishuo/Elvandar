There was no morning.

Xion came up out of sleep the way you do in a strange bed, reaching for the shape of the room before he had opened his eyes, and found nothing where the shape should have been. The light was the same light. It had not moved, or warmed, or come in at any particular angle, because it did not come in at all; it was simply there, the way it had been there when he lay down. The pulse was the same pulse. Somewhere under everything, once every three seconds, patient as a metronome and not remotely interested in whether he was awake for it.

For about four seconds he had no idea where he was.

Then he did, and the knowing was stranger than the not-knowing had been. He was lying on a folded blanket in an alcove off a road, under more rock than he could put a number to, inside a place that had been shut for longer than anybody kept records. He had slept here. He had done the ordinary animal thing of losing consciousness for six hours, and the city had gone on turning underneath him the entire time, and none of it had stopped being true while he was not looking at it.

He lay still and let that settle, and it did not settle.

Around him the camp was waking in the particular quiet of people who had not yet decided what voice to use. They had thrown it together in the dark — packs against a wall, bedrolls in two rows, the quartermaster stacking his crates square to a wall out of pure habit — and now they moved around it doing small practical things because small practical things were available. Straps redone. Water counted. A man went along the line of lamps with an oil flask, checking each one, and got four lamps down the row before he stopped and looked up at the ceiling that was not a ceiling and started laughing.

It was not much of a joke. But it broke something open, and after that people talked.

Xion sat up and put his boots on and listened to it happen. The wonder had not gone anywhere. It had simply stopped being an event and started being the air, which struck him as the more extraordinary of the two states. A junior scholar was eating a strip of dried fruit standing up at the mouth of the alcove, staring down the boulevard, and had the face on him that Xion had last seen on a child at a festival — not delight exactly. The expression of somebody whose eyes were being given more than they had ever been asked to hold.

---

Elara was at the railing.

She had been there a while; he could tell from the way she was standing, which was the way people stand when they have stopped noticing that they are standing. The Crown's outer walk ran along the edge of the level and gave onto the shaft, and the shaft gave onto everything — the levels below turning slowly in their own directions, the warm glow far down at the bottom of it all, the light coming up out of that in a steady column that had no business existing.

He came and stood a respectful distance off, which was a thing he had learned to do around her over the last year and had never once discussed with her.

She did not turn.

Whatever had been in her since the Door opened was still in her, but it had changed state overnight. He had watched her go through the descent with her jaw set and her hands closed. Now the hands were open on the rail and the set had gone out of her jaw, and what was there instead was not peace. It was more like the look of somebody who has finally been handed the one piece that makes the rest of the pieces mean something, and has not yet decided whether that is good news.

He did not ask. He had spent a year learning that asking produced nothing.

After a while she reached out and put her hand flat on the railing. Not to steady herself. She had been holding it for some minutes already. She lifted her hand and set it down again, deliberately, the way you touch the doorframe of a house you have come back to.

Something had happened to her in the night, and he could not read what, and it was not going to be explained.

"Ready?" he said.

"No," she said. And then, after a moment, "Yes."

---

The first problem of the day was not the mystery. It was that they had no words.

He noticed it inside an hour, and once he had noticed it he could not stop noticing it. The expedition had a name for the city — somebody had whispered it in the dark when they came out of the passage and it had gone through them all like water through cloth — and they had a name for nothing else in it. Every conversation was twice as long as it needed to be. The big chamber. The upper level, not that one, the one above. The room with the — you know. The brass things.

He stood at the edge of the Compass, though it was not called that yet, and watched three men fail for five minutes to establish which corridor each of them meant. They were not stupid men. Two of them had spent their working lives reading languages nobody had spoken in centuries. They simply had nothing to point with.

You cannot think about a thing you cannot name.

It sorted itself out the way these things do, which is to say nobody decided anything. Somebody said a word, and somebody else used the same word back, and by the time anyone might have objected it was too late to object.

The top level went first. It was where they had come in and where the camp was, and it sat over the rest of the city the way a lid sits over a pot, except that it was not a lid, it was the best part — the wide roads, the tall frontages, the buildings whose windows still held warm interior light. Somebody called it the Crown because it was on top. Xion heard three separate men use the word before midday, and every one of them said it in the tone of a man producing something he had just thought of.

The rest came through the afternoon like a dialect assembling itself.

The vaulted space at the foot of the long conveyor, where the plain working passage suddenly acquired ornament and brass and a ceiling that meant something — that became the Gatehouse, because it was where you stopped being in a tunnel and started being in a city, and whoever built it had wanted you to know.

The circular plaza where six roads came together around a fountain still running water so clear the basin looked empty became the Compass, because you could find yourself from it. Within a day people were giving directions from the Compass without explaining what the Compass was.

And a long curved gallery lined with alcoves, each alcove holding an object nobody could identify, became the Gallery of Unknowns. That one was a joke and everybody knew it was a joke and the junior scholars said it with obvious pleasure every single time.

None of them were right. Xion understood that perfectly well. The Lathionese had walked these roads for fifty centuries and had called them something, and whatever they had called them had gone wherever they had gone, and what was being laid over the top of it now was a set of noises invented by exhausted foreigners in the space of one working day.

He found himself moved by the smallness of it, and could not have said exactly why. Four little human words, put down carefully on top of something enormous. It was not much. It was the only thing they had.

---

The machines were the other thing that took getting used to.

The first one came around a corner and half the corridor flinched. It was not a man and did not resemble one — it was a shape, waist-high, brass and something clear that was not glass, moving on some arrangement of feet that made no sound at all on the stone. It came down the middle of the corridor at a walking pace, and where the expedition was standing it went around them the way water goes around a stone in a stream, without hesitating, without slowing, and without any indication whatsoever that it had registered a single one of them.

It stopped four meters on, extended something, and began polishing a section of wall that had not been dirty in twenty years.

"Gods," said somebody, with feeling.

The second one, an hour later, was remarked on. By the third, people were stepping aside before it reached them, and by the end of the day the expedition had absorbed the machines the way you absorb weather.

Xion watched one work for longer than he meant to. It was doing something to a fixture at the join of wall and ceiling — adjusting it, or testing it, or performing some maintenance the purpose of which was entirely opaque — and it was doing it with a patience that no living thing could have sustained. He thought about what it took to build something that outlasted the people who built it. He thought that the machine did not know its makers were gone, and could not know, and would go on polishing this wall until its mechanism failed, and that its mechanism was five thousand years old and had not failed yet.

It was a comfortable thought. He carried it around with him for most of the day.

---

Brennek found the air.

The crown surveyor had come down with the rest of them and had been, since the moment the Door finished turning, almost entirely silent. Xion had watched him at the Door camp for three weeks. This was the man who had put four days into a plan for a hinge that turned out not to exist, and eleven pages into a slab of brass, and the better part of seven hours into hunting a join he never found — and had written the failure up in the same hand as everything else, at the same size, without one word of complaint.

Excitement was not in him and never had been. What was in him was a phenomenon and no framework, and Xion knew exactly what that does to a man, having spent a good many nights in that condition himself.

Now Brennek had a city.

He did not exclaim about it. He walked the corridors with a strip of damp cloth held out at shoulder height, watching how it moved, and by mid-morning he had found the place where the air came from — a grille in a corner of a room off the third boulevard, unremarkable in every way, from which came a draught you could not feel on your face but could see quite clearly on a wet rag.

By noon he had worked out the water. He did this by tasting it, which he did at three separate fixtures on two different roads, with the deliberation of a man at a wine merchant's, and then reported that the third one was drawn from somewhere else than the first two and had more iron in it and was, if anything, better.

"You just drank unknown water out of a five-thousand-year-old pipe," Xion said.

"Somebody was going to."

He brought all of it to Xion. Not to the scholars. It was a small thing and it changed nothing — the scholars would have had it inside a day either way, and nobody was keeping secrets — but Brennek walked past twelve of the best-read men in the world with his findings and came and found the Balishan First Counselor instead, and did it three times before noon.

Xion noticed. He did not do anything about it. He put it where he put things.

---

They took the infrastructure and left the inscriptions, and by the afternoon there were two expeditions in Lathion working the same city from opposite ends.

The scholars had the writing. They were up and down the Crown's frontages with rubbing paper and lenses, arguing in low voices, and every so often one of them would make a sound that brought three others at a run.

Xion and Brennek had the plumbing.

It was, he thought, a fair division. It also turned out to be the more unsettling half, though it took him most of the day to work out why.

The water ran. It came from below, and it came hot, and something took the heat out of it before it arrived and put the heat somewhere useful. The temperature on the Crown did not vary. Brennek stood in front of the mechanism that accomplished this for ten minutes without speaking, which from him was the equivalent of a shout, and then said that he could describe about a fifth of it and that the fifth he could describe was better than anything in Kaha'an.

And the terraces grew.

They had seen the green from the Gatehouse the night before and taken it for survival — some tenacious thing hanging on in the dark, the way weeds come up through a paving stone. It was nothing of the sort. It was a farm. There was light coming down onto it in the right measure, and water going through it on a schedule, and something being done to the soil at intervals by machines that came and went, and the whole apparatus was running the way a mill runs when the miller is at his dinner.

It went further than he could see. That was the part he came back to afterward — he walked the near edge for a quarter of an hour and did not come to the end of it, and there were more of them below, tier under tier, going down the curve of the level in the same steady light.

He started doing the arithmetic before he had decided to, and he did it in his father's voice, which was the voice that turned up whenever a quantity of grain and a number of mouths appeared in the same thought. He had spent his childhood in rooms where men said things like a family eats this much in a year as though they were remarks about the weather. He had hated those rooms. He had also, apparently, been listening.

Yield against area. Area against the curve of the level. He was out by a great deal in either direction and knew it and did the sum anyway, the way you take a pulse you already know is bad.

The answer had five digits in it.

He stood on a terrace path with his hands loose at his sides and understood that he was looking at the number of people who had lived here. Not an impression of a lot of people. A quantity. The sort you could write on a page and hand to a clerk. Somebody had built this to feed a city the size of a city, and had built it to go on doing that for as long as the city needed feeding, and it had.

And it was still doing it.

That was the thing he could not get his shoulders around. The light was still coming down in the right measure. The water was still going through on its schedule. The machines were still working along the rows, and the rows were still bearing, at the rate they had been built to bear, for a hundred thousand people who were not there.

He asked, later, what happened to it. Brennek had already gone and found out. Some of it was in stores — vast ones, cool, ordered, stacked in a way that made Xion think of the vessels on the shelf. The rest went the way it had to go. There were beds at the far end where the machines took what had turned, and turned it back into soil, and put the soil where soil was wanted.

Twenty years of harvest. Brought in, put away, gone over, thrown out, begun again.

Nobody had eaten any of it.

---

The room was off a side road on the eastern arc, and Xion went into it because the door was open.

That was all. There was no reason beyond that. He had spent his adult life walking into rooms uninvited to find out what was wrong inside them, and the habit did not switch off because the room in question had been standing empty since he was a boy.

He stopped at the threshold anyway. He got as far as lifting his hand toward the frame before he understood what he was about to do, and then he did it — knocked twice, on the frame of a door that had stood open for twenty years, in a city with nobody in it.

He felt foolish. He went in anyway.

It was a dwelling. That much was obvious immediately and told him nothing, because it was a dwelling in exactly the way that a Kaha'ani house is a dwelling — rooms opening off other rooms, a place where the light was better that had probably been for working in, a place where the light was softer that had probably been for sitting. The proportions were wrong for a man of his height and right for somebody a little shorter. There were shelves. There was a low table.

He stood in the middle of it and waited for the thing that was wrong to present itself.

Nothing did.

That was when he started paying attention properly, because nothing is not what an empty room gives you. An empty room gives you a great deal. He had walked into rooms where somebody had died in the night and rooms where somebody had run, and rooms where a family had been put out into the street with an hour's notice, and each one of those has a signature, and the signature is written in what got left on the floor.

There was nothing on the floor.

There was nothing broken. No vessel had been dropped and swept into a corner and forgotten. Nothing had been knocked off a shelf by a hurrying shoulder. Nothing lay where a hand had let go of it — and something always does; that is the one thing he had learned to count on in twelve years of walking into other people's disasters. People do not put things down when the world is ending. They drop them, and the drops stay where they fall, and you can read a bad night off a floor like a page.

Not here.

His first thought was the machines, and he held on to that for as long as he could, because it was the comfortable answer and he was aware of wanting it. The machines cleaned. He had watched one polish a wall for a quarter of an hour that morning. Of course the floor was clear; something came through here and made it clear.

Then he looked at the shelves, and the comfortable answer came apart in his hands.

The vessels were stacked.

Six of them, graded by size, one inside the next, set back from the edge of the shelf where they would not be knocked. Beside them a row of tools of some kind, all lying the same way round. The low table had been moved against the wall — he could see where it had stood for years by the mark on the floor, and it was not standing there now. The chairs were against the wall too. Everything in the room that could be moved had been moved to the edges, and everything that could be gathered had been gathered, and everything that could be stacked had been stacked.

A machine polishes. It does not stack.

Somebody had stood in this room and put it in order. Somebody had taken the time to nest six vessels by size, and to square the tools, and to push the table back against the wall so the floor was clear — and then had walked out of the door and left it open behind them.

Xion stood in the middle of a stranger's front room with his hands at his sides and read it the way he would have read a body.

They were not surprised. Nobody tidies in a panic; panic is the thing that puts objects on floors.

They had time. Enough of it to do this, and to do it carefully, and this was one room in one house on one road of a city with roads he had not walked yet.

They chose. Whatever this was, it was not something that happened to them while they were facing the other way.

They expected somebody. You do not clear a floor for nobody. You clear a floor so that whoever comes in afterward can get across it.

And the last one arrived on its own and he could not stop it and he did not say it out loud, not then and not to Brennek and not to anyone that day:

They knew they were not coming back.

He went out through the open door onto the road and stood in the light for a while.

---

"How long?" he said.

Brennek was on his knees at the edge of the nearest terrace with his hand in the soil, and had been for some time.

"Wrong question for the plants," he said. "The plants are having a lovely time." He sat back on his heels and pointed with two fingers, along and down, at the beds where the machines put what had turned. "Ask those."

Xion looked, and saw a bank of dark soil, and nothing else.

"It goes in in layers," Brennek said. "Has to. They bring it, they cover it, they leave it, they bring the next lot. Nothing mixes it, because nothing down here does anything it wasn't told to." He worked two fingers into the cut edge of the bank and drew them downward, and the face came away clean, and it was banded, the way a cut riverbank is banded. "That's a year. That's a year. That's a year."

Xion crouched beside him and looked at a wall of soil with lines in it.

"Everything else in this city is brass," Brennek said, "and brass is no use to me. Brass looks the same after twenty years as after two thousand and I can't tell you which. Stone's worse." He wiped his hand on his knee. "But something's been growing here the whole time, and every year of it had to go somewhere, and it came here, and it stacked up in order. This is the only clock in Lathion. It's the one thing down here that ever got older."

"How many?"

"That's what I've been doing since breakfast." A pause. He was not a man who enjoyed being the one to say a thing. "Twenty. Give me a month and I'll give you a season. But it's twenty, and it isn't nineteen, and it isn't thirty."

"Not five thousand."

"Not five thousand. Not five hundred." He looked up. "Somebody was eating this, Counselor. Right up until they weren't."

---

It was Xion who took the next step, and he took it standing on a farm terrace with the light of no sun on the back of his neck.

"Twenty years," he said. "That's the Rending."

Brennek did not answer straight away. When he did, it was in the flat voice of a man reading out a measurement he does not like.

"The Rending was a surface event."

"I know what it was."

"It scoured the province. It came across the ground, and it killed what was standing on the ground, and it stopped when it stopped." He stood up, and brushed his palms together, and looked at the terraces rather than at Xion. "This is four hundred meters down, behind a wall of brass that three weeks of the best-read men on the continent could not open. There's no mechanism. I can't draw you a line from up there to down here. There isn't one."

"No," Xion said. "There isn't."

"Then it's a coincidence of dates."

"Yes."

They stood there.

Brennek was right, and Xion knew he was right, and could not have argued the other side of it for thirty seconds. There was no mechanism. A wave of death had crossed the surface of the world one afternoon twenty years ago and turned a green province into sand, and it had not come down here, because there was no possible road by which it could have come down here, and the door had been shut against the daylight for longer than anyone had a record of.

The number did not move.

---

Elara found the inscription at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, which was to say she walked past it.

Two scholars had been at it since before noon. It ran along the base of a frontage on the second boulevard, twenty feet of it, and they had gone from enthusiasm through disagreement into the flat exhausted courtesy of men who have each decided privately that the other is being obtuse. Xion had heard the tail of it going past an hour earlier and had kept walking.

Elara came along the boulevard with three of the contingent behind her, slowed, stopped, and looked at the wall for perhaps a count of five.

"This one matters," she said.

And walked on.

Behind her, the argument stopped as though it had been cut with a knife. One of the two men got out his notebook. The other looked after her the way a man looks at a barometer that has just dropped, and then also got out his notebook.

She could not tell them why. Ormuth asked her, an hour later, with great care, and she tried — Xion watched her try — and got two sentences in and stopped, and said that the words she had were not the words she would need, and that was the end of it. She did not know what the inscription said. She knew that it had weight, the way a phrase in a half-remembered song has weight even when you cannot produce the line.

What Xion noticed, and went on noticing for the rest of the day, was what the scholars did with it.

They stopped asking her what things said.

It happened without an announcement and without anyone appearing to decide it. By the evening the question being put to Elara was not what is this but is this one important, and that was a question she could sometimes answer. She gave them impressions, hedged to the point of uselessness — that one is older than the others, those two go together, that one I can't feel at all — and they wrote each impression down like a field note, with her name against it and every one of her hedges preserved, and then went back to translating by their own methods.

She was not doing their work. She could not have done their work. She was telling twelve exhausted men where to spend the next fortnight of their lives, and they had worked out, somewhere in the last three weeks, that this was worth more to them than an answer would have been.

---

There was a second thing, later, smaller and considerably stranger.

They were crossing the plaza on the way to a chamber the scholars wanted looked at, and there was a console standing in the middle of it. That was the word people had settled on for these — waist-high, a surface, no controls anybody could identify, no obvious purpose. There were a dozen of them scattered around the Crown and not one had done anything.

Elara went past it, slowed, and laid her hand flat on the top of it.

She did not press. There was nothing to press. She stood with her palm on it for a moment and then took her hand away.

"This one's sleeping," she said, half to Xion and half to nobody at all, and kept walking.

Brennek, two steps back, wrote it down.

Xion watched her go and saw the thing he was not supposed to see, which was that she had surprised herself. It went across her shoulders and was gone: the look of somebody who has said out loud a thing they had been intending to keep.

He did not ask her about it. Whatever she had access to, it was not available to her when she wanted it, and it had never once come when it was called.

---

Ormuth came to find him in the late afternoon.

The Urathan lead scholar had been at the Door for three weeks and inside the city for two days, and had aged about four years across the whole of it, in the specific way of a man who is having the best time of his life and getting no sleep whatsoever. He arrived at Xion's elbow, clasped his hands somewhere behind him, and stood without saying anything for long enough to be noticed, which Xion had learned meant that something had been composed in advance.

"May I say something unprofessional."

"Please."

"I have published twenty-seven papers." He was looking down the boulevard rather than at Xion. "Several of them are quite good. Two of them are, I think, the reason anyone in the towers knows my name." He stopped. Started again. "This morning your Empress looked at a wall for four seconds and told me which half of the Gallery inscriptions to spend my life on. I do not know how. I am reasonably certain that she does not know how either, which is worse."

"It is," Xion said.

"I would trade all twenty-seven." There was nothing behind it at all — no weight, no performance — and that was what made it land. "I have been turning that over since about the ninth hour and I cannot make it come out any other way, and I would rather you heard it from me than worked it out from the way I have been behaving."

He nodded once, and went away, and Xion stood where he had left him for rather longer than he intended to.

It was not flattery. That was the trouble with it. It was a man saying aloud a thing he was going to have to learn to live with, and he had said it to a foreign official in a corridor because there was nobody else down here he could say it to.

Xion put that where he put things, too. The pile was getting large.

---

The dispatch went up on the second day.

It was expected; it had been in the agreement, in the third clause of the access terms, and nobody had made any secret of it. Ormuth wrote it, and brought it to Xion before it was sealed, and stood there while he read it.

It was a good report, and that was the difficulty with it, and the difficulty did not improve on a second reading. It was factual, and measured, and it described the preservation and the functioning systems and the scale of the place in language that would not have embarrassed a surveyor. It did not exaggerate anywhere. It did not need to. The facts as stated, in the flattest possible arrangement, were sufficient to change the world.

He sealed the pouch and handed it to the courier and walked with him as far as the Gatehouse, and watched him step onto the ascending platform with the thing under his arm, and had one clear moment — no longer than a breath — of wanting to call him back.

Not because the report was wrong. Because Xion had spent two years learning how information moves, and he knew exactly one thing about information of this size, which is that it does not travel by itself. It brings people with it.

The platform carried the courier up into the passage and out of sight.

He let it be a problem for later. There was a great deal of later available.

---

Farleen had been there the whole time.

She had been there the whole time in the way she was always there the whole time, which was to say that the camp had food in it, and the water was accounted for, and somebody had noticed that the scholars had not eaten since the previous evening and had done something about it before it became a medical question. She did not go and look at things. When the Gallery of Unknowns was found she asked what was in it, was told that nobody knew, said huh, and went back to counting lamp oil that nobody needed.

The city did not interest her much. It interested her the way weather interests a sailor: as the conditions in which the thing she actually cared about was going to happen.

She watched him. She had been watching him for five years and had got extremely good at it, and Xion knew perfectly well that she could tell the difference between the two halves of his day — the half where he had been walking around inside a marvel, and the half after the room on the eastern arc.

She did not ask. She brought him a bowl at some point in the evening and stood next to him while he ate it, which was her entire method and had been since the Amber Leaf.

"You've got the shoulders," she said.

"I know."

"Right," she said, and took the bowl back, and went away.

---

They gathered at the Compass in the evening, around a fountain running clear water for nobody, and it was the best meeting Xion had ever sat in.

Not because anything was resolved. Nothing was resolved. It was the best meeting he had ever sat in because every single person in it was working at the outer edge of what they knew and was prepared to say so out loud, which in his experience of meetings put it in a category by itself.

Ormuth offered a reading of the foundational inscriptions on the Crown, and then immediately offered two other readings, and explained that the grammar permitted all three and that the difference between them was whether the passage described a founding, a renovation, or a rededication — and that these were not the same thing at all, and that a civilization which rededicated its capital had a history behind it that a civilization which founded one did not. Two of his colleagues disagreed with him and with each other. The argument went on for half an hour and everybody enjoyed it enormously.

Brennek gave the infrastructure. He did it in the order he had found it, without emphasis, and he did not editorialize once, and the effect of hearing water, climate and agriculture read out flatly one after another in a plain Kaha'ani accent was considerably larger than any amount of emphasis would have achieved. When he finished there was a silence.

Everybody in the plaza understood what they had just been told. Nobody was ready for it. It had been two days.

Somewhere in the middle of a dispute between two scholars about which of two passages was the older, Elara said, without looking up from her cup:

"The second one."

Both men turned and looked at her.

She did not elaborate. She drank her water. After a moment the two of them turned back to each other and resumed the argument on the ground she had just moved them to, and did not remark on it, and Xion understood that this was now simply how the expedition worked.

The meeting ran long. Nobody wanted to leave.

---

Late, he walked the Crown by himself.

He could feel the rotation now. It had not changed — it had never changed, it was the one thing in this place that had been doing exactly the same thing at exactly the same rate since before anyone had a word for anything — but his body had learned the rhythm of it in two days and now reported it up to him without being asked, a long slow lean underneath everything, like standing on the deck of an enormous ship in a calm.

He walked past frontages whose purposes he could not guess. He walked past a plaza built to hold a great many people, laid out by somebody who had thought hard about how a crowd moves, for gatherings that were not going to happen again. He walked past a building whose front was worked in interlocking brass panels, and the panels did something to the light that nothing else on the road did, and he stopped there for a while.

Somebody designed that. Not somebody in the abstract, the way you say it about a mountain. A person had stood approximately where Xion was standing, with the same problem in front of him, and had solved it this way rather than one of the other ways, and had been pleased with it, or had not been, and had gone home afterward.

And here was the thing that had been waiting for him all day, and that he had come out here alone to finally look at.

That person had gone home within his lifetime.

Not in some unreachable antiquity. Not in the age of legends, where you can put a civilization and feel nothing about it. Twenty years ago there had been a man on this road, going the other way, looking up at this same steady light — and four hundred meters above him a boy had been sitting on an estate wall in the sun, sick and bored and not allowed out, and neither of them had known the other one was there.

They were not a lost people.

They were neighbours. They had been on the other side of a wall the whole time, close enough that his whole childhood and their whole ending had happened inside the same handful of years, separated by some stone and a door nobody could open. And then they had gone. All of them. Every road, every terrace, every one of these frontages with the lights still burning inside them.

The lights were still burning.

That was what he kept arriving back at, standing in the middle of it. The lights were on and the water was clean and the air was moving and the machines were still going up and down the corridors polishing walls for people who were not coming. The city had not noticed. Twenty years and the city had not noticed, and it was going to go on not noticing, patiently, in the dark, for as long as its mechanisms held.

He stood in a lit street in an empty city and could not think of one single thing to do about any of it, which was, he was aware, the oldest and least useful feeling he had.

---

He found Elara at the railing.

Third time in two days. He stopped a little way off, as he had that morning, and waited until she half-turned, and then went and stood beside her.

They looked down together. The shaft dropped away under them, level below level, each one turning at its own rate and none of them agreeing, down past the last thing he could resolve and further than that, to the slow warm colour everything up here was arranged around. The pulse came up out of it. Three seconds, and then three seconds. It had been the same at the Door and it was not the same here, because here it had weight, and the weight came up through the boots and stayed.

The scholars had found something in the Crown inscriptions that afternoon — cross-references, four of them, all pointing at the same place on a level below this one. A chamber where the Lathionese had put what they knew. They had started calling it the Repository, half certain and half hoping, and had been saying the word all evening in the tone of men who did not want to jinx it.

Neither he nor Elara said that this was where the heart of the place was.

They both knew it. He had known it since he came out of the passage and saw the light at the bottom of the shaft, and she had known it before that, and neither of them had a word for how.

She did not move toward it. She stood at the rail with her hands open on the metal and looked down, and Xion would have staked a great deal, on no evidence he could have produced for anybody, that some part of her had already gone down without her.

The city turned under them. The lights held. Somewhere below, the water ran clear through a system that nobody had corrected in twenty years, and the terraces put up leaves toward a light with nothing behind it, and the machines went along the corridors of the Crown doing what they had been told to do a long time ago by people who had stacked their bowls, and squared their tools, and pushed the table back against the wall, and left the door open.

You cannot think about a thing you cannot name.

They had names for it now. Small ones. Wrong ones. Enough to be going on with.

Now the thinking started.
