Vesk had paid a man to walk under the city, and he reported it the way he reported everything, which was without any expression at all.

"His name is not worth writing down. He holds four shafts on the north side and a stretch of the third tier, and he sold us the use of them for eleven days at a rate he made up while I was standing there." The commander had his hands behind his back and his weight even on both feet, in the yard behind the customs house where they had staged the loads. "He asked whether he should be honored to be dealing with the crown. I told him he should be quick."

"Did he answer for Ronas?" Elara said.

"No, Majesty. That is the part I would want you to have. I asked him for a word from Dermir and he did not know what I meant. He owns his shafts. Dermir owns him. What Dermir does not do is answer letters, and what this man does not do is care, because the letters are not his and the shafts are." Vesk's mouth did something that was not a smile. "I have been in three wars. I have never once been able to buy a road through an enemy's country from a lieutenant while the general was still deciding whether to receive my envoy. It is not a country. It is a market with a king in it."

Elara said nothing for a moment.

Xion watched her not say it. She was looking north over the low roofs toward the ruin field where the Surface Warrens began — the collapsed tenements, the scaffolding grown into the scaffolding, the sixty thousand people who lived above and below that line and had lived there through the whole of her family's absence. Somewhere under all of it a man had just sold the Empress of Balisha eleven days of passage beneath her own capital, and had done it at a price he invented on the spot, and had been perfectly within his rights, because nobody above ground had offered him anything for twenty years that would have made it worth his while to be governed.

"Note it," she said. "Not for today."

"Majesty."

"Note it, Commander. I want it in front of me again when there's time to do something about it."

She turned back to the loads. Xion filed the whole exchange under a heading he was accumulating rather too much material for, which was things Elara has looked at once and put away, and went to see about the lamps.

---

They went down as a working party of nineteen: Vesk and six of the Arol Batae, two surveyors and their instruments, four porters hired out of the Surface Warrens itself because they knew how to carry on a ladder, a quartermaster, Elara, Xion.

And Farleen, who was counted in nobody's column and had been on the manifest since the morning it was drawn.

She had not asked. She had come to him two nights before with the loading list in her hand and had gone down it, item by item, telling him what was wrong with it — the lamp oil was short by a third for the number of hours he had estimated, the rope was the wrong rope, and no one had thought about what happened if a porter broke an ankle at four hundred meters and had to be brought up through a maze on a spar. Every one of the corrections was right. She had not at any point said I am coming, and by the end of the conversation the question of whether she was coming had stopped existing.

The Long Knives met them at the third tier, at a stretch of wall where the blade marks began.

Xion had crossed that line once before, at night, with a dying boy over his shoulder and no idea what the marks meant. He had learned since. Hundreds of stylized cuts in the old stone, patterns that read almost like script and were not script, a border that had been maintained by hand for longer than the Balishan Empire had existed. Nobody guarded it. Nobody needed to. In all the Warrens there was no faction that would cross it for money.

Four of them came out of the dark on the far side, and one came forward, and the one who came forward was a woman of about forty with a face nobody would remember an hour later.

"You're expected," she said. "Two rules. My people walk in front and behind, all the way down and all the way back, every trip, every load. And nobody writes down the route."

The senior of the two surveyors, who had spent four days building a plan for exactly that, opened his mouth.

"Nobody writes down the route," she said again, pleasantly, to Vesk rather than to the surveyor, because she had worked out in about nine seconds which of them was the one to say it to. "Or you can have the route and no guides and see how far that gets you. There are men in the Passage who had maps."

"Agreed," Elara said, before Vesk could.

"Majesty," Vesk said.

"Agreed, Commander. We're not here to survey their tunnels. We're here for the door." She looked at the guide. "You've been told what we intend to do down there?"

"I've been told to bring you," the woman said. "The Mistress will speak to you herself about what you intend."

---

The descent took most of a day.

They went down through the tiers and the midlevels, past the chambers where people lived, and the parts of it Xion remembered were exactly where he had left them and the parts he did not remember were the parts he had walked past in the dark. There were more people than he had understood at the time. Whole families in the mouths of side galleries with laundry strung across them. A well station with a queue at it. Three boys who watched the column go by from a ledge with the particular still attention of children pricing something.

At around two hundred meters the character of the tunnels changed, and at three hundred it changed again, and somewhere below four hundred the last of the human noise fell away behind them and there was only the sound of nineteen people breathing and the small sounds their gear made.

Then Dead Man's Passage.

He had thought about it, in the weeks since. Not often. Not on purpose. But the thing about a body's memory was that it did not care what you had decided to think about, and the first turn they took into the Passage — a low, wrong-proportioned throat of stone that sloped away and then folded back on itself — put the whole of it into him at once, in his shoulders, before he had any thoughts about it at all.

The weight. That was the part that came back first. Tam had weighed nothing at all when he had picked him up and had weighed a great deal more than nothing after the first hour, and by the second hour Xion had been counting his own steps in blocks of a hundred because a hundred was a number he could still finish. The boy's head against the side of his neck. The heat coming off him. Farleen's lamp swinging somewhere ahead and her voice at a pitch he had never heard from her before and had hoped never to hear again, saying his name at intervals to make sure he was still behind her. And in front of all of it Elara's eyes turning over and over in the dark, blue to amber to violet and back, the only thing in the world to follow, going away from them at a pace none of them could match.

He looked ahead. Elara was walking six paces in front of him with a lamp in her hand and her jaw set, and she had not turned her head since they entered the Passage.

She had none of it. That was the thing he kept coming back around to. She had gone into that dark and come out of it on her knees on a brass platform with no idea how she had got there, and everything she knew about the worst night of Xion's life she knew because somebody had told her. She had been the only one of them who was not present.

The tunnel folded again. Somewhere ahead the guide said something quiet about the footing.

It was seven months ago. He could still feel exactly where the strap of his bag had cut into him, and it sat in him like a thing out of another life entirely.

They are here, he told himself, and the door is here, and he put it down, and it stayed down, mostly.

---

The chamber took the newcomers the way it had taken him.

Forty meters across, the ceiling gone up into a dark the lamps could not reach, the walls perfectly smooth brass and covered in a field of symbols that was not decoration — you could see it was not decoration, even knowing nothing, because decoration repeats and this did not. The floor was level. Not level the way a good mason gets a floor level. Level the way water is.

And there was no dust.

That was what stopped the quartermaster, five hundred meters under a desert city, in a room nobody had cleaned since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother. He stood in the entrance with a crate on his hip and looked at the floor and said, "Where's it all gone, then," to nobody, and one of the porters laughed and then stopped laughing.

At the far end, on its raised platform, was the door.

Five meters of it. Brass, or the thing that looked like brass and had not aged like brass, with the engraving worked so deep and so fine that in the blue light off the walls the surface seemed to be moving very slowly, which it was not. Xion made himself look at the frame rather than the face of it, because the face of it did something to the eyes. The frame was seated into the wall with a joint he could not find.

And behind it — not a sound. Something under a sound. The slow deep pulse he remembered and had half convinced himself he had invented, felt in the sternum rather than heard, at a rate that his hands, which had counted several thousand pulses in eight years, put at about a third of a resting man's.

"Well," said Vesk, in the flat voice of a man refusing on principle to be impressed by architecture.

Elara had stopped just inside the chamber.

She was not looking at the door with wonder. Xion had been ready for wonder, and had been half looking forward to it, and what was on her face instead was the look of a woman who has come around a corner in a foreign city and found a face she knows.

Nobody told the expedition to stay where it was. Vesk lifted two fingers about as far as his belt and the six Arol Batae stopped moving, and the porters stopped because the soldiers had, and the surveyors stopped because everyone else had, and by the time the last of them worked out that they had stopped, Elara was already twenty feet out across that impossible floor and walking alone.

Farleen, beside him, had gone very still.

In the same room, at the foot of the same steps, she had once taken Elara apart with about nine sentences — cold and exact and, Xion had thought at the time and thought still, not wrong. You walked into a death trap, and Xion followed you, because of course he did.

She did not say it. She stood with her hands loose and her weight on her back foot and watched Elara climb the platform steps, and what Xion saw in her face was not forgiveness, because Farleen did not deal in that, and not fear either.

It was that she had decided, at some point he had missed, that this was a thing she was going to let happen.

Elara reached the top of the steps. She stood in front of the door for a moment with her arms at her sides, the way you stand in front of somebody's house before you knock.

Then she put her hand flat against it.

---

The chamber went white.

Not white — the light was blue, it was always blue down here, but it came up so fast and so hard out of the engraving that for a half second Xion's eyes gave him nothing but the shape of her hand, black against all of it, small, at the centre of a room-sized bloom of light. Behind him somebody swore. Somebody else dropped something that rang on the floor for a long time.

The light did not flood. That was the extraordinary part, once his eyes came back. It traveled. It went out from under her palm in the order the symbols were written in, spreading along the lines of the engraving the way heat moves out through metal, so that the whole enormous field of it lit in a sequence, and the sequence had a direction, and there were places where it hesitated at a junction and chose.

And the door began to work.

He felt it through his boots before he heard it. Something enormous and unhurried came to life inside five meters of solid brass — a grinding that settled into a rhythm, and then a series of clicks running away from the centre in both directions, and then a long descending sound like a held breath let out.

The pulse behind it changed. He was sure of that. It came up, and steadied at something faster, and Xion stood there five hundred meters under Kaha'an with his fingers moving unconsciously against his own wrist, counting.

She had her hand on it. She had her back to all of them. The engraving went on lighting and re-lighting in patterns that were plainly saying something, one after another, patiently, the way you would speak slowly to a person who did not have your language.

It was awake. It was entirely, unmistakably awake, and it knew exactly who was touching it.

And it did not open.

---

It took a while for anybody to be certain of that, which was its own kind of cruelty.

The mechanisms ran. They ran for a long time, and they ran well — nothing in the sound of them suggested strain or damage or age. They completed something, and there was a pause of perhaps two heartbeats in which every person in that chamber leaned forward very slightly, and then they began again from the beginning.

The second time round, Xion understood that he was listening to a cycle.

Elara worked it for two hours.

Xion had expected, if he was honest, to watch her stand there and press. She did not. She went at it the way he had once watched her go at a man twice her weight in a training yard, which was slowly, and from several angles, and with her attention entirely on where the thing was carrying its weight.

She moved her hand and the pattern changed, and she noted where it changed. She put both palms flat at the height of her shoulders and the sequence ran differently and she said so aloud, clearly, so the surveyor could write it. She traced one line of the engraving with two fingers all the way from the floor to as high as she could reach and the light followed her the entire way, obediently, like a dog. She found a place low on the left where the whole field paused when she touched it, and went back to it four times, and each time it paused, and each time it went on.

Twice she stopped and shut her eyes and stood with her hand flat on the brass and her head down, and Xion knew what that was, because she had tried to describe it to him once and had given it up in the middle of a sentence. Not reading. Something more like the feeling of a word you have known your whole life arriving late. She would come up out of it and go straight to a section she had not touched before, with complete confidence, and put her hand on it, and be right — the door would answer that section differently, and the surveyor would write it down — and it would still not open.

He watched her hands and not her face. Hands were what he had.

And somewhere in the second hour he understood what he was looking at, and wished he did not.

There had been a man in the lower wards, four years ago, a drayman, who had come off a cart badly and never walked again. Xion had sat with him a long time in the weeks after, because there was nothing to be done and sitting was what was left. And he had shown him, once, because the man had asked and had a right to know: he had struck him below the knee with the edge of his hand, and the leg had kicked, cleanly, exactly as a leg should. The reflex was perfect. It was the last thing in that leg that still worked and it would go on working for years. It answered the tap. It would never again answer the man it belonged to, because the road between them had been cut, and there was nothing whatever wrong with either end of it.

That was the door. Every part of the mechanism was intact. The recognition was intact — it was better than intact, it was eager, it lit for her the way nothing in that chamber had lit for anyone in living memory or any memory before it. What was missing was not in the door and it was not in Elara.

It was the road between them, and somebody had cut it a very long time ago, and had not left the other half.

She stepped back at last.

The light did not go out all at once. It faded off the engraving the way light goes at dusk, unwilling, the outer reaches first, so that for a while there was still a glow around her handprint after everything else had gone dark. The mechanisms wound down through their cycle, finished it properly, and stopped.

Then it was a wall again. A very large wall with marks on it, in a room with no dust.

She came down the steps at an ordinary pace and her face was in order by the time she reached the floor, and it was in order because she had put it in order, and Xion, who had watched her do that since the first week he knew her, could see the seams.

She had wanted to be told no. He was fairly sure of that. No was a thing you could work with. What she had been told was yes, at a volume that shook a chamber, followed by nothing at all — the whole of her inheritance held up in front of her and lit from within and locked.

Her eyes were amber when she reached the bottom step. They had been blue all day.

She did not know. She never did. He looked away before she caught him at it.

---

They made the camp forty feet back from the platform, because nobody wanted to sleep against it and nobody was willing to say so.

It was a strange thing to build. Tents in a room that had never had weather in it; a fire pit they could not use and a shielded lamp-stove instead; the quartermaster's crates squared away against a brass wall five thousand years old and doing exactly what a wall is for. By the third hour it had begun to smell like a camp, which meant it had begun to smell like people, and Xion caught himself being obscurely sorry about that.

The Mistress came down to them that evening.

He did not see her arrive. He turned around at some point and she was standing at the edge of the lamplight with two of hers behind her, older than he remembered and dressed like nobody in particular, and it occurred to him that she had probably been standing there for a while.

Elara got up. Vesk got up faster, and put himself where a man puts himself, and Elara said, "Commander," once, and he moved.

"You came back," the Mistress said.

"I said I would."

"You said nothing of the kind. I said find me if you ever work out what you are." She came forward into the light. "Have you?"

"No."

"No," the Mistress agreed. There was a pause, and in it she looked past Elara at the dark platform, and Xion watched her do it and thought: generations. Her people had lived in these tunnels and kept that route and held that border for generations, and had never once been able to make the thing so much as flicker, and this evening they had all felt the floor move.

"Then you have brought me exactly what you brought me last time," she said. "Which is nothing, at greater expense."

"I've brought you a bargain instead," Elara said. "I'm going to open it. Not tonight and not this month. But I am. And when it's open, whatever's behind it, you will hear what it is from me directly, before the court hears it, and before it is written anywhere."

"Before your court."

"Before my court."

"And in exchange I let an empress and eighteen strangers camp in the one room my guild has kept for longer than my guild has kept records." The Mistress's voice did not change at all. "You understand you are asking me to be paid in a story."

"You've been paid in nothing at all for centuries," Elara said. "I'm improving your terms."

Somebody among the Arol Batae made a small sound and turned it into a cough.

The Mistress looked at Elara for a long moment. Then she inclined her head about an inch, which Xion suspected was a great deal, and turned to go, and as she turned her eyes went across him and stopped.

"Master Fen," she said.

"Mistress."

"They tell me it's Counselor now. And that you've kept the name."

"I've kept the name."

She stood there a beat longer than the exchange needed, looking at him with an expression he could not read at all and did not much like, and then whatever it was closed over and she was simply an old woman leaving a room.

"Send word up when you have something," she said, to the air, and was gone.

---

They ate late, sitting on crates, and the discussion that mattered happened the way the discussions that matter usually do, which was sideways and with everybody's mouth full.

The surveyor had eleven pages. He was not a man given to excitement and he was, quietly, in a state Xion recognized from his own worst nights: he had a phenomenon and no framework, and it was eating him. The symbol field was not random and it was not a language he could match to anything in any table he had ever worked from. The mechanism completed a cycle of something between nine and ten minutes and then restarted, and a thing that restarts is a thing that is waiting for an input it has not been given. There were no hinges. There was no seam. He had looked for four hundred minutes for a seam.

"It isn't broken," he said, finally, which was the whole of his report and had taken him eleven pages to be sure of.

"No," Xion said. "It isn't."

Elara looked up.

He put down what he was holding. "It knows who you are. It said so, loudly, in front of nineteen witnesses, and it will say it again tomorrow. What it doesn't know is what you want." He heard how that sounded and did not soften it, because she would not have thanked him. "Somebody built a lock that opens for your blood and then put something else in front of it. A sequence. A word. An instruction. Whatever it was, it was known by people, and the people are gone, and it went with them."

"Then it can't be opened."

"Then it can't be opened by us," Xion said. "Which is a different sentence. Nobody in this chamber knows what that is. That's not a failing; there's nothing down here to have learned it from. But it was made. Somebody made it, deliberately, out of parts, and understood exactly what they were doing when they did it, and that means it can be understood." He shrugged. "We need somebody who knows what this is. Not a scholar of the old empire — that's the wrong depth entirely, this is older than the empire by a wide margin. Somebody who takes a mechanism apart and reads it. And they'd have to be extraordinary, because the man who built this was working at a level that makes our best look like a village smith, and I'd rather not insult a village smith."

Vesk said, "And where does the crown find that?"

"I don't know," Xion said. "I know what it is that we need. That's more than we had at breakfast."

Nobody had an answer to it, and after a while the conversation went where tired conversations go, which was to the quality of the rope and whether the porters could be persuaded to stay a second week. Elara did not join in. She sat with her hands around a cup she had stopped drinking from, and her eyes came back to blue at some point, and Xion did not mention it.

---

He woke sometime in the small hours, which meant nothing in a place with no morning, and her bedroll was empty.

She was on the platform. She had gone up without a lamp, and she did not need one, because she had her hand on the door and the whole chamber was full of that slow travelling light, running out and out along the engraving in its patient sequence, saying the thing it had been saying all day to a woman who could not answer it.

He stayed at the bottom of the steps. He did not think she knew he was there and he was not going to tell her.

She was not trying anything. She was not pressing, or tracing, or working the weight. She had her palm flat against it and her forehead nearly touching the brass, and she stood like that a long time while the enormous machinery inside the door turned over and over, patiently, cycling, going nowhere.

Whatever she had come here to get, she had not got it.

But she had come from something. That was down here, five hundred meters under a city that had spent twenty years using her family's name to do harm in, and it was real, and it had lit up the moment she touched it, and nobody could take that off her or argue her out of it or ask her on what basis she counselled anyone.

She would work out the rest. She had worked out harder things with less.

Xion went back to his bedroll and lay in the dark with the blue light moving very faintly on the ceiling forty meters up, and thought about a man who could read a mechanism, and where in the world such a person would be, and had no idea at all.
