The roads got better four days before anything else did.

Xion noticed it the way you notice a fever coming down — not at the moment it happens but afterward, when you realize you have stopped bracing. Somewhere past the third river the ruts went out of the track and did not come back. Then the cuttings had drainage in them, stone-lined channels running the length of every slope, so that a spring rain would go into the ditch and not into the road. Then the bridges. Two of them in one afternoon, both built to the same pattern, both with a mason's mark cut into the abutment in the same place.

Somebody had decided what a bridge was, once, and then built that bridge everywhere.

"They did the whole road," he said, on the fifth evening.

Elara had her boots off and her feet toward the fire and did not open her eyes. "You've been quiet for two days."

"I've been counting way-stations. They're at the same interval. Not roughly — the same. Whoever laid this out sat down with a map before anybody cut a tree."

"Is that admiration?"

"I don't know yet," Xion said, which was honest, and she let it go.

The escort had joined them at the border and it was not what he had expected either. No soldiers. Nine scholars in traveling robes and one baggage master, and the scholars rode well enough and carried nothing sharper than a penknife, and it took Xion the better part of a day to understand that this was the message. Uratha had been told an empress was coming, and had sent nine men and women who read for a living, and had not thought about it twice.

We do not need to send swords to you was one reading.

We do not think of you as the sort of thing swords are for was another, and the two were not the same, and he was not sure which he had been handed.

---

The Ivory Tower came up out of the plain before the city did.

That was the first thing about it: it announced itself from a distance no building had any business announcing itself from, so that for most of a morning it was the only vertical thing in the world and the city under it was still a rumor. White stone, and not the white of local limestone gone pale — a hard, close-grained white that took the light and held it, and Xion knew enough about the cost of moving stone to do the arithmetic on that and then stop doing the arithmetic on that.

They had quarried it somewhere else. They had brought it here. All of it. To make the tallest thing in the country white.

He had grown up in the Noble District and had been inside the Ruby Palace more times than he could count, and the palace was magnificent, and it had never once said anything to him except we have. This said something different and it said it at a volume that was frankly rude.

We care about one thing. We cared enough to build that.

"Well," Elara said, beside him, in the voice she used when she was not going to say the rest of it.

The city, when they reached it, did not undercut the tower, which was the surprising part. Xion had expected the usual arrangement — a wonder standing in the middle of an ordinary place, the way a cathedral stands in a market town — and instead found a city that had clearly been laid out by the same habit of mind. The streets were wide enough for what happened on them and not wider. The districts were legible; you could tell what a quarter was for by looking down it. There were markets, and they were quieter than Kaha'an's markets, and the crowds moved with the particular unhurried purpose of people who all knew where they were going and had known since breakfast.

It was, he thought, a city that had decided what it was.

He could not decide whether that was the most impressive thing he had ever seen or the strangest.

---

He started reading the robes on the second day. There was no decision in it.

The colors were a system. That much was obvious inside an hour — deep blue and green and a dark purple that was clearly the expensive one, worn less often and deferred to more — and the deference was the part he could actually work with, because deference is a thing bodies do, and bodies were the one language he had never needed anybody to teach him.

Then he saw the cuffs, and understood that the colors were only the first system.

Bands, at the wrist. Varying in width and in material, and correlating with age but not reliably, which meant they were not simply years. Two Sapphire scholars passed the delegation in an arcade near the water gardens; the younger one stepped aside to let the elder go first, and the elder did not acknowledge it — not rudely, not even consciously, in the way a man does not acknowledge a door that opens for him because doors open for him. The whole exchange took about a second and a half, and an entire hierarchy had operated in public in front of him, and he could not read a word of it.

There is a language here that every person in this city reads without effort, and I am illiterate in it.

He filed that. He filed a great many things over those two days, which is what he did instead of relaxing.

What he noticed about the scholars themselves was more familiar than he had expected. They moved with a slight abstraction — the habit of people frequently thinking about something other than where their feet were going — and they clustered, and they argued in the specific low-key way of people who have argued with each other for years and enjoy it. He recognized the posture. He had seen it in a back room at three in the morning with four people and one lamp.

These were people who had organized their whole lives around a practice. The practice was thinking rather than mending. But the shape of the life was one he knew.

---

The library district was on the way to the delegation hall, and the escort took them through it, and Xion understood perfectly well that the route was part of the argument.

It did stretch for acres. That was not a figure of speech either; he had assumed it was. Building after building, connected — and that was the detail that got him, because it would have been so much cheaper not to connect them. Covered passages ran between every hall, arcaded, floored, with lamps set at intervals, so that a scholar could go from one collection to another across the whole district and never once step into weather.

Somebody had thought about the scholar's time. Somebody had decided that a scholar's time was worth being protected from rain.

Xion stood in a covered arcade in a foreign country and did the sum of what that had cost, and could not get to the bottom of it.

Beside him, Elara had slowed down.

She did not say anything. She had not said anything for a while, and he was accustomed enough to her silences by now to sort them, and this one was not the political silence or the tired silence. She had stopped walking at a normal pace and was walking at the pace of somebody who is going to be moved out of a room shortly and would like to see it first.

He knew what he was looking at, and he looked away, because it was hers.

Twenty years in that compound, with people who loved her and gave her every single thing they possessed — and what they possessed was a soldier's library — campaigns, tactics, the histories of four wars, a handful of devotional texts, and the sort of collection that accumulates in a building where nobody ever expected to need anything else. Nobody had denied her books. The shelves had simply run out, early, and the world beyond them had been kept at a distance for reasons that had nothing whatever to do with cruelty.

She got her pace back inside twenty seconds, and her face had not changed at any point, and Xion thought: she is not going to mention this, and I am not going to mention it either, and in thirty years she will still be able to describe this arcade.

---

The receiving cohort at the delegation hall were junior scholars trained specifically in receiving, and they were superb at it.

The ceremony was intellectual rather than martial, which was a distinction Xion had not previously known existed and grasped instantly. Nobody presented arms. Instead, a young man with two thin cuff bands delivered a formal greeting that cited the Concord of the Fourth Accession, two prior Balishan embassies, and — with a small correct nod to Elara — the specific legal instrument under which the Valanar line had historically appointed provincial governors in the Urathan territories, which he characterized, accurately, as no longer in force.

It was flattering. It was also a demonstration, delivered with perfect courtesy, that these people had read everything and had read it recently.

Xion thanked him and meant it and thought: they did their homework, and they want us to know they did their homework, and those are two separate acts.

---

The dinner that evening was where he learned the most, and none of it was on the agenda.

Uratha's hospitality was impeccable and specifically its own. Scholars from several towers, the robe colors making the internal politics of the room legible to everybody except the Balishans; a long table; food that was good without being a performance about being good. And the conversation was — he had not expected this — genuinely curious. They asked about the cartels. They asked how a cartel differed from a guild and whether the distinction held under stress. They asked about Elara's accession with an interest that had nothing sycophantic in it and nothing hostile either, the way a naturalist asks about a habitat.

Nobody asked what the embassy was for. It would have been presumptuous. The matter had its proper setting and its proper hour, and this was neither, and the whole table knew it without anyone establishing it.

And then somebody mentioned the Brass Door — not as a probe; as a subject — and half the table turned toward one woman in Sapphire blue with four heavy bands at the cuff.

"Oh," said Iyanna Mereth, who was the History Provost of Uratha and had evidently been waiting all evening. "Well. Where would you like me to begin."

---

What followed was the happiest twenty minutes anybody at that table had.

There were manuscripts, she said, and rather a lot of them, and what one had to understand first was that most of her colleagues did not take them seriously. References to a door of brass beneath the tunnels of what was now Balisha turned up in sources thousands of years old and turned up repeatedly, in bodies of work with no relation to one another, which was either significant or exactly the kind of thing that happens when a good story gets into circulation. The Sapphire position — her position — was that a source everybody finds easy to dismiss is the source that requires the most care, since nobody has bothered to check it in four hundred years.

The theories, she said, were extensive.

Some had it as a passage between worlds, which was popular in the devotional literature and nowhere else. Some had it as a treasury of the Ancients, sealed against looting by mechanisms whose principles were lost — that one recurred in the mercantile sources, she noted, which told you something about who was writing them. There was a persistent tradition, appearing independently in four separate manuscript families, that it was the gate of Lathion, the lost City of Gears, a civilization of such mechanical sophistication that later generations had reasonably concluded it must be an invention.

And there was the underworld reading: that the door opened onto a road by which the dead could be fetched back, if the traveler were sufficiently brave, or sufficiently stupid.

"On which," Mereth said, "the sources are entirely unanimous. Nobody has ever managed it."

Somebody laughed. Somebody else asked about the four manuscript families and got a genuine answer and then a better question back.

Xion sat with his wine and watched Elara listen to all of it with the pleasant, slightly interested expression of a woman being told a folk tale at a dinner, and thought about the fact that this woman had walked through Dead Man's Passage in a trance and put her hand on that door and made a chamber the size of a cathedral light up under it, and had said nothing about any of that to anybody in this room and was not going to.

He did not think anything in particular about the list. It was a list of legends and one of them was Lathion and none of them was more credible than the others and Mereth herself had said the whole body of it was usually treated as elaboration.

He was, at that moment, more interested in the fact that he still could not read the cuffs.

---

The other thing he noticed at that dinner he noticed the way you notice a draught.

The people who brought the food were not scholars and did not wear robes, and nobody at the table looked at them.

That was all. There was no unkindness in it. Nobody was curt; nobody was served badly; the arrangement functioned beautifully. It was simply that seventeen people who had spent an hour being genuinely, warmly interested in the constitutional mechanics of a foreign cartel system did not, at any point in that hour, direct a syllable or a glance at any of the six people moving around the table.

Xion had spent eight years in rooms where the person who mattered most was the one nobody was looking at. He caught himself watching a woman refill a jug and being the only man at that table doing it.

He filed that too, and had no idea what to do with it, and it stayed filed for a very long time.

---

He and Elara had about four minutes in the passage outside their rooms, which by that stage of their working life together was three minutes more than they generally needed.

The proposal was ready. The framing was agreed. There was nothing left to build and both of them knew it, and the conversation consisted mostly of confirming that neither of them intended to improvise.

"You'll sleep," she said. It was not quite a question.

"I'll sleep."

She looked at him for the length of time it took her to decide not to say the other thing, and then said goodnight, and went in.

---

Leowin Draskin was younger than the title had led him to expect, and considerably more contained.

He had been elected five years ago out of the Department of Cosmological Studies, which was a fact Xion had read in a brief and had not known what to do with, and which made a great deal more sense the moment the man came into the chamber. There was nothing of the courtier in him and nothing of the priest. What he had was the specific stillness of somebody accustomed to holding a very large idea in his head for a long time without dropping it, and the ceremony of his authority had been practiced so far past performance that it had gone invisible.

He greeted Elara with genuine respect, and Xion, watching for it, could tell what kind. It was not the deference a man gives a monarch. It was the acknowledgment one substantial person makes to another on first meeting, which is a rarer and more useful thing, and which Elara returned exactly.

Then they sat down, and the opening ceremony took four minutes, and it was over.

---

Elara put the proposal on the table herself, in the shape Xion had built for it, and she did not decorate it.

Balisha would withdraw its demand for the Scholar's Crown. Not suspend. Not defer to a later administration. Withdraw — formally, permanently, in a sealed instrument, with an acknowledgment on the record that Uratha held the Crown legitimately and that no future Balishan sovereign would revive the claim. Three hundred and some years of standing diplomatic position, closed in one document.

In exchange, Uratha would send its best to the Brass Door.

Xion was watching Draskin's face when the Crown clause landed, because watching faces was what he was there for, and what he saw was not surprise. Draskin had known what was coming; a man in that chair with the Onyx Tower reporting to him knows the shape of an embassy before it crosses his border.

What Xion saw was something letting go.

It was very small. It was in the shoulders and it was in the hands, and it was the specific quality of a tension that has been held so long and so evenly that its owner has stopped noticing the effort of holding it — and then does not have to any more. He had seen it in patients who had been braced against a pain for weeks and were finally given something for it. The body does not celebrate. It simply stops doing a thing it had forgotten it was doing.

Xion filed it, and did not know what it meant, and did not have time to think about it, because Elara was already onto the second term.

---

The second term was discovery rights, and that was where the actual negotiation lived.

Elara stated it plainly: whatever was found behind the door, Balisha held first rights. Material, artifact, knowledge, anything.

Draskin did not accept that and had never been going to. He opened where anyone would have opened — full rights to everything behind the door, on a reasoning that was, Xion noted with some professional pleasure, entirely sound. Balisha was relinquishing a demand it had never been able to enforce and could not enforce now. Uratha was committing named specialists, transport, and an unknown number of months to an outcome nobody could describe. The asymmetry was real, and Draskin laid it out without heat and without any of the theater a lesser man would have used.

Elara declined it, because it was an anchor and both of them knew it was an anchor.

She countered with academic rights: full access to anything of scholarly value behind that door — to read, to catalogue, to copy, and to publish — and she reminded the table, without making a production of it, that three centuries of diplomatic deadlock were being dissolved in the same instrument.

Draskin narrowed. Academic and technological: anything that constituted learning, which would include mechanism, method, and process.

Elara held. Academic only.

And then there was a pause, and Xion read it and knew immediately that it was a different kind of pause from the three that had come before it.

The earlier ones had been the ritual — two competent people moving through positions that neither of them believed was final, in the order such positions are conventionally moved through. This one was the floor. Both of them had run the same arithmetic at the same moment and arrived at the same place: that pressing further would not improve the terms, and might cost the table. Neither of them said so. Neither of them had to. Xion had watched his father find that exact silence perhaps forty times, and had learned to recognize it before he was fifteen, and it had never once looked as clean as this.

"Academic," Draskin said. "Acceptable."

---

The division they arrived at was the only one available, which was why it worked.

Nobody in that chamber knew what was behind the door. That was the whole difficulty and everybody at the table had it. You cannot rank claims on a thing whose nature is undefined — there is no honest way to agree who takes precedence over something that might be a treasury, or a library, or four hundred years of dust. Priority is unusable. The only cut that can be made is a cut by kind: material to Balisha, academic to Uratha, and let the door decide which of them turns out to have won.

It required neither party to guess correctly. It only required both of them to agree the rule before the door was open, which was a thing they could actually do.

Xion, listening to it settle, was careful not to admire it more than it deserved. It was not clever. It was the only shape the problem permitted, and two capable people had found it in about ninety minutes because there was nothing else to find. That it happened to give Uratha the category Uratha always fought for, and Balisha the category Balisha was in desperate need of, was not why they chose it.

It was why it would hold.

---

Draskin signed with a pen that was older than anybody in the room.

Lacquered wood, worn pale at the grip, with a metal nib that had been reset at least once by somebody good. It was clearly an instrument with a history, and it came out of a case, and there was a small correct pause while everyone at the table understood that they were being shown something.

That part was ceremony and Xion read it as ceremony.

Then Draskin put the nib to the parchment and did not write.

It was not long. A man clearing his throat takes longer. If Xion had been looking at the document instead of at the man he would have missed it entirely, and every other Balishan in that chamber did miss it — he checked, afterward, by asking two of them separately about the signing and getting two accounts in which nothing whatever had happened.

But he was looking at the man, because looking at the man was his function, and the pause was not the ceremonial one. It had a different quality altogether. It was the pause of somebody who has decided, and committed, and is going to do it, and for whom the doing of it still costs something.

Then the pen came down, and the ink was very dark, and the signature was precise.

---

Both delegations came out of that chamber satisfied, and both of them were right to be.

Elara had given up a demand that had never once been enforceable, kept material rights over an unknown, and secured the foreign expertise she had crossed a continent to get. Draskin had surrendered nothing Uratha valued, won the scholarly access his institution existed to want, and closed three hundred years of diplomatic irritation with one signature. The terms were fair. Xion had built the thing and watched it survive contact with a serious opponent, and there was no part of it he would have argued differently.

He found it reassuring at every level except one.

---

He worked at it on the walk back through the library district, under the arcades, past the rain that was not falling on anybody.

The negotiation had been sharp. Draskin had anchored where a good negotiator anchors, narrowed where a good negotiator narrows, and taken the floor when the floor arrived. Nothing in ninety minutes had been out of place. Xion ran the whole session again and it tracked — every position, every concession, every pause.

Until the pen.

A man does not control himself that precisely for ninety minutes and then lose the thread for two seconds in front of a signature. That is not how it works. Control of that quality does not slip at the end; if it slips at all it slips in the middle, under pressure, and there had been no pressure at the end because the pressure had been over.

Which meant the pause was not a slip.

He turned it over the length of the arcade and got no further than that. He did not know what it meant. He was entirely certain that it meant something, and he was aware that those two statements together were worth nothing at all to anybody, and he intended to say it to her anyway.

---

She was ahead of him.

He got about four words into it, in the passage outside their rooms, before she said, "The pen," and he stopped.

"You saw it."

"I was signing. I was looking at his hands." Elara leaned against the frame of her door. "He hovered. It was nothing. It was very much not nothing."

"You didn't react."

"No." A small, dry look. "I've had a good teacher in not reacting. Two of them, and one of them was your father." She let that sit exactly as long as it deserved and no longer. "You never let the other side see that you've seen it. You take the information and you keep signing."

They stood in the passage a moment.

"It doesn't change anything," Xion said at last, and heard himself testing it as he said it.

"No. It doesn't." She was working it the same way he was. "The terms are fair, and I'd sign them again tomorrow, and their people are going to be genuinely useful — everything I've seen here says they'll be good at this. And if there is something they want from behind that door, then as of this afternoon they are contractually obliged to tell us when they find it." She shrugged. "Which is better than the arrangement we had this morning, when they weren't obliged to tell us anything and we didn't know there was anything to tell."

"So the deal is good."

"The deal is good," Elara agreed. "And there is something about it we do not understand. I've stopped assuming those two can't both be true."

She said goodnight and went in.

Xion stood in the passage for a while afterward, in a beautiful building in a country that had thought carefully about where to put its lamps, and could not have said what was wrong, and could not stop looking for it.
