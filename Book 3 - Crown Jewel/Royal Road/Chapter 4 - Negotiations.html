Kael found her in four lines.

He brought them himself, which he did not have to do; there were three men whose entire function was carrying paper from the registry to the south gallery, and Kael Issamil had walked past all of them and waited outside a door for twenty minutes rather than send it up.

"It isn't in the seizure inventory," he said. "It was never going to be. Those are lists of property." He put the sheet down on the table and turned it so it faced Xion. "It's in the other books."

The other books. Xion had carried them out of six sites in a single night at the end of the worst month of his life, and had not looked at them since, and would have been content never to look at them again. The crown had been reading them for months. A team of eleven clerks in a room without windows, working through the private arrangements of a man who had understood since before Xion was born which of his three rooms would ever be shown to a magistrate.

Four lines. A payment, to a man, for the keeping of one person. Repeated eleven times.

And then not repeated.

"Eleven days," Xion said.

"Eleven payments." Kael did not soften it. "In a book like that, a release isn't written down as a release. It's written down as somebody ceasing to be an expense."

Xion sat with the sheet in front of him and did the rest of it himself, which took about as long as reading it had. Eleven days of paying a man to hold a weaver, and then a decision that a twelfth was not worth making. They had wanted the records — every name, every district, every date, in a cipher that two people in the city could read — and the records had been ash on the floor of her own shop before the cartel men reached the shelf, because she had put them there herself with her own lamp while he stood in front of her and failed to stop her.

So they had held her for eleven days to get a thing that had ceased to exist before they ever took her. And then, on the twelfth, somebody had decided she was not worth the money.

Kael watched him work it out and did not help.

"It doesn't say which," Xion said, when he got there.

"No. It doesn't." Kael did not soften that either. "A man who is let go stops being an expense. So does a man who isn't. The book was kept by somebody who considered the distinction his own business and nobody else's, and there is nothing in those four lines that decides it."

Xion looked at the sheet for a while longer.

He had known this was possible since the morning he asked the question in front of a clerk with ink on his hand, and he had known it every week since, in the way you know a thing you have deliberately not sat down with. It was a different weight in the room now that it had a date attached. Eleven days, and then a decision, and then four inches of blank ruled paper where the twelfth entry would have gone.

"Then we're not finished," he said.

"No," Kael agreed, and turned the folio around, and put down the second sheet.

---

It was the water levy that found her, which Xion had said it would.

He had told the undersecretary as much in the first ten seconds of asking — she owned her shop outright, which means she paid her own water levy; there will be a token roll with her on it, or there was. He had meant it as the only handhold he could offer a man being sent into a great deal of material. It had not occurred to him that the roll would still be running.

It was still running. A token drawn every quarter without a gap, in her own name, against an address in the Middle District, and the current quarter's was among them — issued six weeks ago by a Water cartel clerk who would have looked at her across a counter and not thought about it at all.

Xion read the line twice.

Six weeks ago somebody had put a token in her hand, and the quarter it belonged to had not yet run out.

"She's alive," he said, and heard his own voice do something on the second word, and did not look up until it had stopped.

---

The address was two streets from the shop.

The shop was shuttered and it was hers, on every document that predated the cartel, and she could not go into it — because the Grain cartel had taken it over six journals, and the crown had seized everything the Grain cartel held, and somewhere in an inventory that Xion had personally helped compile there was a line that said so.

She had been walking past it to buy bread. For months.

He had been in this city for most of those months. He had walked the Middle District a dozen times on reconstruction business and had gone down Vessel Street twice in a week without once turning north, and the only reason for any of it was that a man behind a desk had once looked up from a register and been sorry, and had been sorry very convincingly — and he had let the answer stand, because a man who is busy enough is never short of a reason, and he had been very busy indeed.

Kael offered to come. Xion said no.

Farleen didn't offer. She walked him as far as the corner of the street and stopped there, and made nothing whatever of stopping, and was still there an hour and a half later when he came back out.

---

The room was small and cheap and clean, and it was arranged the way a workroom is arranged.

That was the thing that got him, before anything was said. The chair was set where the light was best. The basket was within reach of the chair. Everything else in the room — the bed, the shelf, the small cold stove — had been pushed to the edges to make that possible, and the whole arrangement had the unmistakable quality of something worked out over months by somebody who intended to go on working.

She was in the chair with the basket in her lap, unpicking a row.

Backward, stitch by stitch, the way she had unpicked a row every time he had ever walked into a room she was in. One thread miscounted the day before and the entire row came out, without hesitation and without complaint, because a structure with a flaw in it is a structure that comes out.

She was sixty-one years old and living in one room and she was still good at it.

She heard his step before he was through the door.

"Master Fen."

No ceremony in it at all. The same two words in the same register she had used every morning for eight years, dropped into the air the way you would greet a man who had come by yesterday. He had put that name into the imperial record three weeks ago, in a session with two cartel heads and three noble houses in the room, and it had been the most deliberate act of his life. To her it was eight years old and had never once been in question.

She did not know she had done anything. He stood in the doorway for a moment and got his face in order.

---

She did not ask where he had been.

It took him some time to notice that this was happening, because absence is harder to see than presence. She did not mention the eleven days. She did not mention the man who had been paid, or the journals, or the shop. She did not ask about the Empress, or the appointment, or the war, or his father — and his father's name had been in every mouth in Kaha'an for the better part of a year.

She asked whether he had eaten. She said the light in here was better than the shop's had been, which was the only reference she made to the shop, and she made it as a fact about light.

And she did not forgive him, because as far as she was concerned there was nothing there to forgive. The transaction she believed had taken place was one she had completed on her own terms and closed, and she did not reopen closed accounts.

He had come with a list.

He had made it three nights ago and revised it twice, and every item on it was real and immediate and within the gift of the office he now held. A shop — the lease, the frontage, the fittings, the stock, all of it, in a better street than the one she had lost. A pension against the seizure fund, in perpetuity, entered as a first charge. A house, if she wanted a house. Her name on the register of persons the crown acknowledged the Grain cartel had wronged, which was being compiled at that moment, by the body he chaired.

He did not offer any of it as charity. He offered it as owed, because he believed it was owed, and because he had not understood until he said it aloud that owed was the form of the offer she could least accept.

She let him finish. She always had.

Then she said, "No," and put the row down in her lap, and explained it once.

If he paid her, then there had been an account. There had never been an account. The entire point of the thing he had done for eight years was that people took it and walked out owing nothing — and she had given him eight years of cover on exactly those terms, deliberately, knowing what it cost and choosing it, and she was not going to sit in a rented room and watch him convert either direction of that into arithmetic now that he finally had the arithmetic to do it with.

She did not say I'm giving you something. You don't get to write it down. She had said it once, standing at her own counter with her hand on a lamp, and she was not a woman who repeated herself. It had not become a different argument merely because he had become a different man.

He sat with that for a while.

He had known, walking up the stairs, that the list might not survive contact with her. He had not known that it would be dismantled in about forty seconds by an argument he had helped build.

And then, sitting there with all four items refused and nothing left to offer, he found the fifth thing — and he found it because the other four were gone, which was the only order in which he could have found it.

"Your shop is still standing," he said.

"I know where my shop is."

"It's in an inventory." He did not let anything into it. "The cartel took it over the journals. The crown seized what the cartel held. It's sitting in a schedule of impounded property with a number against it, and I chair the body that has the schedule." He watched her hands stop. "I'm not offering you a shop. I'm telling you that somebody stole a building from you and the people holding it now know it was stolen, and there's a line in a book that has to be struck out."

Janice looked at him for a long moment.

"That isn't a gift," she said. It was not a question. She was checking a figure.

"No."

"There's nothing in it to write down."

"There's nothing in it to write down," he agreed. "It was never anybody's to give you. It's a correction."

He was aware, saying it, that he was not being generous. He was being correct, and he had reached correct by having generous taken away from him three times in a row, and he was not sure the distinction reflected well on him. It did not matter. Correct was the only register this woman had ever accepted anything in.

She said, "Then strike it out."

That was all. She did not thank him at length. She did not soften, or revise her position on the pension, or the house, or the register — those stayed refused, and stayed refused for the rest of his life. What she did was take one thing out of five and leave the other four on the table, and Xion understood, watching her do it, that she had never in eight years been merely stubborn. She had a line. It fell precisely between what was returned and what was given, and she had located it inside a situation nobody had ever put her in before, in the time it took to check two figures.

Then she picked the row back up.

"It won't fix your clinic," she said.

---

He had solved it, and she declined to let that stand, and it cost her one sentence.

"The shop's a building," she said, working. "What made it worth anything was that a man with a false name worked in the back of it and nobody knew who he was." She drew a thread through. "That's over. You put the name in the record. Everyone I buy fish from knows it."

"I know."

"A room the city can point at isn't a clinic. It's a room people are seen going into." She said it the way she would correct a miscount. "You'll get the trade back. You won't get the arrangement back. Those aren't the same and I'd rather you didn't decide they were on the way down the stairs."

She was not being cruel. It did not occur to her that there was anything in it to be cruel with. She was being accurate, which was the only service she had ever performed for him, and she performed it flatly and immediately after taking the deed, because to have softened it there would have been to make the deed a payment.

He sat in a rented room with a returned building in his hands and understood that he had solved the smaller half.

And underneath that, quietly, the other thing.

He could have done all of it anyway. Not one item on that list had required her consent. A lease can be signed before anybody thinks to tell the tenant. A pension can be entered against a fund by a man with a seal. A name can go onto a register without the person on it ever being asked, and the register was being compiled by his own office, and there was nobody in Kaha'an who would have queried it.

He was First Counselor to the Crown. She was a woman in a rented room with a work-basket.

The distance between what he could have done and what she had permitted was the exact width of the country his father had operated in for twenty years, and Xion recognized it instantly, the way you recognize a smell. There was nothing revelatory about it. He had lived in that country himself, for a stretch of months he could put dates to, and had known the entire time exactly where he was standing.

And the shop did not settle it. That was the part he kept turning over afterward. He had found the one thing on the list he could have done without asking her, and had asked her — and it was worth precisely nothing as reassurance, because a man who asks once has established only that he asked once.

---

The question came near the end, and it came with no weight on it at all, folded into the middle of a sentence about whether the stove drew properly.

"—and I've been meaning to ask. Did you ever start charging?"

Eight years. She had asked it on mornings when there were four people waiting and on mornings when there were none, and she had asked it the day Tam was taken, hours before he was taken, over a silver coin. She asked it now the way she had always asked it, which was without any expectation of a different answer and without any intention of arguing about it.

Xion opened his mouth and found that he could not answer.

Because the honest answer was not no.

The clinic was finished. Master Fen had ended somewhere in the middle of the worst year of his life, not through anyone's malice, but because a name that half the city could put to a face stopped being a room where a man could bleed and be poor and be helped without it going into somebody's account. There had been no last patient. There had been a slow reduction and then a stopping, and he could not now say with any confidence when it had been, which was its own answer.

He was the second man in the empire. He had a title, a seal, an office, a schedule of sessions, and a chair at the table where the whole city's arithmetic got done.

He had not put his hands on a sick person in longer than he could name.

She was the last living human being who would think to measure him by that particular yardstick. She had picked it up without ceremony, in the middle of a sentence about a stove, and laid it against him, and it did not reach.

"No," he said. "I stopped."

Janice did not look up. She drew the thread through, and turned the work a quarter, and considered the row.

"Well," she said. "That's a bigger hole in your books than there is in mine."

And she went back to it.

She did not tell him what to do about it. She had never once in eight years told him what to do about anything. She told him what was true and let him carry it out of the room himself, which was the entire method, and which he had never previously had cause to notice was a method.

---

He stood in the door for a moment on his way out.

He was waiting for it. He understood that he was waiting for it about half a second after he started, which was too late to stop. Twice she had ended it for him — on the day the name was invented, and on the night the journals burned — and both times the last thing in the room had been Go, Master Fen, and both times he had gone, because she had told him where he was needed and she had never once been wrong about it.

She had gone back to the row.

There was no boy in a square. There was no ward waiting. There was nothing in the whole of Kaha'an that she believed he had to be at, and she did not manufacture one out of kindness, because she did not have kindness of that kind and never had.

So he had to leave under his own authority.

He got as far as the corner, where Farleen was, and did not say anything for two streets, and she did not ask.

---

The session on Gunastran took an hour, which was about fifty minutes longer than the decision needed.

Nobody argued about the destination. Valorian had made the case in one sentence a fortnight earlier and nobody had improved on it since: the door was, by every appearance anybody had, an engineering problem, and there was one nation on the continent whose entire self-regard rested on being able to say what a mechanism was for. Silvanno said one sentence about the Tor'anar — that the corps had been asked, had come, had tried, and had said plainly that the instrument was wrong — and the room let it lie, because it was a settled fact and not a wound, and because the man who had said it had made it easy to leave alone.

What Xion argued about was the shape of the approach.

"Not a state visit," he said. "You don't go."

Elara raised an eyebrow at that but did not interrupt.

"If the Empress of Balisha crosses a continent to stand in front of the Industrial Consortium, then this is a matter of state, and a matter of state implies we have something to bring to it. We don't. What we have is a locked door and nine months." He turned a page he did not need to turn. "Send a small delegation with a technical brief. Let it look like an inquiry, because it is one. The moment it looks like an overture we have told them we need this, and I would rather they found that out slowly."

"You'll go," Elara said.

"I'll go."

"And what are you authorized to tell them?"

"A sealed brass door of ancient construction. Visibly mechanical. Unresponsive to local attempts at operation. Historical value unknown." He said it in the flat administrative cadence of a brief, which was what it was. "Not the treasury. Not the scale. Not what we think might be behind it, since we don't know."

Somebody at the far end of the table said something about candor between friendly powers, and Xion agreed with him pleasantly and did not change a word of it. You do not lead with your desperation. He had learned that in a house where the man across the breakfast table would have used it inside a week.

---

Gunastran received them correctly and without any theater at all, and Xion found it, privately, restful.

There was no banquet. There was no delegation at the gate with a formula to recite. There was a meeting room with good light and a long table of a wood he did not recognize, and four engineers who had been briefed on the technical parameters two days before and had evidently spent both of them thinking about it.

They started asking questions inside four minutes.

"Surface material — you say brass. Is it brass, or does it look like brass?"

"It looks like brass. It hasn't aged like brass."

That got a glance between two of them. The lead was a woman of about fifty named Adren Thulgrav with ink on three fingers and a way of leaning back when she was thinking that made her look bored and was the opposite of bored.

"Thermal," she said. "Has anyone applied a differential? Heat one region, cool an adjacent one, watch for expansion behavior at the join?"

"No."

"Directional pressure? Not force — pressure, applied along a plane rather than perpendicular."

"No."

"When your mages worked it." She did not put any weight on your mages, which he noted and appreciated. "Was there resistance, or was there nothing? Those are entirely different failures. A thing that pushes back is engaged. A thing that does nothing may simply not have been addressed."

Xion thought about three people on a platform, narrowing.

"There was nothing," he said. "It was extremely engaged with the Empress and it was not engaged with them at all. Their word was that the working had nowhere to stand."

Thulgrav made a note. One of the others asked about the cycle — nine to ten minutes, restarting — and when Xion confirmed it, the man sat back and said "hah" to nobody at all, the way people do when a small private thought has just pleased them, and Xion felt something in his chest come loose that had been tight for two weeks.

They were not being polite. They were leaning in. Two of them had begun, without discussing it, to sketch the same thing from different angles on the same sheet of paper.

For about twenty minutes it was the best room he had been in since the winter.

---

Then the consortium's factor began to speak, and the room did not change temperature so much as change category.

He was not hostile. Xion wanted to be clear about that afterward, when he reported it, because the word people reach for is obstructive and it would have been wrong. He was a professional asking the questions his position obliged him to ask, in order, from the top.

Who bore transport for specialists and equipment across a continent, and on what schedule of advance.

Who indemnified the consortium against loss or injury to persons in an unmapped subterranean site under the jurisdiction of a foreign crown.

Who held rights in any mechanism, process, or principle recovered — not the objects, which were plainly Balisha's, but the knowledge, which was the only part Gunastran had ever considered worth owning.

What guaranteed return was offered if the door proved unworkable, and what portion of that return was payable regardless of outcome.

Under what enforcement, in what forum, before which arbiter, in the event of a dispute between a private consortium and a sovereign crown.

Each question was reasonable. Each question was one Xion would have asked. Together they were a wall four hundred years thick, and he stood in front of it and answered what he could, which was not much of it, and then answered honestly that he was not in a position to answer the rest today.

Thulgrav watched that exchange with the expression of a person who knows exactly how a thing ends and would have preferred a different ending. She did not intervene. It was not her table.

The factor's answer, when it came, was polite and correct and final. Gunastran would be delighted to engage on appropriate contractual terms. Those terms, as presently described, were not available. Should Balisha produce a fuller proposal — one addressing transport, indemnity, rights in recovered principle, and guaranteed return — the consortium would review it with interest.

Xion thanked them, and meant it, and shook the engineer's hand at the door, and she held it half a second longer than the courtesy required.

---

The clarity arrived in the corridor, which was the correct place for it, because the corridor was where Gunastran said what Gunastran actually was.

Every door hinge fitted. Every joint square. A brass rail along the wall at the height a hand naturally falls, polished by forty years of hands, and behind a glazed panel a water clock keeping time to a tolerance nobody in Kaha'an could have measured. This was a nation that did things properly, that had asked him exactly the right questions, that had wanted the problem — and that could not touch it, because the thing standing between them was not skill and was not goodwill.

It was that they wanted paying.

And there was nothing wrong with wanting paying. That was the part that took him the length of the corridor to arrive at. The consortium had not failed him and had not insulted him. They had done the ordinary thing that every competent body in the world does, and the ordinary thing was fatal, because the resources that would satisfy those terms were sitting behind the door they needed Gunastran to open.

We cannot buy this.

He stopped walking. Someone behind him stopped too, and then pretended to be examining the water clock.

They could not buy it. Not from Gunastran, and not from anyone who wanted buying — which meant the entire category was closed, and had been closed before they set out, and the trip had cost three weeks to establish a thing that was structurally true.

So the question was not who else builds. It was not who might be more generous, because nobody was going to be more generous; nations are not generous.

The question was whether the Balishan crown held anything at all that was not money.

He stood in a Gunastran corridor with his hand on a rail worn smooth by other people's hands and went through the inventory of the Balishan crown, item by item, the way he had once gone through a body, and somewhere on the road home he found it.

---

The debrief was short, because there was not much to report and Xion did not pad it.

Gunastran's engineers were excellent. Their interest had been genuine and technical and immediate. The consortium's terms were terms Balisha could not meet, and would not be able to meet until the financial position changed, and the financial position would not change until they had solved the problem they had gone to Gunastran to solve.

There was a small silence around the table while everybody did that circle once.

"So the option's closed," Elara said.

"That option's closed."

She caught the article. She always did.

"Uratha," Xion said.

He was careful about the next part, because it was the part everyone got wrong.

"Not because Urathan scholars are better people than Gunastran engineers. They aren't. If we walked into the Ivory Tower and asked them to come and look at a mysterious door in our basement out of love for knowledge, they would decline as politely and as finally as the consortium did, and they would be right to. Their scholars' time is worth money to them. Everybody's is."

"Then what's different?"

"What's different isn't them," Xion said. "It's what we're sitting on."

---

He explained the Crown the way you explain a thing you have known so long you have to remember that other people find it tedious.

A divine artifact, and not a symbolic one. In its active years it had done something that no other object in the recorded world had done: it accumulated. Each worthy bearer's understanding passed into it and forward, so that a man who put it on inherited the considered judgment of every man who had worn it before him. A library that wore itself. Balisha had held it at the height of the empire. When Uratha was made a province the Crown was lent, formally, with the expectation of return.

Then the empire fell. Uratha kept it. Balisha asked for it back. Uratha refused.

That had been three hundred and some years ago, and every Balishan ruler since had formally demanded its return, and every Urathan ruler had formally refused, and the exchange had gone on so long past the point of anyone expecting a different outcome that it had stopped being a dispute and become a liturgy. Balisha performed the demand because stopping would require saying out loud that they had never had the leverage to enforce it.

"So we stop," Xion said.

The room did not immediately understand him.

"Formally. Permanently. In writing, sealed, on the record, and communicated to the Ivory Tower. Balisha withdraws the claim, closes it, and acknowledges that Uratha holds the Scholar's Crown legitimately." He let that sit for exactly as long as it needed. "In exchange, Uratha sends scholars to the door."

---

He gave them the arithmetic from the other side of the table, because that was where it looked best.

From Uratha's chair: a handful of scholars travel to Kaha'an at Balisha's expense and look at something ancient, which might be extraordinary and might be, as Silvanno had put it in the first session, a store room with four hundred years of dust in it — and they cannot know which in advance, and neither can anyone else. In return, a dispute that has sat on their books since before their grandfathers' grandfathers is closed in their favor, permanently, with a Balishan seal on it. They never hear the demand again.

Even if the door led to nothing, they would not have lost anything real. A three-century diplomatic headache resolved, in exchange for a funded scholarly excursion.

"They'd call that a bargain," Xion said. "I would."

An advisor got most of the way into a sentence about the relationship with Uratha being delicate and Xion cut across it with the actual word.

"Complicated," he said. "It's complicated. Let's use the word; we'll need it later."

Then a silence, and then somebody said the thing the room was thinking, which was that withdrawing the claim would be read as capitulation. As Balisha conceding, after three hundred years, that Uratha had been right to refuse. Three centuries of formal position, abandoned in an afternoon by a court six weeks old.

Xion had been waiting for it, and he did not enjoy it as much as he had expected to.

"We are not giving up the Scholar's Crown," he said. "We do not have the Scholar's Crown. No Balishan has touched it in three hundred years and no Balishan is going to. What we are giving up is the performance of wanting it — the biennial letter, the formal demand, the ritual refusal that has never once moved the thing an inch closer to Kaha'an and costs us a little dignity every time it runs."

He put his hand flat on the table, which he had watched his father do a thousand times and had not intended to do.

"I'm proposing we trade the appearance of a claim we cannot enforce for a thing we actually need. That's all this is. You cannot surrender what you do not possess."

He did not tell them it was painless. That was the mistake available, and he had seen men make it, and it never held. He let the discomfort be real — there were families in that room whose grandfathers had drafted those letters — and he let them sit in it, because he was fairly sure the argument won on its own and did not need him standing on it.

It won on its own.

Elara asked one question, near the end, which none of the advisors had thought to ask: not what Uratha would gain, but what it would mean to them. Why would they say yes.

"Because they've never had it acknowledged," Xion said. "They kept the Crown because they could. They refused for three hundred years because there were no consequences for refusing. What they have never once had is Balisha saying, formally, on paper, with a seal — you were entitled to it. For a nation that organizes itself around being right about things, that isn't a minor consideration. That's the record corrected."

He watched her take that, and file it, and approve it, all in about the same second.

"Then draft it," she said. "Both instruments. I want to see the withdrawal before it goes anywhere."

The meeting was over. The advisors began to gather paper.

---

She did not leave when the room did.

Xion was squaring the brief under his arm when he realized the sound of the chamber had changed, and he looked up and there were two of them and the Arol Batae at the far doors, and Elara was leaning against the edge of the table with her arms folded, watching him the way she had watched him at the window six weeks ago.

"How do you know they'll take it?"

He could have managed that. He was aware of the version that managed it — three sentences about Urathan institutional psychology, delivered warmly, and she would have accepted them, because he had just spent an hour earning the right to be believed in that room and the credit was still good.

"Honestly, Elara? I don't."

She didn't move.

"We're short on options and shorter on money, and this is the only door I've found that doesn't have a price on it we can't pay. It's all in how we package it — the withdrawal has to reach them looking like a resolution and not like a request, because the moment it reads as a request they'll price it, and the second they price it we're back in the room I just came out of." He shrugged, and heard how small it was, and did not repair it. "We do it right and they'll take it. I think. I hope."

"You hope."

"I'm not going to stand here and tell you it's certain. It isn't." He set the brief down. "It's better than what we had this morning, which was nothing at all, and I'd rather bring you an honest maybe than a confident lie. You've had enough of those in this building."

Elara took that in without moving.

"Damn it," she said. "You're right." And then, on a long breath, "Fine. We'll go with this."

And that was all of it. She did not thank him for the honesty and she did not remark on it. She pushed off the table and went to find Vesk about the escort, and Xion understood that the asking had been the remark.
