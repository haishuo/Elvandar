It took them four days to get down to it, and three of those days were spent being careful.

Xion had expected them to run. They had a name for the place and four cross-references pointing at it, and he had watched men with less than that dig on a rumour. Instead Ormuth put two of his people onto the corridor inscriptions above the descent and would not be hurried, and when a junior man asked why they were reading the signage on a road they already knew went down, Ormuth said that he had been in exactly one place in his life he could not go back into, and that he had gone into it fast, and had been paying for the first ten minutes of it ever since.

Nobody asked again.

Brennek found the conveyor on the third day, which was not difficult; it was where the road said it would be. It was a smaller thing than the one that had carried them down from the Door — no ornament on it anywhere, a plain moving floor in a plain shaft, wide enough for four men abreast or one loaded cart, worn in two grooved lines down the middle where a great many centuries of feet had gone. Whatever the Haureni had used it for, it had not been ceremony. It was the sort of thing you built to get people to work.

They went down on the fourth morning, if morning meant anything, which it did not.

Xion rode with Elara and Brennek and three of the senior men. The shaft wall was solid for the first stretch and then began to open — long horizontal gaps, unglazed, evidently there so that whoever was riding could see where he was — and through them the central shaft showed itself in slices. Levels below. Levels above. Each of them turning, and none of them turning together, so that the gaps came past at a rate that had nothing to do with the rate of the descent.

He tried to hold the geometry of it and gave up. The city was not complicated; it was doing several simple things at once at speeds his body wanted to reconcile and could not, and after a few minutes of it he had the beginnings of the particular nausea he associated with reading in a moving cart.

He looked at Elara instead.

She was doing what she did on every descent, which was to look down. Not out at the shaft, not up at where they had come from. Down, past her own boots, at the part of the city that was still below them. She had done it at the Door, on the long ride, for the better part of an hour without speaking. She did it now.

The pulse was louder here. That was the thing he had not expected and could not stop noticing — that it had a volume, and that the volume went up as you descended, the way a heartbeat comes up through a chest when you put your ear against it instead of your hand.

Three seconds. Three seconds.

Nobody on the platform said anything the whole way down.

---

The corridor at the bottom was broad.

That registered before anything else did, because everything on the Crown had been either a boulevard or a working passage and this was neither. It was perhaps four times the width of the conveyor and it went straight, and it had been built by someone who wanted a person walking along it to understand that they were going somewhere. There were no shops off it. There were no doors. It was a corridor whose entire function was the arrival at the end of it.

They walked it in a quiet Xion could not immediately classify.

It was not the quiet of caution. They had been past caution for days; men were sleeping in the alcoves off a road in a dead city and complaining about the bedding. It took him about a hundred paces to work out that it was the quiet of a queue — of people who know exactly what is at the front, and have decided, without discussing it, not to be the one who spoils it.

Two of the scholars were walking faster than the rest. They were doing it without knowing they were doing it; Xion watched the gap open, ten feet, then fifteen. Ormuth put a hand out sideways, not touching either of them, and they came back into the group without a word and without appearing to notice that they had been recalled.

Then the corridor ended, and it did not end in a door.

It ended in a sphere.

That was the first fact and it took some time to become a fact. Xion stood at the threshold with the others piling gently up behind him and his mind offered him *hall*, and then *vault*, and then gave up, because it was neither of those and there was no third word available. The corridor opened onto the inside of a ball. The floor he was standing on ran forward for thirty feet and then curved, and kept curving, and went on curving until it was a wall, and then a ceiling, and then came back around over his head and down the other side to meet itself behind him.

The far side of it was far enough that he was not certain he was seeing all of it.

There was light and he could not find where it came from. It was not the panel light of the Crown, which sat in fixtures a man could point at. It was simply present in the volume of the room, the way water is present in a bath, and it fell on nothing and cast almost nothing.

And every surface of it — every foot of that curving inner face, arranged in nested arms and shelved galleries and long shallow arcs that followed the curve like the veins on a leaf — every surface of it was covered in brass.

Not brass panelling. Objects. Rows and banks and blocks of small brass objects, set into the galleries in their thousands, and the light went into them and came back changed. It caught the way light catches on the surface of still water when the water is deep; it did not glare, it *lay* on them, and underneath it there was a second thing happening, some slower refraction going on inside the material that the eye kept trying to resolve and could not.

The room hummed. That was the last thing he noticed, and only because someone behind him stopped breathing hard enough to make the silence audible. The whole of Hauren had a low mechanical purpose running under it, everywhere, at the edge of hearing. Here it was not at the edge of hearing. Here it was in the floor.

Xion stood in the mouth of the corridor and understood something that he would spend the rest of the day failing to say to anybody.

The city had been giving them size. For three days it had given them size and beauty and cleverness, boulevards and terraces and water that ran hot from a place they could not see, and all of it had been *outward* — a thing you stood in front of and craned at. This room was not doing that. This room went the other way.

It was not showing them how much the Haureni could build.

It was showing them how much they had **known**.

---

They were not books.

He had known that before they got there, in the abstract; Ormuth had been saying so for a week in the tone of a man protecting himself against disappointment. Knowing it did not help. The mind reached for *book* and there was nothing else on the shelf to reach for.

They were objects that sat in the palm. Xion did not hold one that day, but he stood close enough to a gallery to have the size and the wrongness of them: faceted, in patterns that varied — three within arm's reach that were plainly not the same as one another, and no saying in what the difference consisted. Something close to brass and not brass. Heavier than they had a right to be, which he could see in the shelving built to carry them.

And the facets did something with the light. Take a step and the surface of one would throw out a thin bright line, and if you moved your head slowly the line would hold, and hold, and then arrive at an angle where it stopped being a line and briefly became a **structure** — a lattice, hanging in the air a hand's breadth off the object, complicated and coherent and gone the instant your head kept moving.

Every man in the room found that within the first ten minutes. Xion watched twelve of the best-read men in the world discover it one after another and each of them made the same small movement, a slow tilt of the head, like a bird.

Nobody touched anything.

That was not an order. Nobody had given an order; nobody had needed to. They spread into the lower galleries and began documenting from a distance of two feet, hands clasped behind their backs, voices down at the level people use in a sickroom, and the reason they kept their hands behind their backs was that hands are stupid and go out on their own.

The reverence was not manners. The room made it. Xion had been in three temples in his life and had felt nothing in any of them, and stood in this one with his own hands behind his back and did not remember deciding to put them there.

Somewhere in the first hour one of the contingent said, to nobody, in the voice of a man doing a sum he has already checked:

"The Great Library is a room."

He was not making a speech. He was looking up the curve with his head back, and he said it and went on working, and none of his colleagues answered him, because there was no answer available and every one of them had been getting to the same place by his own road.

Xion had walked through their Great Library seven weeks ago. Acres of it, halls joined by covered arcades so that a scholar need never step into weather, and stairs going down at intervals to a depth nobody had wanted to specify, and in the middle of it a building holding a copy of every book ever written in the Balishan Empire. He had thought it the largest thing of its kind in the world, because it was, and because the man who told him so had said it the way you mention the weather.

It would have gone into one arc of this room.

---

The other thing about the Repository took him longer, and he did not have a word for it until Brennek gave him one hours later, in the middle of a conversation about something else.

It was clean.

Not *preserved.* He had been ready for preserved, and ready to be generous about it, because the Crown had trained him to be — you walked into a chamber up there and told yourself it was intact, and some part of you went on doing quiet arithmetic about dust and drafts the whole time. There was none of that here. The galleries were bare between the objects. The forms sat at even intervals along their runs, and where a gap occurred it occurred deliberately, in a way the scholars would spend the next two years arguing about.

The machines came through. He watched one go along a gallery at head height on some arrangement of feet that gripped the curve, and it did not touch a single form, and it left.

But a machine does not decide.

Somebody had decided. Somebody had stood in this sphere and worked out which object went beside which other object, and had built the galleries to hold them in that order — and the order was not alphabetical and not chronological and not any other thing the men on the floor had a word for, but it was an *order*, because by the end of the first hour they were finding adjacencies and calling them across the room in voices that kept rising and being pulled back down. Four forms in a row about the same thing. A fifth, three arms away in a different arc, about it as well, and no telling yet why it had been put over there instead of here.

Whatever the reason was, it was a **reason**.

The room had not been abandoned.

It had been *arranged*.

---

Elara did not come in.

The rest of them had gone through the threshold in ones and twos and begun to spread, and Xion, turning at some point to check, found her still standing where the corridor opened, exactly where she had stopped, with the whole sphere in front of her.

He came back and stood a little way off and did not say anything, which by now was a practised skill.

He had watched her at the Door for three weeks. He had watched her at the rail three times in two days. He knew the outside of this and knew he had never once known the inside, and what he could see now was that whatever had been pulling at her up on the Crown had been pulling at her the way a current pulls, from all sides and vaguely.

This was not vague. This had a direction.

She went in eventually. He was watching for it and still could not have said what tipped it, and the thing he noticed — the thing he would still be turning over that night, in a corridor, with the day sitting on him — was that she did not go in like a person going into a room.

He had walked into ten thousand rooms behind ten thousand doors, and he knew what the body does. The head comes up. The eyes go around the edges first, because the edges are where the trouble is.

She did none of it. She stepped over the threshold and started walking with her eyes at the middle distance, and her shoulders came down, and Xion, watching, thought: *she has been here.*

She had not been here. She had been an infant twenty years ago, and every year since in a compound on the surface, and there was no arrangement of facts on the continent by which Elara Valanar had ever stood in this room.

The thought would not go away for being false.

---

She worked the sphere wrong, and it took the scholars most of the morning to notice, because they were working it right.

They had started at the corridor and were going out from it, section by section, in a spiral, the way you do. It was correct. It would take them four years. Nobody minded; two of them said as much out loud, cheerfully, in the tone of men who have just been handed the rest of their working lives and find that they approve of the arrangement.

Elara walked past three galleries that had four scholars in them and did not slow down.

She stopped at a fourth, in an arc the spiral would not have reached before the autumn, and stood in front of a cluster of forms with her hands at her sides for long enough that Xion, drifting over, had time to get bored and stop being bored.

Then she said, to nobody, in the voice of someone reading a street sign:

"These belong with the ones at the back."

And put two fingers up, without turning her head, at a point somewhere behind and above her on the far curve of the sphere, where the light was coming off a gallery at a different angle from everything around it.

Xion looked at the scholars.

The scholars looked at each other.

Then one of them — a heavy-shouldered man in his fifties whose name Xion had heard four times and could not hold — put down what he was carrying and went off around the curve without a word, and was gone rather a long time.

He came back with his face arranged.

That was what Xion noticed. Not the answer; the man never actually gave the answer, not in his hearing, he went straight to Ormuth and said something short. What Xion saw was a scholar in his fifties walking back across a room with his features under deliberate management, the way a physician's are when he comes out of the inner room and the family is in the outer one.

He had confirmed something. And he had wanted, badly, another year or two before he had to.

---

The watching started that afternoon, and it did not start rudely.

Xion had been aware of it on the Crown — the contingent's attention going to Elara whenever she stopped walking, which he had put down to the ordinary gravity of an empress in a room. It was not that. He had it clearly by the middle of the afternoon, and what he had was two men with notebooks who were not writing about the Repository.

They were writing about her.

He watched one of them for a full minute. Elara moved; the man's eyes went with her; his hand went to the page. She stopped at a gallery. He wrote. She stood for some time and then moved on without doing anything at all, and he wrote that too, and Xion understood that the not-doing had been recorded as data.

They were not concealing it. There was no furtiveness in it whatsoever — one of them, catching Xion's eye across the floor, gave him a small courteous nod, the nod of a man who assumes he is doing something unobjectionable and is glad to be seen doing it.

He probably was. That was the trouble. They had crossed a continent to study something that had never happened before, and it was in the room with them, and it was going around pointing at things. It would have taken a positive act of will not to write that down.

Xion did not say anything. He had a rule about it, or had had one once: that you do not make a thing into a confrontation on the day you notice it, because the day you notice it is the day you understand it worst. So he stood at the edge of a gallery under a mountain and watched twelve men take notes on the Empress of Balisha, and let it be for now, and was aware while he was doing it that *for now* is a loan and the interest on it is not fixed.

Elara noticed none of it. He was sure of that. She was three arms away with her head tilted, having stopped again in front of a cluster nobody had catalogued, and whatever was happening in her had no attention left over for anything as small as being observed.

---

Brennek had not been in the galleries at all.

Xion found him at the perimeter of the sphere, in the sense that a sphere has a perimeter — down where the floor's curve began to lift, following the join of one arc into the next with his fingertips at the height of his ear, doing the thing he had done to the Crown for two days and to the Brass Door for three weeks.

"It's not one kind," he said, without preamble, when Xion came up.

"The forms?"

"Four kinds. Maybe five." He took his hand down. "Weight's different. You can see it in the shelf; they built the arms heavier where the heavy ones go, and they didn't do that for the look of it, because you can't see it unless you're on your knees." He nodded at a bank three arms along. "That lot's dense. And the surface work on them isn't like the surface work on the pretty ones by the door. The pretty ones vary all over. Those repeat."

"Repeat how?"

"Regular. Like an index." He said it without any weight at all. "If I wanted to find one particular thing out of forty thousand things, that's what I'd do to them. I'd put a mark on the outside so a man could find it without opening it."

Xion looked at the bank of forms.

"Engineering," Brennek said.

"You're guessing."

"I'm guessing," he agreed, comfortably. "I've been guessing since the eleventh hour. Come and look at what I did with the guess."

---

What he had done with the guess was work out how they opened, and he had done it, as far as Xion could establish afterward, by being extremely patient at the least valuable shelf in the room.

"Corner arc," Brennek said. "Nothing in it that isn't in six other places. If I broke one it'd be a shame and not a disaster." He put his hand flat over a form without touching it. "It's not pressure. I did pressure for an hour. It's contact and it's *time*. You have to leave your hand there long enough to be boring."

He put two fingers on the form and left them there.

Xion counted four seconds, and then the air above the shelf came apart.

There was no sound. Light came up out of the facets in a dozen fine lines, and the lines met, and where they met there was a diagram standing in the air at about the height of a man's chest, turning slowly, perhaps three feet across.

It was a water system.

Xion knew that inside a second and a half, and he knew it because he had spent eleven days of his life in a cellar under the Middle District with a Guild engineer who drew like that — intake, settlement, filtration, distribution, drawn as a schematic rather than as a picture, with the flow marked. The difference was that the Guild engineer's drawing had covered a single district and had been wrong, and this one covered a city, and had four separate redundancies drawn into the settlement stage alone, each one hatched in a way that plainly said *if the one before it fails*.

There was heat in it. He could see the exchange stage, and could not read it, and could see that it was there and that it was drawing from below.

Brennek breathed out through his teeth.

"That's the whole thing," he said. "Source to tap. That's — " He stopped. He looked at it for a while. "In Kaha'an we don't have the whole thing. We've got eleven men who each have a piece and they don't like each other."

He took his fingers away and the diagram folded up and was gone.

The next form was agriculture: enclosed growing, soil renewal, mineral cycles, the whole thing scaled and cross-referenced, with a section Brennek stared at for ninety seconds and then said, in a slightly odd voice, was about bringing dead ground back.

The next was energy distribution.

The next was the management of air and temperature in a sealed volume.

They were in a row. That was what Xion could not get past, standing at a shelf in the corner arc of the room while Brennek opened them one after another with two fingers and four seconds. They were in a row on one shelf, in order, water and food and power and air, and the order was not an accident, and they were on the *cheap* shelf — the duplicate arc, the one Brennek had chosen precisely because losing something out of it would not have mattered.

Somebody had made forty thousand of these and had thought it worth keeping a spare set of the important ones by the door.

---

Xion did not say anything for some time, and Brennek, who had worked beside him for ten days, opened the next one and did not fill the silence.

The thing standing in front of Xion was a water system.

He kept arriving back at that and it kept refusing to become smaller. It was a water system that removed what was in the water, at the scale of a city, using heat that came out of the ground for nothing, drawn at a level of detail that assumed the reader would be building it.

He had watched a child die of bad water in the sixth summer of his practice. He could still produce the room: a ground-floor back room off the tannery road with a curtain instead of a door, and a father who had done everything right and had done all of it a day and a half late because the first day and a half you tell yourself it is nothing. Xion had been twenty. He had known what it was within a minute of walking in and had known there was nothing in his bag for it, and he had stayed anyway, because staying was what he had.

He had done that fourteen more times over the following three years. He knew the number because he had written every one of them down, in cipher, in a book that had burned.

And the whole of it — every one of those rooms, the curtain, the father, the specific smell of the tannery road in a hot week — the whole of it had had an answer. The answer had existed the entire time. It had been under his feet, on a shelf, indexed on the outside so a man could find it without opening it, in a room that somebody was **keeping** clean.

Nobody had been keeping it from him. That was the part his chest could not get around. There was no villain in it, and nobody to be angry at. There was a shelf, and a spare set kept by the door, and a civilization that had gone somewhere without leaving a note, and everything between that room and the tannery road was rock and twenty years and no malice at all.

He became aware that he had put his hand on the edge of the gallery.

"Counselor," Brennek said. Not looking at him.

"I'm all right."

"I didn't ask."

---

They were most of the way back along the corner arc when Brennek stopped and did not start again.

Xion had learned to wait through this. The man had done it on the terrace, and at the air grille, and twice at the Door, and what it meant was that something had been sitting in him for a while and had just been given a name.

"The marks," Brennek said.

"The index marks."

"Come here."

He crouched at the end of a run and put a thumb on the edge of the shelf, on a plate set flush into the metal beside the last form in the row — a small worked panel, cut with the same repeating figures Brennek had been copying out for an hour.

"That's one hand," he said. "Or one tool, I can't tell you which and it doesn't matter. Everything in this room from the door round to here is that. Same depth, same spacing, same everything, forty thousand of them, and I'd have said it was done in one go by one shop and I still would."

He moved his thumb four inches along, to the plate at the end of the next run.

"That isn't."

Xion looked. He could see nothing whatsoever. He said so.

"You wouldn't. It's not worse and it's not older, and don't ask me about older, I've told you what I think of brass." Brennek sat back on his heels. "It's *narrower*. Whoever cut that was working to the same pattern with something that took a finer edge, and he got it very close, and if you laid the two side by side on a bench you'd want a lens." He nodded down the arc. "There's about sixty of them. They're all in this quarter. They're all engineering. And they're all at the end of the runs."

"At the end."

"Where you put a thing you've just added." He said it flatly, the way he said everything. "Somebody came in here after the room was finished and put more in. Not a lot. Sixty, seventy. All in one subject."

Xion crouched down beside him and looked at four inches of brass and did the thing he had been doing since the terrace, which was to keep his voice ordinary.

"Can you date it?"

"No."

"Can you—"

"No." Brennek stood up and brushed his palms off against each other. "There's no clock in this room. There's no soil in here and nothing grows and nothing rots and I can't count a single thing. I can tell you there are two hands in this room and one of them came later. That's the whole of it and I'm not going past it."

"That isn't nothing."

"No," Brennek agreed. "It isn't nothing."

He looked down the arc for a while, at sixty index plates cut by somebody with a finer tool, at the ends of the runs, in the engineering quarter.

"There's one more thing and then I'd like to stop for the day," he said. "The last run down there isn't finished. Six plates cut and four blanks after them, and the forms are already shelved behind the blanks, so they weren't waiting on the forms."

Xion did not say anything.

"He put them up," Brennek said. "And he was marking them so somebody could find them. And he got six done."

The pulse came up through the floor of the Repository, patient, three seconds and three seconds, and neither of them said the number out loud, because it was not a new number and there was nothing to be done with it that had not already been done on a farm terrace six days ago and lost.

It was the same answer as the soil. That was all. It was the same answer, in a second place, arrived at from a completely different direction by a man who had refused to be led there.

"Right," said Brennek. "That's me."

---

He had spent the week putting things somewhere.

It had become a joke with himself, almost — that there was a place he had for the ones he could not do anything about, and that it had been filling steadily since the Door turned. The number that would not move. Ormuth in the corridor saying he would trade all twenty-seven. The courier going up with the pouch under his arm. He had put every one of them in the same place, and the place had held, because everything in it had one thing in common, which was that there was nothing to be *done*.

This would not go in.

He stood at the corner arc with his hand on the gallery and tried to put it where he put things and it would not go, and he worked out why standing there, which was that it was not a thing that had happened to him.

It was a thing he could do.

Water for Kaha'an. Not a proposal for water for Kaha'an; not a scheme, not a five-year argument in a council chamber with the Water cartel's men counting on their fingers. The **design**, complete, with the failures already thought about by somebody who had built it and watched it run.

It would not go in the pile because the pile was for things he could only carry, and this was not a thing to carry. It was a thing to go and do. And a man who knows the answer and does not go and do it has stopped being a man who could not help.

---

Past the engineering arcs, deeper around the curve, the scholars had found something that was breaking them.

Xion went through their working area twice that afternoon and it was the same both times and it was not the same men. They had gone into shifts without anyone proposing shifts. Ormuth was there for both passes; Xion was fairly sure Ormuth had been there for everything in between.

The forms in that region were patterned unlike any of the others, and what was in them was not engineering and not history and not, as far as anyone could establish, an account of anything at all.

"Mathematics," Ormuth said, when Xion asked. He said it the way you name a disease you have only read about. "I think. I have a colleague at the towers who would know, and he is a very long way from here, and I would give a finger to have him for an hour."

"You can't read it."

"I can see that it is beautiful." He rubbed his face. "That is the whole of my report, Counselor, and I have been at it for six hours. It has a shape. I can see the shape the way you can see that a man is speaking a language and not making noises. And then I go into it and there is nothing to hold, because the thing it is built on top of is something they had and we do not, and it is not a *fact* they had, it is — "

He stopped and started over.

"We are not missing an answer. I could work with a missing answer. We are missing the question that came before this one. There is a floor under this and we have never stood on it."

Behind him two of his people were arguing at a volume that would have been rude anywhere else. They had drawn something on a sheet of paper on the ground and were both pointing at it, and neither of them was winning, and a third man was standing over the pair with his arms folded and an expression of naked longing.

"They will not stop," Ormuth said, watching them. "I have told them to eat twice."

"Your woman with the food," he added, after a moment. "Down from the camp. She stood in front of the youngest of them until he took the bowl out of her hands, and she did not say one word, and he ate all of it. I could not have made him do that."

"No," Xion said. "Nobody can."

---

The one that stayed with him happened in the corridor.

He was going back up toward the conveyor in the late hours, and Ormuth was sitting on the floor of the approach corridor with his back against the wall about thirty feet outside the sphere, in the stretch where the room's light gave out and the ordinary light took over, and he had been crying.

He made an attempt at not having been. He got about halfway through it and then gave up on it, which Xion respected more than the attempt.

"Sit down or go past," he said. "Do not hover, I can't bear it."

Xion sat down.

They sat there for a while with the hum coming out of the doorway thirty feet away.

"Thirty-one years," Ormuth said. "I have been doing this thirty-one years." He had his hands loose over his knees. "I have never been this stupid in my life. I want you to understand that I am not being modest. I am a good scholar. I am — in three narrow places I am the best there is, and I have known that quietly for about a decade and it has been a comfort to me on bad nights."

"And?"

"And I have spent my whole life making chairs," he said. "Very good chairs. And this afternoon somebody showed me a table."

He said it without any bitterness at all, which was the thing that made it land, and Xion recognised the absence of bitterness because he had heard it once before, from this same man, in a corridor on the Crown, and had not understood then that it was going to be a habit.

"Thank you," Ormuth said.

"For what?"

Ormuth waved a hand vaguely, at the corridor, at the door, at the whole arrangement — the treaty, the descent, the weeks of horse-trading in a room a long way north of here that had ended with twelve scholars standing in front of this — and did not answer, and got up, and went back in.

Xion watched him go through the doorway into that light.

*It is happening to honest people*, he thought. *And it is happening fast.*

---

He found Elara at the back of the sphere in the last hours, in a region that was not catalogued and would not be catalogued for a year.

She was standing in front of a gallery by herself with one hand laid flat against the side of a form. Not on top of it. Against the side, the way you put your hand on the flank of a horse to let it know where you are.

He stopped where he was.

She did not open it. He was fairly sure she could have; he was not sure she knew that. She stood with her hand on it and her head slightly down, and the light off the arc put an edge on the side of her face, and she stayed like that long enough that Xion's legs began to complain.

"They knew," she said.

He came closer. "Knew what?"

She did not answer straight away. He watched her try to get at it and watched it not be available, which he had seen enough times now to recognise from the shoulders alone.

Then she took her hand off the form and made a small gesture with it — at the gallery, at the arc above it, at the whole enormous curving inside of the room — and there was nothing precise in the gesture at all, and he understood her perfectly.

The order. The index marks on the outside so a man could find a thing without opening it. The spare set of the important ones kept by the door. The way the shelf ran water, then food, then power, then air, in that order, which was the order you would put them in for somebody who had none of them and had to start at the beginning.

Forty thousand objects, arranged by somebody, for a reader who was going to know nothing.

Not stored. *Set out.*

"For whoever came," Xion said.

"Yes," Elara said.

Behind them Brennek had come up and stopped, because Elara had been speaking; he had that much sense and had always had it. He stood with his hands at his sides and looked up the curve of the sphere with the whole thing rearranged in his head, and after a while he said, slowly, in the flat Kaha'ani voice he used for measurements:

"That's what it is."

Nobody answered him. He did not need answering.

"You wouldn't index it for yourself," he said. "You know where your own things are."

---

Late, in a side corridor off the approach where the air was ordinary and the sound had somewhere to go, Xion sat on the floor with his back against the wall in the exact place Ormuth had sat six hours earlier, which he was aware of and did not move for.

He thought about the second dispatch for as long as it took to arrive at the bottom of it, which was not long. The first one was gone and moving north and had stopped being his some days ago. There would be another, and Ormuth had started composing it in his head before he got off this floor, and there was no clause in the access terms that would have let Xion stop it and no argument he would have wanted to make — because the alternative to an honest account of this room was a careful one, and a careful one would frighten people considerably more.

He noted it and put it down. A decision made at the end of a day like this one is a decision made by somebody else.

What he had instead was the arithmetic, and it would not stop running.

They had known they were ending. He had understood that days ago in a front room on the eastern arc with his hand on a curtain, and had not been able to do anything with it except carry it. Now there was a second half to it. They had known they were ending, and they had been given some quantity of time — weeks, months, he had no way to price it — and they had spent a part of that time in this room.

Not hiding what they knew. Not sealing it, not warding it, not taking it with them.

**Sorting** it. Putting the water next to the food. Marking the outside of the important ones so that a stranger who could not read a word of the language could still find the thing he needed.

He tried to think about what that took. Not the labour, though the labour was enormous. The other thing. Sitting in a room with the end coming and deciding that the best available use of the hours was to make your mathematics legible to people you would never meet, who would not thank you, whose faces you could not imagine, and who would arrive — you had to assume this, you had to *build* on this — knowing nothing.

They had made the ignorance navigable.

They had made it kind.

He sat with his head back against the wall and found that the awe had gone out of it somewhere in the last hour, and what had come in underneath was not comfortable, and was not going to leave, and was going to want paying.

Elara came around the corner and sat down beside him without asking and without saying anything, close enough that her shoulder was against his, which she had done perhaps four times in a year.

They sat.

The hum came up the corridor from the sphere, patient, unchanged, doing what it had been doing since before either of them had a name.

"They were not strangers, Xion," she said. Very low. "Not exactly."

He waited.

She did not say anything else.

---

The Repository stayed lit.

It had no other setting; it had been lit when they found it and it would be lit when they left, and it would go on being lit afterward for as long as whatever was underneath the city kept doing what it did. The forms sat in their galleries throwing back the light they had been throwing back since long before anybody up above had a word for anything, in the order somebody had put them in, at the very end.

Inside, three of the scholars were still arguing, and would be at the fourth hour, and one of them had run out of paper and was writing on the inside of his forearm.

Brennek was at the corner arc with a lamp he did not need, going along the spare shelf and copying the index marks into a notebook, one at a time, in a hand that would still be legible in fifty years, in the manner of a man who has been handed work that will outlast him and intends to be worthy of it.

And Elara stood at the threshold with the whole sphere in front of her and her shoulders set in a way Xion had not seen on her before — not the set she went into a room with, and not the set she had come off the conveyor with either.

He looked at her for a while from further up the corridor and could not name it, and then found that he could, and wished he could not.

She was standing the way a person stands in a house that has been left to them.

They were not exploring any more.
