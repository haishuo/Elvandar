The change in Elara was four days old before Xion found a way to put it to himself, and the way he found was a negative, which irritated him, because a negative is what you write down when you cannot make a diagnosis.

She had stopped following the scholars.

That was the whole of it. For the first fortnight she had done what everybody did, which was to go where the work was — stand at the shoulder of whichever argument had got loudest, look at what she was shown, say the two or three things she could say and let the rest go. She had been one of thirty people inside a problem.

Now the work went where she went.

It had happened without a decision, the way the other thing had happened in the Crown, and Xion had watched the whole transfer and could not point to the day. She left the camp earlier than the scholars. She came back later. She went down — always down, never out along a level, always to the descents — and she did it without telling anyone, and by the third morning there were two men from Ormuth's contingent waiting at the head of the service stair with their satchels already on, not asking to come, simply standing where she would have to walk past them.

She let them. She did not slow down for them.

Brennek had noticed and had said nothing, which from Brennek was a statement. Ormuth had noticed and had gone quiet in a way Xion did not like at all, because the man's whole method was to talk until the shape of a thing came out, and he had stopped talking.

Xion had noticed on the first day and had done nothing about it for three, which he was not proud of.

What he did on the fourth was follow her.

Not close. Fifty paces, sixty, far enough back that she could pretend not to have seen him and he could pretend not to be there. It was a coward's distance and he knew it. But whatever was happening to her was happening on a register he had no instrument for — no pulse to take, no colour to read, nothing in eight years of practice that had prepared him for a woman walking steadily downward through a dead city toward something she could not name — and the only thing he could offer was the refusal to let her do it alone.

So he walked sixty paces behind her, down four levels of a city built by people who were not coming back, and she never once looked around, and he was fairly sure she knew exactly where he was the whole time.

---

She found it on the morning of the last full day, though nobody knew it was the last full day until much later.

Xion was at the camp when she left. He came out of the mess tent with a cup in his hand and saw her already at the far end of the boulevard, moving at the pace she had been moving at all week, and he put the cup down on a crate that belonged to somebody else and went after her.

Brennek fell in beside him at the second junction without being asked.

"Where," Brennek said.

"Down."

"Right," Brennek said, and did not ask anything else, which was one of the four or five best things about him.

She took a service descent on the western arc that the scholars had catalogued in the first week and shelved — Ormuth had a cross-reference for it off a Crown inscription, one line, no elaboration, a road that went somewhere the road did not say. It had gone on a list. There were nine hundred things on that list.

She went down it without notes.

Xion had seen her navigate a passage once before, in the dark, under the Warrens, with three of them following her lamp and no idea where she was taking them. He had told himself since that it had been the fever and the fear and the dark, and that he had made it larger in his memory than it was, the way you do.

He had not made it larger. If anything he had shaved it down to a size he could carry.

By the time they reached the bottom of the descent she was already a hundred metres into the corridor, walking at the unhurried pace of a woman going somewhere she had been told to go a long time ago and had only now got round to.

It was Brennek who stopped.

Xion went another three steps before he registered it, turned, and found the surveyor standing with his lamp up against the wall — which was itself strange, because there was no need for a lamp anywhere in Lathion and Brennek carried one anyway, out of what he had once described as professional distrust.

"Counselor."

"What."

"Look at the fitting."

Xion looked at the fitting. It was a brass housing where two wall panels met, about the size of a fist, and it was doing the job a brass housing does.

"I don't know what I'm looking at."

"That's the point," Brennek said. "There's nothing to look at. That's a plain lap joint with a cover plate over it. Up in the Crown that same joint has got about forty minutes of somebody's life in it. I've been drawing them for three weeks." He moved the lamp along the seam. "This one took four."

Xion put his hand flat on the wall. It was the same brass. It was the same warmth, the same faint good grain under the fingers that everything in the city had.

"Is it worse?"

"No." Brennek said it immediately, and then took his time over the rest. "That's what I've been standing here about. It isn't worse. It's *right*. It'll hold as long as anything up there will hold. It just hasn't been —" He stopped and searched and came out with it plainly, the way he came out with everything. "Nobody loved it."

Xion looked back down the corridor. Elara had not stopped and had not slowed. The scholars were coming down the descent behind them now, three, then five, the sound of them arriving before they did.

He looked at the wall again, and this time he saw it, because he had been given the trick and could not now unsee it: the brass unburnished in stretches where nobody had come back to polish it out. The proportions plain. A run of ceiling supports at even intervals with the covers left off two of them, not damaged, not fallen — never fitted.

Every other passage in this city had been finished by people with all the time in the world.

"How long," Xion said.

"To build this? At the scale I'm looking at, and the fitting standard, and no ornament anywhere —" Brennek did the thing he did, which was to go somewhere behind his own eyes and come back with a number he would defend. "Somebody made this in months."

Behind them Ormuth arrived at the foot of the descent, out of breath, and heard the last four words, and said nothing at all.

---

The corridor ran down for a long way and then opened.

Xion had walked into the Repository nine days earlier and had thought, at the time, that nothing else in his life was going to do that to him. He had been wrong in a direction he had not anticipated. The Repository had knocked the breath out of him with its order. This did the opposite.

The chamber was enormous — larger than the Repository, half again as large, a volume that took the eye a moment to accept — and it was **unfinished**.

Not ruined. Not collapsed. Nothing in Lathion had ever collapsed. It was unfinished the way a room in a good house is unfinished when the money has stopped: the floor laid perfectly for two-thirds of its run and then simply ending in a level bed of grey composite that nobody had ever come back to face. Stretches of wall left bare where the panelling ran out. The great ribs of the ceiling supports carrying their load exactly as they had been calculated to carry it, and carrying it naked, without a single one of the brass sheaths that covered every structural member in the city above.

Somebody had built this to the very edge of what it needed and had stopped there, and had not stopped there because they ran out of care.

The scholars came in behind him and stopped at the threshold in a rough line, the way men stop at the edge of water.

Ormuth made a small sound. It was not a word. Xion had spent enough of his life in rooms where bad news arrived to recognise it as the noise a man makes when the thing in front of him is not on the list of things he had prepared himself for on the way over.

At the centre of the chamber stood the Engine.

It was the size of a house. That was the first fact and it took a while to get past. It sat on a raised base of the same grey composite, and it went up through the middle of the room in a mass of brass and dark metal that resolved, the longer you looked, into an enormous number of separate parts fitted together with the precision they had all stopped remarking on two weeks ago.

And it was ugly.

That was the second fact, and it was the one that mattered, and Xion understood — standing there with his hands at his sides — that he was going to remember the order in which those two facts had arrived for the rest of his life.

Everything else in Lathion had been *made*. A stair rail had a curve in it that no stair rail needed. The panels on a frontage in the second boulevard turned the light in a way that had cost some man a season of his working life and had improved nothing whatsoever except the light. He had walked past a drinking fountain in the Crown with a lip on it worked so that the water would break correctly, for nobody, forever.

This thing had none of that. Not a line of it. Every surface was the surface required, every joint the joint that would hold, and where two assemblies met at an angle that would have embarrassed the men who built the boulevards, they met at that angle anyway and were bolted through.

The precision was Lathionese. The precision was terrifying. The beauty had simply not been in the budget.

Brennek came up beside him and stood and looked at it and did not say anything for a very long time. Xion let him. He had watched this man describe a water main with something close to reverence; he had heard him call a corridor's ventilation the deepest piece of thinking he had ever been allowed to look at.

"Well," Brennek said at last, and stopped.

"Go on."

"That was made by people who only got one go at it."

And under all of it, from inside the mass, came the pulse.

It was louder here. It had been getting louder for eleven days and it was loudest here and this was, obviously, where it had been coming from the entire time — but the pitch had not changed by a hair, and the interval had not changed, and that was the part that got into Xion and stayed. Three seconds. Three seconds. The same count he had first heard through a brass door with his hand on Elara's shoulder, the same count that had come up through the floor of the Repository while he stood in front of a water system and could not breathe.

Not faster down here. Not deeper. Exactly, precisely, indifferently the same.

He had spent his adult life listening to hearts. He knew what it meant when a rhythm did not change no matter what you did to the body around it, and he did not like it, and he could not have said why.

---

The scholars got to work, because they could not do anything else.

The walls carried inscription. So did the Engine's casing — bands of it around the base, more of it up the flanks where a man could reach and none above that. Within four minutes of arriving, six Urathans were spread along the north wall with their notebooks out, and the chamber had filled with the low irregular murmur Xion had learned to find comforting, and one of the juniors was on his knees taking a rubbing with the side of a pencil.

He drifted along behind them and listened.

"It's the same language," one of them said, to nobody. "It is not the same *hand*."

That much even Xion could see. The Lathionese script in the Repository had been cut into brass plates the way a good clerk writes: even, unhurried, each mark the same depth as the mark before it, over acres of surface, in a room where forty thousand objects had been indexed on their outsides so that a stranger could find one without opening it.

These cuts were deep. They were uneven. In places the stroke had run past its stop.

Somebody had done this fast, and had done it by hand, and had cared enormously about getting it down and not at all about how it looked when it was down.

Ormuth worked the east wall for half an hour and then came away from it and stood in the middle of the floor with his notebook against his chest.

"It is not technical," he said.

"What is it?"

"I don't know yet." He looked at the wall, then at the Engine, then back at the wall. "It is not instructions. I had assumed instructions. There is nothing here that tells you how to do anything." He turned a page back, and forward again, and Xion saw that his hand was not entirely steady. "I have four words I would stake something on. *Seal.* *Threshold.* *Time.*"

"That's three."

Ormuth did not answer for a moment.

"Necessary," he said.

He said it flatly, and out loud, and then he heard himself say it, in a room built in months by people who did not have months, and he did not say anything else for a considerable while.

Xion stood beside him and looked at the wall of deep hurried cuts and thought: *that is not a manual. That is an argument.*

Somebody had stood here with the thing behind them already built, and had written down, at length, at speed, why.

You do not write down why if the people who come after are going to agree with you.

---

By the sixth hour Ormuth had recovered enough to be a scholar again, and was doing it well.

"Nothing is touched," he was saying. "Nothing at all. We survey the chamber, we complete the inscription record, we build a plan of approach to the mechanism and we bring it to the Empress and the Counselor before any part of it is executed. Days, not hours. There is no version of this where we improvise." He had his hands up, marking the stages in the air. "I would rather lose a fortnight than lose the one chance anybody gets to see this correctly. You two have the west arc. Nobody steps onto the base."

He was right. Xion, standing four paces off, thought it through twice and could not find any part of it that was wrong. It was the correct approach, arrived at quickly, by a tired man, under conditions no training had covered.

Elara had walked to the edge of the base.

Xion saw it out of the side of his attention and then all at once with the whole of it. She had come across the floor while Ormuth was talking and she had stopped at the foot of the raised platform with her hands loose at her sides, and she was standing the way she stood at the railing on the Crown, which was to say entirely still, with her weight forward.

He had spent two years learning to read this woman and had got about a third of the way. He knew this one.

He crossed the floor.

He did not run. Running would have made it a thing, and he understood — walking — that whatever was about to happen had stopped being open to discussion some time before any of them came down the corridor, and possibly some considerable time before that.

Ormuth was still talking. *We approach it in stages.* One of the juniors laughed at something.

Elara stepped up onto the base.

"Elara —"

She put her hand flat against the Engine.

Not the outer casing. She had reached past a standing rib into the shadow inside the frame and laid her palm on the inner surface, the way you would put your hand on the flank of a horse to let it know where you are, and Xion's *no, don't* got as far as the front of his mouth and stopped there, because it was already done, and because he had seen her face.

---

Nothing happened.

He wanted that written down somewhere afterward, in the record, in whatever account got made of it: *nothing happened.* The Engine did not light. It did not turn. No part of it moved. There was no sound out of it that had not been coming out of it for eleven days and there was no sound out of it after, either. The pulse went on at three seconds and three seconds, unaltered, incurious, exactly as it had been while Ormuth was assigning the west arc.

Afterward, six men who had been in that chamber gave six accounts and no two of them agreed. One said the light dimmed. Two said the air moved. Ormuth, who was the most careful man in the room and had been looking directly at her, wrote that he had experienced a sensation of falling and could not defend the observation and was recording it only because he had made it. The junior with the pencil had felt nothing at all and was ashamed of it for a year.

Xion, who was closest, saw a woman standing with her hand on a machine.

He was on the base beside her in three steps and did not touch her.

He wanted to. Every part of the training said get her off it, break the contact, put her flat, and he stood there with his hands half up and did not, because the one thing he could see clearly was that she had not been taken by anything. Her hand was flat and steady. Her arm was not rigid. She was not being held there.

She was **listening**.

So he did the only thing that was available to him, which was to work.

Pulse first. He had her wrist before he had decided to take it, two fingers laid in, and it was ninety and even and utterly unremarkable, the pulse of a woman standing in a warm room. He counted it twice because he did not believe it. Breathing shallow, regular, no catch at the top. He got round in front of her: no slack anywhere in the face, no droop, jaw not set, the small muscles at the corners of the eyes doing nothing.

Her eyes were open. The pupils were wide, which meant nothing in this light, and they were not tracking anything in the chamber. Not the Engine, not the wall behind it, not him at eight inches.

He passed his hand across her line of sight. Nothing.

He said her name twice, at conversational volume, and then once loudly enough that two scholars turned round.

Nothing.

And that was it. That was everything he had. He knelt on the base of a machine the size of a house, beside the person he cared about more than any other person alive, with the whole of his instrument out and running, and every single reading came back **well**.

He had built a life on this. He had been fifteen years old with a woman's hand in his hands and had learned, over eight years, in a room behind a weaver's shop, how to look at a body and be told the truth by it. It had never once failed him. It had told him about a child on the tannery road inside a minute of walking through a curtain, when the father still had hope, and it had told him the truth then and he had hated it for it.

Now it said: *she is fine, her heart is good, her breathing is good, she is in no distress of any kind.*

And it could not tell him where she was.

He stayed on his knees on the base and counted her pulse for the third time because it was the only useful thing his hands knew how to do, and understood with complete clarity that for the first time in his adult life he was standing next to somebody and the whole of his art had nothing whatsoever to say about it.

It went on for forty seconds.

He knew that precisely. He had been counting her pulse the entire time, and afterward, when he stopped shaking, he did the arithmetic, and it was a little under forty seconds from the moment her hand went flat to the moment her legs let go.

---

She came down onto her hands and knees on the base.

Not a fall. He caught her under the arm and most of the weight was already hers again by the time he had hold of it, and she stayed there on all fours with her head down and her hair forward and her hand — the hand — still open against the composite.

She was not crying. She was not making any sound.

Behind him the chamber had gone completely silent. Six men had stopped where they were, at various distances, in the postures they had been in, and Ormuth had gone the colour of paper and was standing with his notebook still up against his chest like a man holding a shield he had forgotten he was holding.

"I've got you," Xion said. "I'm here. Take your time."

She breathed for a while.

Then she said, quietly, in the conversational tone of somebody picking up the middle of a long exchange with an old friend:

"I'm sorry it took us so long."

She was not talking to him.

He knew it the way you know which room in a house a voice is coming from. She had her head down and she was speaking to the base of the Engine, or to the chamber, or to something further off than either, and the six men behind him heard every word of it and did not understand a single one.

The Engine did not answer. It went on at three seconds and three seconds.

It did not need to answer. It had been answered forty seconds ago, when she put her hand on it.

She got herself up onto her heels. She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, once, briskly, the way she did everything with her hands, and looked at Xion for the first time since she had come down the corridor.

"The Rending," she said.

The word cost her. He watched it cost her — a small hitch on the front of it, gone before it finished.

And then he watched the whole of it come apart and reassemble in the space of about two heartbeats, and he was aware, distantly, of putting his hand out and taking hold of the frame rib beside him.

Twenty years. The number Brennek had found on the shelving in the Repository and had been too honest not to say out loud. The number that had been sitting in the middle of this expedition since the workshop, when a junior had raised the coincidence and been told, correctly, by everyone in the room including Xion, that a coincidence is what you call two facts you have not yet earned the right to connect.

They had not been two facts.

Lathion had not emptied around the time of the Rending. Lathion had emptied **in** it. The same event. The same week, the same day, in some accounting that nobody up above had ever had the information to make. Four hundred metres under a city that had spent twenty years calling itself a survivor, another city had been dying of the identical wound.

And it had not died by accident.

That was the part his chest could not get round, and he got no warning of it; it arrived whole. Nobody builds a chamber this size in months by accident. Nobody writes an argument on a wall in a hurried hand and cuts the strokes past their stops because a disaster happened to them. They had **known**. They had had time, and they had spent it, and what they had spent it on was this room and the room nine days behind him with forty thousand objects laid out in an order a stranger could learn from.

They had known what was coming, and they had built the thing that stopped it.

The Rending had halted mid-desert. It was the first fact anybody learned about it. It was in every account, every history, every temple record in Kaha'an — the wasting stopped, in the third season, at a line on the ground, for no reason anybody had ever been able to give. It was the closest thing the empire had to a miracle: a catastrophe that simply ceased, and twenty years of scholarship and liturgy built over the top of the hole where the cause should have been.

He was standing in the cause.

He looked up at the ugly enormous thing above him — no ornament, no line spent on anything but function, bolted through where it needed to be bolted through — and he thought about a man he had never met standing in this chamber with four months left, deciding that the cover plates did not matter.

---

Elara stood up.

She did it without help and without hurry, and she turned round, and Xion — still on one knee — saw the room see her.

"This chamber is closed."

Her voice was not loud. It did not have to be.

"No one comes here again. Nothing in this room is studied, moved, drawn, or opened. The record you have taken today is the whole of the record. Ormuth — your people leave with what is in their notebooks and they add nothing to it."

Ormuth's mouth opened.

Xion could see the entire protest arrive in the man, complete, and it was not a bad protest. It was the third clause of the access terms. It was six weeks of negotiation and a treaty signed by two nations and the unrepeatable, unprecedented, once-in-the-history-of-the-world opportunity standing four metres away with a woman's handprint still warm on the inside of it. He had every right to say it, and the right had been written down, and he had come four hundred metres under the ground and given up the only reputation he had ever built in order to be standing here.

He looked at Elara.

Xion watched him look, and watched him find whatever was in her face, and understood that he was watching a man discover the outer wall of something.

Ormuth closed his mouth. He nodded, once, and then again, a small second nod that was for himself.

He would write the protest. He would write it that night, at length, in the careful, wounded, scrupulously courteous prose of a man who has been overruled and intends the record to show it, and he would hand it to Xion at the surface with his hands shaking, and every word of it would be reasonable.

He did not say one syllable of it in that room.

---

The practical work took the rest of the afternoon, and Xion did it, because it was what he could do.

The Lathionese had not built a door across the corridor mouth. They had built the *fitting* for a door, and left the door in its housing, and never used it — one more thing on the list of what four months does not cover. Brennek found the housing in about an hour and spent three more on the mechanism, working the way he worked, which was to touch nothing until he had drawn it, and then to touch exactly one thing.

At the eighth hour a slab of brass came out of the wall of the corridor and rotated across its mouth and seated with a sound Xion felt in his back teeth.

It was smaller than the Brass Door above, and it was plainly kin to it: the same metal, the same unhurried certainty, the same total absence of anything resembling a handle on the outside.

Then he put a watch on it.

Four men on rotation at the head of the descent, reliefs arranged, a standing order in writing, the whole thing set up inside twenty minutes because he had done a great deal of this in the last two years and it had stopped requiring the front of his mind.

"Guard detail," one of the Tor'anar said, writing it down.

"No," Xion said. "Witnesses."

The man looked up.

"There is nothing down there to guard," Xion said. "Nobody is going to steal it and nobody is going to break it. I want four men who can say what happened at that door and what did not, for as long as anybody is asking." He heard himself and did not soften it. "In fifty years somebody is going to want to open that. I want it on a record that starts today."

Ormuth, ten feet away, did not turn round, and did not miss a word of it.

---

They left that night. Nobody had ordered a withdrawal; it simply became what everybody was doing.

They went up through the lower levels and through the Repository, where the news had arrived before them by whatever means news arrives, and the work had stopped. That was the thing Xion carried up out of the city. Not the Engine. The Repository at the ninth hour with nobody in it — the forms sitting in their galleries throwing back the light, the tables abandoned mid-argument, three notebooks left open on the floor of the corner arc where somebody had simply put them down and gone to find out whether what he had heard was true.

Brennek went and got the notebooks and shut them and brought them along.

Up through the Crown. Along the second boulevard, past the frontage whose panels did that thing to the light, past the fountain running clear water for nobody. Onto the ascending platform at the Gatehouse, all of them, in the dark, in a silence Xion did not try to fill.

The city fell away below them slice by slice through the gaps in the shaft wall: levels turning, none of them together, each at its own rate, exactly as they had been turning when he came down and exactly as they would be turning tomorrow.

The pulse came up after them for a long way.

He noticed the moment it stopped being audible and started being something he was supplying himself, and he could not have said afterward where on that ascent it happened.

Elara stood at the rail with her hands open on it and looked down, the way she had every time, and did not speak once in an hour and a half.

Through the threshold space. Through the antechamber where a boy had first put his hand on a brass door and felt the count come through it. Out.

Farleen was standing at the staging camp forty feet from the Door with a lamp in her hand, having been told nothing by anybody, because somebody had come up ahead of them and she had made her own arrangements off the look on his face.

She took one look at Elara.

She did not ask a single question. She turned round and started giving quiet orders to people who had been asleep ten minutes earlier, and by the time the column reached the camp there was hot water on and a route arranged and two of her people already gone ahead up the tunnel to say they were coming.

---

They came up through the Warrens in the small hours.

The Long Knives had the middle passages and knew about it well before the column got there. Xion saw the watchers on the ledges at the second junction and the third and did what you do, which was to keep walking and let them count.

Nobody stopped them. Nobody asked. At the long gallery where the territory changed hands there were four of the Mistress's people standing well back from the line of march with their hands where everyone could see them, and one of them lifted his chin at Xion as he went past, and that was the whole transaction.

Elara had stood in the chamber at the foot of Dead Man's Passage in the spring and told that woman that when the Door was open she would hear what was behind it from her directly, before the court, and before anybody wrote it down. It had bought them the camp. It had bought them every load of timber and every barrel of oil that had gone down that passage since.

The account had come due about nine hours ago.

He thought about sending word up before they reached the surface, and decided it could hold until the morning. Then he looked at the men on the ledges and understood that it could not hold at all — that four of them had just watched an expedition come out of the ground at speed, in the dark, weeks early, with the Empress in the middle of the column and nobody talking — and that the Mistress would have all of that inside the hour and would be doing the arithmetic on it before he had finished climbing.

She would not send anyone. That was the thing about her. She would wait, correctly, for what she had been promised, and the waiting would be the entire message.

He kept walking.

Up through the tunnels. Up through the surface levels with the smell changing at every stage — stone, then smoke, then the particular sourness of the lower city before dawn — and out.

The sky was doing the thing it does about an hour before sunrise, which is nothing much, a thinning at one edge.

Elara stopped when she came out into it. Everyone behind her stopped too, and waited, and she stood in a bad street in the low city with her head back and looked at a sky with almost nothing in it for a count of perhaps thirty.

Then she said, "All right," and walked on.

Farleen had a room by the time they got up to it, in the quiet wing of a merchant house near the wall that had been vacated inside a quarter of an hour by people who understood exactly whose party this was and had elected not to be told. A fire lit. A drink that turned out to be hot and sweet and not particularly medicinal. A blanket, put around Elara's shoulders from behind without a word or an inquiry, in the manner of someone putting a bowl down next to a man who is not going to notice he has eaten it.

Then a closed door, and Farleen on the other side of it, and no one at all getting past her for six hours.

---

For a long time neither of them said anything.

She sat on the end of the bed with the blanket around her and the cup in both hands, and Xion sat in the chair by the fire, and the room ticked and settled the way rooms do.

He wanted to check her over. It sat in his hands the entire time. She had been unconscious on her feet for forty seconds and had walked nine hours since and had eaten nothing, and there were four things he could have established in under a minute.

"Can I have a look at you?"

She surfaced from wherever she was and almost smiled.

"Not yet."

"All right."

He sat back down. That was harder than it should have been and he let it be hard, and did not ask again, and after a while it stopped costing anything, which he thought was probably the point.

She started because the silence got long enough, not because he asked her anything.

"There was a market."

"A market."

"On the second boulevard. Where the frontage is, the one you like, with the panels." She turned the cup round. "It's a market. Three days a week — no, I don't know that, I don't know how I know that. There's a man selling something out of flat baskets and he's got them stacked up his arm like this." She showed him, forearm out, the stack going up to the shoulder, and stopped with her arm in the air, and looked at it.

She put her arm down.

"That's the wrong end of it," she said. "I'm sorry. I don't know how to do this from the front."

"Do it from wherever it is."

So she did.

And somewhere in the first minute of it the account came off its hinges, and she stopped telling him what she had seen, and started telling him what she was seeing, in the present tense, and did not come back out of it for a very long time.

*There are children in the boulevards.*

That is the first thing and it is the thing that will not leave her: that the roads she has been walking for eleven days with nine hundred lamps and thirty exhausted men have children on them. Not ceremonial children, not a procession — a boy going flat out on the inside of a corner, badly, the way boys run, and somebody shouting after him without any real anger in it. The noise of the place. She has been in that city for eleven days and the only sound in it has been the pulse and her own party, and now there is sound on top of sound on top of sound, and the light she has been calling beautiful for a fortnight is just the light, and nobody is looking at it.

There is an evening quality to all of it. That is the part she cannot make land, and she tries three times. It is not a young city. It is a very old one, in a long quiet evening, doing the twenty-fourth century of a thing it has been doing well for a very long time and expects to go on doing.

And they are **watching**.

That is what the long tables are, deeper in, on a level below anything the expedition ever reached. Men and women at instruments, taking readings off the surface — the surface, four hundred metres up, that Xion has spent his whole life on — and writing them down and shelving them. Twenty centuries of it. They stopped going up long before, and closed the Great Shaft and filled it, and they never once stopped looking, and there is a wall of it, in order, going back further than the Balishan Empire goes back.

Then the day.

A place with nothing around it. She does not know where it is and she has never been shown a map with it on. There is one man there and he is alone and he is doing something, and the instruments in Lathion begin to register it — she has no word for what they register — and go on registering it, and the register climbs, and the people at the tables stop writing.

The Gray Gate.

She has the word for it in her mouth without knowing where she got it. It is not a place and it is not a door. It is the join. It is the thing that holds the dead on their side and the living on theirs, and it has been shut since before there was anybody to notice it was shut, and it flexes.

Then it opens.

Then it does not close.

She stops here, in the room, for a while. Xion does not say anything.

The first wave goes out across the surface and she watches it go from underneath, on instruments, as a number, and the number is everything alive between one place and another.

And her parents are in it.

They are riding out that morning with the imperial party, out past the walls, because that is what the record says and she has known it since she was old enough to be told, and she has known it as a sentence — *they were outside the city, and they did not come back, and they were never recovered.* It has been a sentence her entire life, in the same tone as the other sentences about them.

She sees them.

She is not shown their faces. She could not describe them afterward and she never tries. What she is given is a party on a road in the morning, at a distance, doing something entirely ordinary, riding toward a thing that is already coming and that nobody on that road can see or hear or stop.

She knows it is them.

She knows it the way she knows her own face.

Xion said nothing for a long time after that. It was the longest he had ever gone in a room with somebody who was hurting, and every second of it was a decision, because there were four things a counselor says here and he could feel all four of them fully formed and available, and every one of them would have been for him.

He sat still and let it be quiet.

Then, because she was waiting for it and needed to be able to go on:

"Keep going."

The instruments go on measuring after the first wave. That is the part the surface never had. Up above they think it stopped because it stopped; underneath they watch the opening keep widening, and they model it, and they run it again, and they hand it round the table and somebody else runs it, and it keeps coming back the same.

It does not stop at a province. It does not stop at a continent. There is no line on the model where it stops. Everything that is alive.

Then the council, and this is where her voice changes.

They know what it will take. That is the first thing established and the last thing argued. The Engine will need to be made and there is no version of using it that does not spend them — all of them, every one who is left — and they know this before the first support goes up.

And they build it anyway, and it takes months, and she watches the months.

That is the thing she keeps returning to and cannot get past, and she says it four separate times, in four different ways, and by the fourth time she is not really talking to Xion.

They had *months*.

Months of getting up in the morning knowing. Months of the ordinary work — of somebody carrying two lengths of brass down a corridor and setting them where the drawing said, and going home, and coming back the next day and doing it again — with the end of everything they had built five thousand years ago in the diary. They did not do it in a fever. Nobody does anything in a fever for four months. They did it as a **job**.

And in the middle of that, with the chamber half finished, some of them went and did something else.

They went into a room on the level above and took forty thousand objects and put them in an order.

Water first. Then food. Then power. Then air. Then the deeper things, and the theory behind the deeper things, in the order you would use to teach somebody who knew none of it and had to start at the beginning — and they indexed every one of them on the outside so a stranger could find a thing without opening it, and they made a spare set of the important ones and left it on a shelf by the door.

For nobody. For whoever. For a man who might come in five hundred years and might not come at all, who would not thank them, whose face they could not imagine.

They did that with four months left.

*They knew,* she says, in the room, coming half out of it and then going back under, *that's what I couldn't say, that's what I was trying to say to you on the arc.*

Then the last day.

They walk in. That is all. There is no ceremony that she can identify and no coercion of any kind, and it is not a crowd being herded and it is not a rite; it is a great many people coming down a plain corridor into a room and standing in it, the way people stand in a room when they have agreed to be there.

The Engine runs.

And they do not fall. She is absolutely fixed on this and says it twice. Nothing collapses. There is no moment where they are victims of the thing they are standing in. What happens is closer to a giving — the whole of what a person is, going where it was asked to go, in order, for as long as it takes.

She watches them choose it.

She watches them die.

And then, at the very end, the seal: the Gray Gate coming back onto its seat, and out in the third season of a desert eight hundred miles away, a line where the wasting simply stops, and men on the edge of it who will spend the next twenty years thanking the Gods for it and building on the hole where the reason should be.

And the shockwave.

It goes out through the rock in a cone, the way sound goes, and up. Four hundred metres up, at the very outer edge of it, are the foundations of the Ruby Palace — which was put where it was put because that is where you put a palace, at the middle, exactly as they had put the Engine at the middle for exactly the same reason, five thousand years apart and neither of them knowing.

And in a room in that palace, six months old, asleep, is her.

*I'm in the cradle,* she said, "and I'm in the room. Both. At once. I can't —"

That was where it let go of her.

She came back into the room in Kaha'an all at once, with the cup gone cold in her hands and the fire down to almost nothing, and looked at Xion with an expression he had never seen on her and hoped very much never to see again.

"It's not a gift," she said. "That's what I keep — it isn't a gift, Xion, it isn't a blessing, there was no one deciding. It's *residue.* It went through the rock and it went through a floor and it went into the only thing in the building soft enough to take an impression, and that was me, because I was six months old and hadn't finished yet."

"Yes," he said.

"So all of it. The doors. The inscriptions. Every time I've known something I have no business knowing and everyone has looked at me like —" She stopped. "That's them. That's not me and it's not the blood and it's not the Gods and it isn't the crown. That's what's left of forty thousand people going through a floor."

He thought about the shelf. The spare set of the important ones, kept by the door, indexed on the outside, in a room they had organised while the chamber below them was still being built.

He thought about how much of a man's last four months you would have to spend on that.

"They didn't know about you," he said. "You're right. They couldn't have."

"No."

"They knew there'd be somebody."

She looked up.

"That's the whole room, upstairs," Xion said. "Forty thousand objects put in a teaching order for a reader who was going to know nothing. They didn't do that for the world in general, it's not a monument, nobody builds a monument you have to open one at a time. They did it for a *person*. Somebody specific, standing in that room, one day, not knowing anything." He found that his voice had gone somewhere he had not authorised. "They didn't know it would be you. But you're not a stranger to them, and you weren't an accident either. They were reaching."

She did not say anything for a while.

"They did know about you," he said. "In some sense."

Elara nodded, once, and then put her hand over her mouth and held it there, and Xion moved from the chair to the end of the bed and sat down beside her, and she leaned into his shoulder, and he put his arm round her and did not say anything else for a long time.

The messages had been waiting downstairs since before they came up out of the ground.

Farleen had them in a satchel and had not mentioned them once, which meant she had looked at the outsides and made a decision, and Xion loved her for it and would tell her so at some point in the next year if he remembered.

He went down at the sixth hour while Elara slept, and stood in a cold hallway, and went through them without opening any.

It took about four minutes and told him a great deal.

There were too many of them. That was the first thing. There were more of them than there had been in the entire month before the expedition went down, and they had accumulated in eleven days.

The second thing was the seals. He read the outsides of nine and put them back and stood there with his hand flat on the satchel.

Two of those houses did not write to him. One of them had not written to anybody in the Valanar administration in two years, on principle, at some expense to itself. There was a seal in there from a man who had sat across the long table in the south gallery and told him, courteously, in front of everyone, that he considered the throne a temporary arrangement.

He did not open them. He knew what was in them without opening them, in outline, and outline was enough to be going on with: the dispatch had reached Uratha, and Uratha had done something with it, and whatever had been done had come back down the mountain and arrived here, and the world he had walked out of eleven days ago was not the world standing in this hallway.

It was going to be a very hard morning.

He left the satchel where it was and went back up. She was awake when he came in, which he had known she would be, and neither of them said anything, and he sat down on the end of the bed and after a while understood that he was still counting — and then that her hand was moving on the blanket, once and once again, three seconds apart, and had probably been doing it the whole time he had been in the room.
