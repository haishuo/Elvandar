The first session of the advisory council was supposed to have been about the Warrens.

Xion had asked for that in front of witnesses, and the schedule had been fixed before the month turned exactly as he wanted it, and then a door five hundred meters under the city had lit up under the Empress's hand and refused to open, and the schedule had done what schedules do. Nobody argued about it. Nobody needed to. The Warrens would keep — that was the sentence in everyone's head, unspoken, entirely reasonable, and Xion sat down at the long table with the taste of it in his mouth and could not think of a single thing to say against it that was true.

The chair for Grain was still empty. So was the other one.

"We have the surveyor's report," Elara said, "and we have four hours, and I would like to leave this room with a name in it. Somebody who can open that door."

Kael had the report. He went through it economically, which meant he went through it twice — once as the surveyor had written it, and once as the thing it actually said. No seam. No hinge. A field of symbols that lit in a determined order and matched nothing anyone in Kaha'an had a table for. A mechanism that ran a cycle of between nine and ten minutes, completed it correctly, and began again.

"That last part is the whole of it," Kael said. "A thing that repeats is a thing that is waiting. It isn't damaged and it isn't hostile. It's been left switched on for a very long time, and it wants an instruction nobody alive knows how to give it."

"Then the question is who does," Elara said.

And the room began, without any one person deciding to, to go abroad.

It was not stupid. That was what Xion noticed about it afterward, turning it over: at no stage did anybody say anything foolish. Lord Merchant Hadris raised Gunastran, because Gunastran built things and had built the finest things in the world for a century, and their engineers would at least understand what they were looking at even if they could not move it. Somebody else raised Uratha, because if the door was old — and it was plainly old — then what was needed was not a mechanic but a scholar, and Uratha had spent three hundred years assembling the largest collection of scholars in existence and had never once been shy about saying so. A third voice began costing them out — transport, indemnity, what a Gunastran consortium would ask against what a Urathan chair would ask — and got a fair way into it before Kael observed, without any particular emphasis, that they were weighing two prices nobody had ever been quoted, against a treasury that had spent four hours last month failing to find nine months.

That was allowed to stand. It was the kind of true thing that does not help.

It took perhaps six minutes to get from *who can open it* to *which foreigner*, and the step between those two was never taken out loud. It was simply gone, the way a stair is gone in the dark, and everyone had walked over the place where it had been.

Hadris was two full sentences into a comparison of transport costs when Vesk spoke.

"Majesty."

He had been standing where he always stood, at the end of the room by the door, in the posture Xion had stopped seeing months ago, and in six weeks of these sessions he could not remember hearing the man make a sound. It was not his place to have opinions here. He was extremely clear about what his place was.

Elara turned. "Commander."

"Before we send abroad." Vesk said it without any emphasis at all, in the voice he used for reporting the weather and the number of men on a gate. "Has anyone in this room considered that the Empress has mages?"

Nobody said anything.

It was not shock. Xion had been in rooms that went silent from shock and this was a different silence entirely — the specific, embarrassed quiet of a group of intelligent people discovering that they had skipped a step, all at the same moment, and that the step had been quite a large one.

One of the junior advisers got about half a sentence out — "The Tor'anar are hardly—" — and then heard himself, and stopped, and looked down at his papers with the expression of a man rehearsing what the end of that sentence had been going to be and finding he did not much like it.

"They exist," Vesk said, to nobody in particular. "They have existed the entire time. I stood six feet from that door when it went up and I could not have told you whether what I was looking at was dangerous, and that is not a failure on my part; it is what I am and what I am not. When a thing happens that I have no means of assessing, my understanding is that there is supposed to be somebody on the other side of it whose business it is." A pause of exactly the right length. "That is the whole of my contribution, Majesty."

Xion looked at Elara.

He had watched her manage a great many faces in front of a great many rooms, and she was managing this one, and he could see the seam because he always could. She was not embarrassed. It was worse and quieter than that: she had gone somewhere in her own head and found something there she had not previously looked at.

She had not forgotten them. Xion was certain of that, and he was right, and it was not the point. She had simply never once thought of them **first**. She had been raised in a compound full of soldiers by people who loved her and taught her everything they had, and when Elara Valanar's hand went out for an instrument in a crisis, the shape it made was the shape of the Arol Batae, and it made that shape before she was aware of reaching.

She looked at Vesk for a moment longer than the exchange needed.

"Send for them," she said.

---

The sending took a day and a half, and most of that was the palace administration.

Nobody knew the form. That was the difficulty. There existed a correct way for the crown to summon the Tor'anar — a specific instrument, with specific language, issued under a specific seal — and no crown had issued one in twenty years, and the last man who had known how to draw one up had died on the same afternoon as the Emperor. What was left was a registry, and the registry had an index, and the index had an entry, and the entry pointed at a volume that had been moved during the reorganization of the east archive some fourteen years ago and had been, in the strict sense, missing ever since.

It was found. Xion did not ask by whom; he received, on the second morning, a folded note from the same narrow undersecretary with the ink on his hand, stating that the form had been located, that it was shorter than anticipated, and that it required a seal the palace still possessed but had not used in living memory, which had accordingly needed cleaning.

He read that last clause three times and then put the note down and looked out of the window for a while.

The summons went out on the third day.

---

They arrived that same afternoon.

Xion did the arithmetic on it before he had entirely decided to, the way he did arithmetic on everything, and there was almost none to do. The corps kept its establishment in the Imperial Quarter, in among the administrative buildings of the old empire — four streets from the palace, and something under half an hour on foot from the room in which a council had spent an afternoon deciding to look abroad. The summons had left the registry after breakfast.

It had taken the palace three days to work out how to ask. It took them the length of a morning to answer, and most of that was somebody walking.

They had not been scattered and they had not been unready. They had been where they had been for twenty years, which was four streets away, in whatever state of readiness people sit in when they have been waiting that long — and when it finally came they had put their coats on and come.

Xion had walked past that gate a thousand times in his life without once wondering what was behind it. So, evidently, had everybody else in that chamber. Nobody said any of it aloud, then or afterward, and he suspected he was not the only one who had done the sum.

There were three of them, and the first thing that struck him was that he had prepared for the wrong thing entirely.

He had expected something like the Arol Batae, because the Arol Batae were what he knew: hard young men and women with a great deal of carriage, moving as a unit, taking up a precise and deliberate amount of space. What came up the colonnade instead was three people in plain coats over plain working clothes, unarmed as far as he could see, walking at the unhurried pace of professionals who had been sent for and had come, and who were not going to arrive out of breath about it.

And they were **old**.

Not old. He corrected himself even as it landed, because it was not accurate and he was a physician and accuracy was the one thing he owed a body he was looking at. The senior of them was perhaps fifty-five and the youngest of the three could not have been under forty, and every one of them carried themselves like a person who had been doing the same difficult work for a very long time and had made whatever accommodations that required.

But he had spent eight years reading rooms full of people for what was wrong with them, and this was wrong, and it took him until they had crossed half the colonnade to find the word for it.

*There were no young ones.*

Not one. No twenty-year-old carrying somebody's case. No half-trained boy standing at the back learning how it was done, the way there was always a half-trained boy standing at the back of everything. An institution sends its old hands out to speak and its young ones out to watch, because that is how the thing gets passed along, and this institution had sent three people in middle age and no witnesses at all.

Xion looked at that for as long as it took them to reach the steps, and understood that he was looking at an entire generation not being there, and that everybody who had known how to summon these people had died in the same season as everybody who was supposed to be walking behind them now.

The senior of the three stopped in front of Elara at the correct distance and inclined his head the correct amount.

"Majesty. Ilareth. Storm-Marshal."

That was all of it. No formula, no assurance of loyalty, no reference to twenty years of anything. He gave her his name and his function and stopped, and stood there, and waited to be told what she wanted.

---

The corridor beat them before they ever reached the chamber, and Xion saw it happen four hours before anybody said so.

They went down the way the expedition went down now — Long Knives at the front and the back of the column, the same woman with the forgettable face at the head of it, nobody writing anything. Elara came. Nothing anyone said was going to prevent that and nobody wasted the breath. Farleen was already at the camp when they arrived, having gone down the day before with the second load of lamp oil, and she looked at three strangers in traveling coats coming out of Dead Man's Passage and then went and stood where she could see all three of them and the platform at once, and stayed there for the rest of it.

Xion walked the last stretch behind Ilareth, and so he was watching when the man came through the low throat of stone that folded back on itself before the chamber, and stopped, and looked up.

He did not say anything. He looked at the ceiling — perhaps a meter and a half above his own head, wrong-proportioned, pressing — and then he looked back the way they had come, at the length of the passage and the narrowness of it, and then he went on.

It took less than three seconds and nobody else saw it, and Xion, who could not have explained what storm magic was if a man had put a knife to him, understood that he had just watched a professional take a measurement and not like the answer.

He filed it. He did not mention it. There was no version of mentioning it that was not a healer telling a battle-mage his business.

Then the chamber, and the door, dark and enormous and patient in the blue light off the walls, and the three of them went up onto the platform to work.

---

Xion could not follow it.

He wanted to be exact about that afterward, when he came to set it down: not that he followed some of it, not that he grasped the general shape. He could not follow it at any point, and the whole of what he took out of that chamber came in through the wrong door. What he had was pressure. Something in his ears, twice, of the kind you get on a fast descent; a change in the air like the minute before weather breaks, arriving and then not arriving, over and over, the way a sneeze does not come. Once a low sound he felt in the floor of his chest and not in his ears at all.

And bodies. Bodies he could do.

They worked for three hours and they worked well. He would say that afterward to anybody who asked and to several people who did not. The two younger ones took positions at the platform's edges and the Storm-Marshal took the center, and none of them talked much, and what they said to each other was short and technical and completely opaque, and the shape of it was the shape of any three good practitioners in a room with a difficult case.

The first attempt was large. Xion did not need to understand it to know that — you could feel the size of it in the floor, in the pressure, in the way the two at the edges braced. It went on for perhaps a quarter of an hour. Nothing happened. The engraving did not so much as flicker, and Xion, watching, had a moment of something close to affront on their behalf: the door had lit for Elara like a lamp and would not give these three so much as a glow.

The second attempt was smaller.

So was the third, and the fourth, and by the fifth Xion had stopped watching the door entirely, because he had realized what he was actually looking at and it was more interesting than the brass.

*They were narrowing.* Each attempt was tighter and more particular than the one before it, more localized, more carefully placed — a man does not do that when he is flailing, he does that when he is eliminating. They had begun by trying to do the largest thing they could do and had found it useless, and were now working down through a series of progressively smaller and more precise applications, and there was nothing in their faces that suggested any of them expected the next one to be different. They were finishing the list. That was all this was. They were going to the bottom of the list because the crown was standing behind them and because a man does not report *it cannot be done* having tried three things.

And it was costing them.

He read it in the order he always read it. The breathing first, which changed in the second hour and did not change back — deeper, slower, deliberately managed, the breathing of someone who has decided to control the one thing still available to be controlled. Then the jaw on the younger of the two at the edges, set in a way that was going to give him a headache by evening. Then, in the third hour, the hands.

The tremor came late, which told him it was fatigue and not fear. It arrived in Ilareth's left hand between one attempt and the next, a fine thing, nothing dramatic, and the man put the hand flat against his thigh for the length of four breaths and it stopped, and he lifted it again and went back to work. Twenty minutes later it came back. He did the same thing. It stopped.

Xion had watched a hundred people do some version of that, at bedsides, over patients, in the last hour of a long bad night. He had done it himself. It is what a competent person does when their body has begun to file its objections and the work is not finished.

The seventh attempt was very small indeed and lasted less than a minute.

Then Ilareth lowered his hands and came down off the platform.

---

"We cannot open it."

He said it to Elara from four feet away in the voice a man uses to read out a figure, and then he stopped talking.

Then nothing. Xion waited — he was aware of waiting, aware of the shape in his chest where the rest of it was supposed to go — for the qualifications. *With more time.* *With more of us.* *If we might send for instruments.* *The conditions here are not favorable.* Every one of those was available and at least two of them were true, and the man did not reach for any of them, and the silence after the sentence went on just long enough for everybody in the chamber to understand that there was not going to be any more of it.

"Can you tell me why?" Elara said.

"Yes." And then, without any change of register at all: "What we are is a weapon for open ground. Weather, pressure, force — at distance, at scale, against something that can be pushed or broken or moved. That is the entire art and I will not dress it up for you. Give us a formation on a plain and we will end it." He nodded, once, back at the platform. "That is a lock. It is a very fine lock in a stone throat with no room in it, and there is nothing there to hit and nowhere for the working to stand. We are not being defeated by it, Majesty. We are not engaged with it. It is the wrong instrument and it was the wrong instrument before we came down the passage."

*Before we came down the passage.* Xion thought about a man stopping under a low ceiling for three seconds and going on anyway.

"You knew," Elara said.

"I suspected in the corridor. Suspecting is not knowing, and the crown asked." A very slight movement of one shoulder that was not a shrug and did not aspire to be. "Now it is known."

Xion looked at his face while he said it, because that was his job in this room, and what he found there was considerably worse than the words. The man was not embarrassed. Embarrassment would have been comfortable and would have passed. What was in Ilareth's face was the thing Xion had seen exactly twice in his life on people who had gone to fetch him too late — a specific stillness that comes when a person is holding something and has decided that the room is not going to be given any of it.

Twenty years. A summons that had to be dug out of a lost volume and a seal that had needed cleaning. Three days for the palace to remember how to ask, and half an hour to be answered. And this.

He did not say any of it. There was nothing to be done with it here and saying it would have been for his own benefit. He glanced sideways once and found Vesk looking at exactly the same place on exactly the same face, and the two of them did not acknowledge each other at all.

"Then I am where I started," Elara said.

"No, Majesty." Ilareth said it immediately. "You have eliminated an option, which is worth what it cost, and you should now go abroad."

The chamber, Xion thought, went very slightly quiet.

"What that mechanism requires is either magi-tech engineering or magical scholarship. It is a made thing with an operating procedure and the procedure has been lost; that is a problem for people who take mechanisms apart and read them, or for people who read languages nobody speaks. Neither is us and neither will ever be us. Gunastran has the first. Uratha has the second." A pause of no particular length. "It is my professional judgment that the crown should send, and send soon, and that whichever you choose you will not be wasting the journey."

Xion sat with that for a moment and found that he had to look away from the man to manage it.

Every institution he had ever watched, in all his years inside his father's house, would have spent that sentence differently. There was a version of this available that any competent body would have taken — *this requires further study; we would want to bring instruments; with the crown's leave we will consult our own archives and return in a month* — and it would have cost the treasury a month and the corps nothing, and it would have been entirely defensible, and every man in that chamber would have accepted it. And the alternative was to stand in front of the only authority you had waited twenty years to be given, having just failed in public, and tell her that what she needed was in another country.

They had come at a run and argued themselves out of the work in the space of about ninety seconds.

"And there is a request," Ilareth said.

---

He delivered it standing exactly where he had been standing, without preamble, in the same voice.

"While we appreciate the efforts of the Arol Batae to guard the Empress's person, since the earliest days of the empire the Tor'anar have been the arcane arm of the Valanar. We are the authority on all things arcane, and to have foreign mages so close to the Empress requires our expertise. With respect, we request that at least three Tor'anar agents be dispatched to accompany the Empress on any expedition to such a dangerous magical place with unknown magical people."

Xion took the sentence apart while it was still in the air, because that was what he had been built to do and it happened whether he wanted it or not.

It opened by conceding the Arol Batae. Not politely — **precisely**, at the exact thing the Arol Batae were for, which was Elara's body, and it did not encroach on it by a finger's width. It did not ask for a share of the guard. It asked for something else, on a different ground, and the ground was named in the middle of it: *we are the authority on all things arcane.* That was not a boast. Xion had heard men boast in this register all his life and this was not it. It was a position — the sort of thing that used to be written into an instrument somewhere and then sat unexercised for two decades until there was finally somebody entitled to hear it stated.

And the number was theirs. *At least three.* Not *whatever your Majesty thinks appropriate*, not *such number as may be convenient* — a professional minimum, arrived at by people who had thought about the problem before they walked in, offered to the crown as a figure rather than as an opening. It did not occur to Xion until much later that this was because they had never in their institutional life haggled with a Valanar and would not have known how to begin.

Elara let it sit for a moment.

"Let me be certain I have this," she said. "You are telling me to bring these people into Balisha, and warning me about them, in the same sentence."

"Yes, Majesty."

He did not smile. He did not soften it, or expand on it, or produce the small laugh that a man produces to show he is aware of how a thing sounds. He agreed that this was so, in the tone of somebody confirming a figure in a column, and stood there waiting for her answer with no apparent sense that anything had occurred.

Xion had to look at the floor.

"Both things are true," Ilareth added, evidently having decided the point required support. "The expertise is real. The risk is real. I do not find them difficult to hold at once."

"No," Elara said. "Clearly not."

She granted it.

That was all. She said, "Three, then. Name them when you have chosen," and turned to Vesk about the arrangements for the column going back up, and it took perhaps four seconds from beginning to end. There was no ceremony in it. There was no acknowledgment that anything had been settled that had been unsettled for twenty years, because as far as Elara was concerned nothing had — a professional body had made a reasonable request in its own area and she had said yes to it, in a tent forty feet from a door that would not open, in the middle of a bad afternoon.

It cost her nothing. Xion was sure of that. He watched her turn away from it entirely and begin discussing lamp oil.

He was watching the other side of the chamber, though, because he had spent his life watching the other side of chambers, and what happened there was very small and he would have missed it if he had been anywhere else. The younger of the two battle-mages at the edge of the platform — the one with the jaw, who had not spoken once in three hours — went absolutely still for a moment. Not startled. Not pleased. Still, in the way people go still when something arrives that they had stopped constructing a version of the future that contained.

Then he picked up his coat.

---

They went back up with the column, and at the surface they took their leave in about the same number of words they had arrived with, and walked back across the city to whatever it was they had been doing with themselves for twenty years.

Xion stood in the yard behind the customs house for a while afterward with the ledgers under his arm and the smell of the tunnels still in his clothes, and did the accounting, because he could not help it.

They had been summoned by an instrument that had to be excavated out of a mislaid volume, under a seal that had needed cleaning. They had come the same morning, from four streets away. They had gone five hundred meters into the ground and worked for three hours in a space that was wrong for them, and failed in front of the crown. They had said so in five words. They had then, unprompted, told the Empress that what she needed was in Gunastran or in Uratha and that she should go quickly. And having argued themselves out of the only work anyone had offered them in twenty years, they had asked for one thing, which was permission to stand near her while somebody else did it.

And in the whole of that afternoon, from the first attempt to the leave-taking, Xion could not recall that anybody had thanked them.
