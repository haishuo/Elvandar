They came down on a grey morning and forgot the weather inside a minute.

That was the first thing Xion learned about Urathan scholars. He had gone up to the third tier to meet them out of courtesy, and walked back down beside a man who explained the difference between two schools of transcription with real warmth for about four hundred meters, and then stopped talking in the middle of a clause, because the passage had opened out and there was forty meters of dark over their heads and the Door was in the far wall.

He did not finish the sentence. He never came back to it. Later that day Xion watched a junior scholar walk into a crate he had been looking directly at, and be steered around it by a colleague who was also not looking where he was going, the two of them navigating by some sense that was plainly not sight.

They had come prepared to work. Glass-fronted cases, reference stands that unfolded on articulated brass joints, instruments Xion had no names for — every item of it carried down four hundred meters on the backs of men the crown was now paying by the day for the privilege. They had not come prepared for the size of it.

Nobody was. The Door was five meters by three, faced in an alloy that had gone five thousand years without marking, and cut corner to corner with script and geometry so fine that the eye kept sliding off it and having to start again. Their lamps did not reach the ceiling. The lead scholar stood six feet back from the surface and looked at it the way a surgeon looks at a patient before the first cut, with total professional hunger, and Xion recognized the expression from the inside and thought better of him for it.

Then they went to work, and the Door did nothing.

---

It went on doing nothing for a long time.

That was the shape of the next three weeks, and nobody had warned him, because nobody had known. He had understood in advance that it would not be quick. He had not understood what *not quick* would be like from the inside: rubbing paper and magnification lenses, sequences cross-referenced against manuscripts that had come down through four centuries of increasingly tired copying, two hours of argument over whether a translation made by a man who might never have seen the original could be trusted on a single character. It was not dramatic. It was people being extremely careful in a cold room, on a plank floor, six hours at a stretch, for weeks.

The camp took on the rhythms of a place people lived in. Lamps were refilled on a rota. The Long Knives walked the loads in and out and spoke to nobody. Somebody started a tally of the days on a beam and somebody else rubbed it out, and Xion never established which of them he agreed with.

And underneath all of it, every three seconds, the pulse.

It had been down here before the crown had ever heard of it, and the camp stopped hearing it inside a week, the way you stop hearing your own heart. Once, at the end of a long evening, he caught himself realizing he had not noticed it in two days — and then could not stop noticing it for an hour.

---

Elara worked with them, and she was strange to watch.

The scholars read the Door as a text. They deciphered, proposed, cross-referenced, argued about whether a sequence was numerical or phonetic or both. She did none of that. She would stand in front of a section of the surface for a long time without speaking, and then say something that had not come out of any manuscript in the camp, and it would be right.

Nobody could account for it, including her. She did not try. She had told him once that she had not been present for the most important moment of her own life — that everything she knew about it, she knew because somebody had described it to her afterward. Watching her lay a hand flat on five thousand years of alloy and say *this part is later than the rest of it* with no more ceremony than a woman checking whether a pan was hot, Xion thought that she was probably not present for this either, and had simply stopped expecting to be.

The scholars found it maddening. They also stopped working whenever she spoke, which told him everything about how far they had come to rely on it.

The disagreement came on the fourteenth day.

The lead scholar had a translation, a documented sequence, and four centuries of transmission behind him. Elara had a sense that the thing began in the wrong place — that it was a song starting at the second verse, which sounds like music and never resolves. Neither could prove the other wrong. They held it for two days, working around each other, both entirely civil, neither giving a finger's width.

Xion watched that and was shown something he had not expected to be shown down here. He had spent his life among people who wanted to be the one who was right. This was two people making each other more accurate, at cost to both, in front of witnesses. It was not agreement. Agreement would have been worth a great deal less.

The sequence came out of the friction on the sixteenth day, and it belonged to neither of them. Her hand went flat against a section low on the left. The scholar looked at his manuscript, and then at her hand, and then the two of them turned to the same point on the surface at the same moment, and said nothing for long enough that the room noticed.

Xion did not follow what had happened. He did not need to.

---

The morning they tried came without ceremony.

People checked lamps that did not need checking. Positions were confirmed that everybody already knew. He had seen it before in operating rooms — instruments laid out twice, hands wanting a job — and he recognized it for what it was, which was not preparation. It was the last few minutes of being able to do something.

The scholars had their notebooks open. Farleen had put herself near the mouth of the passage in a way that was not obviously anything. Elara stood in front of the Door for a while, looking at it.

Not the scholars. Not twelve of them with four centuries of manuscripts between them, and not Xion, and not anyone else the crown had paid to be down here. A girl raised in an Arol Batae compound, who had spent years going after things nobody had told her to want.

Then she began.

He could not have described what she did to anybody who had not been standing there. She touched points on the surface in an order. She spoke, briefly and quietly, in something that was not a language he knew and that she had not been able to tell him she knew either, when he had asked. She performed a sequence that had taken nineteen days and twelve people to assemble, without the notes, without checking, without the particular carefulness of somebody doing a thing they have learned.

She did it the way you do something you have always known.

The pulse changed before anything moved.

It did not get louder. It got *closer* — the way a sound you have stopped hearing becomes a sound that is aware of you. Something a long way down had registered what had just been done to its door. The scholars went absolutely still. Nobody spoke, because there was nothing yet to say.

Then the Door began to move, and it was not opening.

It was turning. The whole circular face of it started to rotate, slowly, without a catch anywhere in it, without any of the grinding that five thousand years should have earned. It made one sound: a single deep tone, like a bell struck at the bottom of a well. And the script and the geometry cut across its surface turned with it and became, in turning, what they had always been — not decoration, not inscription, not a message. Teeth. Bearings. Graduations.

It was a gear. The camp had spent three weeks reading the face of a gear.

Then the wall engaged. Gears in the surrounding stone, each of them the size of a house, took up the load and began to move with it, and to begin with the sound of that was not a sound at all but a pressure in the soles of the feet and in the teeth and behind the eyes. Not a door in a wall. A wall that was a door, and behind it something that had been waiting to be asked.

Light came through the widening edge.

Blue. Perfectly steady. It did not flicker, because nothing in it was burning. It had been making that exact color in a sealed chamber since before anyone's grandparents' grandparents, and it did not know it was supposed to have gone out. The gap widened and the light came out across the plank floor and up the walls and over the faces of everybody standing there, and it was wrong for underground and wrong for night and wrong for anything Xion had a word for.

Not one of twelve scholars said anything at all.

---

The Door finished its rotation.

What it revealed was a room eight meters across. Perfectly circular. Walls so smooth they seemed not to be made of anything — no grain, no seam, no join. No dust. No fall of debris, no webbing, no mineral crust, no rot. Clean the way a room is clean when somebody has cleaned it that morning.

It was also small.

The silence had a very particular quality, and Xion knew the differences between silences. This was a great many people trying to fit something enormous into something much smaller and finding that it would not go. He was doing it himself. Three weeks, a treaty, a claim surrendered in perpetuity, and a right of way bought in a room under a hundred and eighty-five meters of rock — for a closet.

A junior scholar said, into the middle of it, very quietly: *"It was a broom closet after all."*

One or two people almost laughed. It was not unkind. It was exactly what everyone in the chamber was thinking.

Then the pulse reached them, and inside, it was louder.

Not dramatically. Perceptibly, and past all argument. One beat every three seconds, arriving in the chest a moment before it arrived in the ear. The scholar who had made the joke was now paying very close attention to the floor. Whatever was making that sound was underneath this room, and it was large, and it was regular, and it had not stopped in five thousand years, and it had not stopped now.

The lead scholar stepped to the threshold — the threshold only — and put a hand out into the chamber, palm up, the way you put a hand out to check for rain.

He held it there. Then he turned round and looked at Elara.

"The air is fresh," he said.

Not stale. Not sour. Not the flat dead stillness of a sealed space. Moving air, coming from somewhere and going somewhere. Something had been drawing breath through this place the entire time it had been shut, and had never once stopped, and was doing it now with twelve Urathan scholars standing in the doorway.

Xion put the thought together carefully, the way he assembled thoughts that had to be right.

"It's still running," he said, to nobody.

Past the antechamber the floor kept going. A passage, descending, five meters wide, lit the whole way in the same steady blue — and the proportions of it had changed. Still smooth, still seamless, but considered now, made by somebody who had thought about what it would be like to walk down it every working day of a life, and not only about how to hold up the rock.

And in it, running, a conveyor.

Xion looked at it for a while to be certain of what he was seeing. A mechanical transport. Moving. A platform going down at a walking pace and another coming up beyond it, built to carry people between depths at a scale that made climbing an inconvenience worth engineering away. Not a ladder. Not stairs. A system, for a population, in daily use.

"The antechamber was never the destination," he said.

The lead scholar had come to stand beside him. His professional certainty had been rearranging itself for twenty minutes and he had the face of a man who had been very confident about the dimensions of a room.

"It was the waiting room," he said.

"Yes."

---

Somebody stepped onto the conveyor.

It was not ceremonial. The platform was moving and somebody had to be first, and the decision got made the way practical decisions get made. It took the weight without comment. It carried him down. The rest of them followed, and the passage took them, and the camp's light fell away behind and stopped mattering.

The descent took a long time. The grade was shallow enough that it did not feel like dropping — more like walking down a hill that had been very carefully made. Nothing about the passage hesitated. It had been cut by people who knew where they were going and had no doubt at all about arriving.

The pulse grew as they went. Never faster. Always that same three-second interval, patient as a metronome, and deepening — chest, then sternum, then somewhere behind that.

The passage stayed plain the whole way down. Well-made and unbeautiful. Built to work and not to impress, which told Xion something he turned over for most of the descent: whatever this had been for, the people who used it had not thought of the journey as an occasion.

Then the conveyor slowed, and the ceiling went up.

They came through a space built to be crossed rather than used, and Xion understood its function before he had any word for it. Vaulting where there had been none. The first carved ornament since the Door itself — restrained, deliberate, beautiful. Brass fittings that took the blue light and gave it back warmer. Somebody five thousand years ago had stood here and decided that a person arriving ought to be told, without being told, that they were arriving.

The scholars had been silent for the whole descent. They were quieter now.

Then the space opened outward, and Xion stopped, because everyone in front of him had stopped.

---

The first reflex is automatic. You come into a room and you find its edges.

He looked for the far wall and could not find it. There was something in the distance, but it was not a wall; it was a building. And between here and there was more city than his eye could account for — streets wider than anything in Kaha'an, rooflines going up past easy reckoning, and it curved, gently, away and around, and he still could not find the end of it.

Then he understood that there were no shadows.

The light was everywhere at once. Sourceless, even, arriving from every direction in exactly the same measure, so that nothing on the whole level cast anything. He looked down at his own hands to check. Nothing. Four hundred meters under the desert, sealed since before his language existed, and lit like open ground at noon.

The scholar beside him said, very quietly: *"Those are plants."*

A terrace, near enough to see properly. Green. Growing — actually growing, putting leaves up toward a ceiling that was not a sky, under a light with no sun behind it, alive when it had every reason on earth not to be.

He looked back at the big building he had been using to steady himself, the one whose proportions said, in a language he did not speak and could read anyway, that it had mattered enormously to somebody.

He looked at its windows.

The lights were on. Warm light, quite different from the blue-white everywhere else, coming from inside. From here it looked like a building people had stepped out of for the afternoon.

Then he noticed that its corner had moved.

He had been looking at it long enough. He had not seen the movement start — you could not, it was much too slow — but it had been in one place and it was now in another. He fixed his eye on it and held it there and waited, and it drifted. Slowly. Patiently. Without any effort at all.

The entire level was turning.

And at the center of it, a shaft. Open straight down through the floor of the world, past level after level, past where the light went and past where the eye could follow, and at the bottom of it a deep warm glow the color of the earth's own heat.

That was what had been beating. He knew it now, in his chest and in the floor and in his teeth. It had been running since before anybody alive had been born, and it had never stopped, and it had not stopped for them.

It was running now.

Nobody moved. Nobody spoke.

The lead scholar sat down. Slowly, on the nearest surface, without any drama at all. His legs had simply reached a decision ahead of him.

Farleen found his hand. She did not say anything, and neither did he, and they stood there together looking at the shaft and at the slow impossible drift of a city turning underneath them.

The moment held.

A city, lit from inside. Still turning. Five thousand years old and still running. No name for it. Not one word among any of them that fit what they were looking at.

That was all. That was everything.

Somebody — Xion never knew who, and thought afterward that it might have been himself — whispered it.

"Lathion."
