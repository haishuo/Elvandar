Elara found the inscription at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, which was to say she walked past it.

Two scholars had been at it since before noon. It ran along the base of a frontage on the second boulevard, twenty feet of it, and they had gone from enthusiasm through disagreement into the flat exhausted courtesy of men who have each decided privately that the other is being obtuse. Xion had heard the tail of it going past an hour earlier and had kept walking.

Elara came along the boulevard with three of the contingent behind her, slowed, stopped, and looked at the wall for perhaps a count of five.

"This one matters," she said.

And walked on.

Behind her, the argument stopped as though it had been cut with a knife. One of the two men got out his notebook. The other looked after her the way a man looks at a barometer that has just dropped, and then also got out his notebook.

She could not tell them why. Ormuth asked her, an hour later, with great care, and she tried — Xion watched her try — and got two sentences in and stopped, and said that the words she had were not the words she would need, and that was the end of it. She did not know what the inscription said. She knew that it had weight, the way a phrase in a half-remembered song has weight even when you cannot produce the line.

What Xion noticed, and went on noticing for the rest of the day, was what the scholars did with it.

They stopped asking her what things said.

It happened without an announcement and without anyone appearing to decide it. By the evening the question being put to Elara was not *what is this* but *is this one important*, and that was a question she could sometimes answer. She gave them impressions, hedged to the point of uselessness — that one is older than the others, those two go together, that one I can't feel at all — and they wrote each impression down like a field note, with her name against it and every one of her hedges preserved, and then went back to translating by their own methods.

She was not doing their work. She could not have done their work. She was telling twelve exhausted men where to spend the next fortnight of their lives, and they had worked out, somewhere in the last three weeks, that this was worth more to them than an answer would have been.

---

There was a second thing, later, smaller and considerably stranger.

They were crossing the plaza on the way to a chamber the scholars wanted looked at, and there was a console standing in the middle of it. That was the word people had settled on for these — waist-high, a surface, no controls anybody could identify, no obvious purpose. There were a dozen of them scattered around the Crown and not one had done anything.

Elara went past it, slowed, and laid her hand flat on the top of it.

She did not press. There was nothing to press. She stood with her palm on it for a moment and then took her hand away.

"This one's sleeping," she said, half to Xion and half to nobody at all, and kept walking.

Brennek, two steps back, wrote it down.

Xion watched her go and saw the thing he was not supposed to see, which was that she had surprised herself. It went across her shoulders and was gone: the look of somebody who has said out loud a thing they had been intending to keep.

He did not ask her about it. Whatever she had access to, it was not available to her when she wanted it, and it had never once come when it was called.

---

Ormuth came to find him in the late afternoon.

The Urathan lead scholar had been at the Door for three weeks and inside the city for two days, and had aged about four years across the whole of it, in the specific way of a man who is having the best time of his life and getting no sleep whatsoever. He arrived at Xion's elbow, clasped his hands somewhere behind him, and stood without saying anything for long enough to be noticed, which Xion had learned meant that something had been composed in advance.

"May I say something unprofessional."

"Please."

"I have published twenty-seven papers." He was looking down the boulevard rather than at Xion. "Several of them are quite good. Two of them are, I think, the reason anyone in the towers knows my name." He stopped. Started again. "This morning your Empress looked at a wall for four seconds and told me which half of the Gallery inscriptions to spend my life on. I do not know how. I am reasonably certain that she does not know how either, which is worse."

"It is," Xion said.

"I would trade all twenty-seven." There was nothing behind it at all — no weight, no performance — and that was what made it land. "I have been turning that over since about the ninth hour and I cannot make it come out any other way, and I would rather you heard it from me than worked it out from the way I have been behaving."

He nodded once, and went away, and Xion stood where he had left him for rather longer than he intended to.

It was not flattery. That was the trouble with it. It was a man saying aloud a thing he was going to have to learn to live with, and he had said it to a foreign official in a corridor because there was nobody else down here he could say it to.

Xion put that where he put things, too. The pile was getting large.

---

The dispatch went up on the second day.

It was expected; it had been in the agreement, in the third clause of the access terms, and nobody had made any secret of it. Ormuth wrote it, and brought it to Xion before it was sealed, and stood there while he read it.

It was a good report, and that was the difficulty with it, and the difficulty did not improve on a second reading. It was factual, and measured, and it described the preservation and the functioning systems and the scale of the place in language that would not have embarrassed a surveyor. It did not exaggerate anywhere. It did not need to. The facts as stated, in the flattest possible arrangement, were sufficient to change the world.

He sealed the pouch and handed it to the courier and walked with him as far as the Gatehouse, and watched him step onto the ascending platform with the thing under his arm, and had one clear moment — no longer than a breath — of wanting to call him back.

Not because the report was wrong. Because Xion had spent the better part of a year learning how information moves, and he knew exactly one thing about information of this size, which is that it does not travel by itself. It brings people with it.

The platform carried the courier up into the passage and out of sight.

He let it be a problem for later. There was a great deal of later available.

---

Farleen had been there the whole time.

She had been there the whole time in the way she was always there the whole time, which was to say that the camp had food in it, and the water was accounted for, and somebody had noticed that the scholars had not eaten since the previous evening and had done something about it before it became a medical question. She did not go and look at things. When the Gallery of Unknowns was found she asked what was in it, was told that nobody knew, said *huh*, and went back to counting lamp oil that nobody needed.

The city did not interest her much. It interested her the way weather interests a sailor: as the conditions in which the thing she actually cared about was going to happen.

She watched him. She had been watching him for five years and had got extremely good at it, and Xion knew perfectly well that she could tell the difference between the two halves of his day — the half where he had been walking around inside a marvel, and the half after the room on the eastern arc.

She did not ask. She brought him a bowl at some point in the evening and stood next to him while he ate it, which was her entire method and had been since the Amber Leaf.

"You've got the shoulders," she said.

"I know."

"Right," she said, and took the bowl back, and went away.

---

They gathered at the Compass in the evening, around a fountain running clear water for nobody, and it was the best meeting Xion had ever sat in.

Not because anything was resolved. Nothing was resolved. It was the best meeting he had ever sat in because every single person in it was working at the outer edge of what they knew and was prepared to say so out loud, which in his experience of meetings put it in a category by itself.

Ormuth offered a reading of the foundational inscriptions on the Crown, and then immediately offered two other readings, and explained that the grammar permitted all three and that the difference between them was whether the passage described a founding, a renovation, or a rededication — and that these were not the same thing at all, and that a civilization which rededicated its capital had a history behind it that a civilization which founded one did not. Two of his colleagues disagreed with him and with each other. The argument went on for half an hour and everybody enjoyed it enormously.

Brennek gave the infrastructure. He did it in the order he had found it, without emphasis, and he did not editorialize once, and the effect of hearing water, climate and agriculture read out flatly one after another in a plain Kaha'ani accent was considerably larger than any amount of emphasis would have achieved. When he finished there was a silence.

Everybody in the plaza understood what they had just been told. Nobody was ready for it. It had been two days.

Somewhere in the middle of a dispute between two scholars about which of two passages was the older, Elara said, without looking up from her cup:

"The second one."

Both men turned and looked at her.

She did not elaborate. She drank her water. After a moment the two of them turned back to each other and resumed the argument on the ground she had just moved them to, and did not remark on it, and Xion understood that this was now simply how the expedition worked.

The meeting ran long. Nobody wanted to leave.

---

Late, he walked the Crown by himself.

He could feel the rotation now. It had not changed — it had never changed, it was the one thing in this place that had been doing exactly the same thing at exactly the same rate since before anyone had a word for anything — but his body had learned the rhythm of it in two days and now reported it up to him without being asked, a long slow lean underneath everything, like standing on the deck of an enormous ship in a calm.

He walked past frontages whose purposes he could not guess. He walked past a plaza built to hold a great many people, laid out by somebody who had thought hard about how a crowd moves, for gatherings that were not going to happen again. He walked past a building whose front was worked in interlocking brass panels, and the panels did something to the light that nothing else on the road did, and he stopped there for a while.

Somebody designed that. Not *somebody* in the abstract, the way you say it about a mountain. A person had stood approximately where Xion was standing, with the same problem in front of him, and had solved it this way rather than one of the other ways, and had been pleased with it, or had not been, and had gone home afterward.

And here was the thing that had been waiting for him all day, and that he had come out here alone to finally look at.

That person had gone home *within his lifetime*.

Not in some unreachable antiquity. Not in the age of legends, where you can put a civilization and feel nothing about it. Twenty years ago there had been a man on this road, going the other way, looking up at this same steady light — and four hundred meters above him a boy had been sitting on an estate wall in the sun, sick and bored and not allowed out, and neither of them had known the other one was there.

They were not a lost people.

They were **neighbours**. They had been on the other side of a wall the whole time, close enough that his whole childhood and their whole ending had happened inside the same handful of years, separated by some stone and a door nobody could open. And then they had gone. All of them. Every road, every terrace, every one of these frontages with the lights still burning inside them.

The lights were still burning.

That was what he kept arriving back at, standing in the middle of it. The lights were on and the water was clean and the air was moving and the machines were still going up and down the corridors polishing walls for people who were not coming. The city had not noticed. Twenty years and the city had not noticed, and it was going to go on not noticing, patiently, in the dark, for as long as its mechanisms held.

He stood in a lit street in an empty city and could not think of one single thing to do about any of it, which was, he was aware, the oldest and least useful feeling he had.

---

He found Elara at the railing.

Third time in two days. He stopped a little way off, as he had that morning, and waited until she half-turned, and then went and stood beside her.

They looked down together. The shaft dropped away under them, level below level, each one turning at its own rate and none of them agreeing, down past the last thing he could resolve and further than that, to the slow warm colour everything up here was arranged around. The pulse came up out of it. Three seconds, and then three seconds. It had been the same at the Door and it was not the same here, because here it had weight, and the weight came up through the boots and stayed.

The scholars had found something in the Crown inscriptions that afternoon — cross-references, four of them, all pointing at the same place on a level below this one. A chamber where the Haureni had put what they knew. They had started calling it the Repository, half certain and half hoping, and had been saying the word all evening in the tone of men who did not want to jinx it.

Neither he nor Elara said that this was where the heart of the place was.

They both knew it. He had known it since he came out of the passage and saw the light at the bottom of the shaft, and she had known it before that, and neither of them had a word for how.

She did not move toward it. She stood at the rail with her hands open on the metal and looked down, and Xion would have staked a great deal, on no evidence he could have produced for anybody, that some part of her had already gone down without her.

The city turned under them. The lights held. Somewhere below, the water ran clear through a system that had gone twenty years without anybody to drink it, and the terraces put up leaves toward a light with nothing behind it, and the machines went along the corridors of the Crown doing what they had been told to do a long time ago by people who had stacked their bowls, and squared their tools, and pushed the table back against the wall, and left the door open.

*You cannot think about a thing you cannot name.*

They had names for it now. Small ones. Wrong ones. Enough to be going on with.

Now the thinking started.
