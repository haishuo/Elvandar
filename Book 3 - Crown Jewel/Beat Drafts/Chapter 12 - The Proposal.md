# Chapter 10: The Proposal

**Timeline:** Days after the Gunastran decision (which Balisha has not yet received)
**Characters:** Xion, Elara, Kalden's envoy
**Emotional work:** The reader must hold two incompatible truths at once — that this offer is genuinely good, and that accepting it is genuinely impossible. By the end, the reader understands why Elara will refuse, and is not sure she should.
**POV:** Xion

---

## Beats

1. The envoy arrives in form. Not a courier with a sealed letter slipped through a side gate — a formal Erulian delegation, announced at the palace doors, bearing the seals of the Principality and requesting official audience with the Empress of Balisha. The ceremony of it is itself a message: Kalden is not making a private approach that can be privately declined. He is placing this offer in the diplomatic record where it exists whether Elara responds to it or not.

2. The delegation is received in one of the formal audience chambers — not the throne room, not the informal rooms where Elara actually governs, but the middle register of ceremonial space that says *we are taking this seriously without committing to anything yet*. Xion is present. The reader should register that without explanation — his presence in formal proceedings has become normalized enough that no one remarks on it, even though he holds no title that justifies it. He is there because Elara wants him there.

3. The envoy is good at his job. He does not read awkwardly from a scroll. He speaks the offer as though he believes in it — which he may, because Kalden has sent someone who genuinely thinks this is a good idea, not a functionary who knows it's theater. The offer as presented: Prince Kalden of Erulius proposes formal marriage alliance with the Empress of Balisha. The Erulian Principality, upon marriage, pledges full military and diplomatic realignment — away from the forming Compact, toward Balisha and its sphere. In plain terms: marry Kalden, and the strongest piece on the opposing board switches sides.

4. The envoy does not dress this up beyond its logic. He does not speak of Kalden's personal qualities or the beauty of Erulius in spring. He speaks like someone who understands he is delivering a political instrument, and the instrument's value is self-evident. This, too, is a signal from Kalden — that the offer respects Elara's intelligence, that it is not trying to seduce her through flattery but to convince her through math. It is, in this way, almost respectful.

5. Elara listens without expression. This is deliberate — she was trained by the Arol Batae, and one of the things you learn when you are trained by soldiers is that your face is information your opponents do not need. She lets the envoy finish. She thanks him with correct formal language. She tells him an answer will come within three days, as protocol requires. The envoy withdraws. The chamber clears.

6. Xion and Elara are left alone — or near enough to it, with whatever Arol Batae guards stand at the far edges of the room, close enough to protect and far enough to be functionally absent. The first exchange between them is not political. It is a look. The reader should feel, in that look, that both of them already understand what the other is thinking, and that neither of them has decided what to do with that understanding yet.

7. Xion speaks first. And what he says is not what Elara expected, and not what the reader expected: he tells her the offer is good. Not good in the way that polite advisors call things *good* before explaining why they can't be done. Good in the way a physician names a treatment — this is the correct intervention, applied to the correct site, at the correct time. His voice is careful and exact. He walks through the geopolitical logic the way he reads symptoms: Erulius is the founding pillar of the Compact; without Erulius the Compact lacks both the military credibility and the coalition legitimacy to hold; without the Compact there is no continental war; there is no body count. He says the last part quietly. The body count.

8. He is not recommending she accept. He is not telling her what to do. He is making sure she understands what the offer actually is — which is not an insult dressed as a proposal, not a trap, not a power play. It is a genuine solution to the problem that has been keeping them both awake. This is the healer in Xion, the reader of systems, the man who was trained to see the elegant intervention: he cannot encounter a real solution and pretend it isn't one. Even now. Even this.

9. Elara asks what she already knows he'll say: does it work. Does the math actually hold. Xion confirms it does. If Erulius withdraws — formally, publicly, citing realignment through marriage — the Compact loses its spine. The remaining members are smaller, less credible, less unified. They may still form some coalition, but not one capable of sustaining a continental war. The marriage doesn't just buy time. It ends the threat.

10. A silence. Xion fills it with something he hasn't said yet: that he understands why she can't accept. Not won't — can't. He knows her. He knows how she was raised, what the Arol Batae made of her, what it means that she has spent her life learning to solve problems with a weapon in her hand. He is not asking her to become someone she isn't. He is just making sure — the word is careful, chosen — making sure that when she refuses, she knows what she's refusing.

11. What Elara does not do: she does not immediately refuse. She sits with it. The reader needs to see that Elara is someone who takes the offer seriously enough to actually weigh it — that her refusal, when it comes, is not reflexive but considered. The Arol Batae trained her to fight, not to negotiate with her body; but the Arol Batae also trained her to evaluate terrain before she moves. She is evaluating terrain.

12. What Elara thinks about, in the silence, is not Kalden. She does not know Kalden — not his face, not his voice, not what kind of man he is in the rooms where no one is watching. What she thinks about is the architecture of the offer: that she would be traded, however willingly, for the peace she was put on the throne to create. That the thing she offers Kalden is her body and her name. That the thing Kalden offers Balisha is the lives of thousands of people who will not die if she says yes.

13. She asks Xion — and the question costs her something to ask — whether he thinks she should accept. This is not a request for permission. It is something closer to wanting to hear him say what she knows he won't say. What she is actually asking is whether he would let her go, and whether he would call it the right choice if he did.

14. Xion does not answer immediately. The reader sits in that pause with him, feeling what the healer in him is fighting: because the healer's answer is yes, and the man's answer is no, and those answers live in the same body and he has never had to separate them like this. When he speaks, what he says is this: it's her decision, and whatever she decides, he will help her bear the consequences. He does not say what he would choose. He does not say what he wants. He gives her something that looks like freedom and feels like grief.

15. Elara refuses. Not in this scene, not yet — the formal answer will go to the envoy in three days, through correct diplomatic channels, in careful language that declines without insulting the Principality. But the reader knows, in this moment, that the answer is already decided. The refusal lives in the room before she says it aloud.

16. What changes between them in this beat is small and devastating. Xion knows she's going to refuse. Elara knows Xion knows the cost of what she's refusing. Both of them know — without saying it, without needing to — that there is someone else present in the conversation even though there is no third person in the room: the version of the future where she said yes, where thousands of people lived, where the war never came. That person is not them. But they can see it. They will not be able to un-see it.

17. The scene ends without resolution or catharsis. They don't embrace. They don't argue. They go back to the work of governing, because there is always more work, and because stopping to feel things fully is a luxury that Empresses cannot afford and healers learn not to. The beat draft's final image is simply Xion moving to the table where the maps are, and Elara following, and the two of them returning to the war they are trying to prevent by methods that will, in the end, not prevent it.

---

**The logic of the offer must be airtight:** The reader cannot be allowed to dismiss Kalden's proposal as obviously cynical or obviously wrong. If the politics feel contrived, the chapter's emotional weight collapses — Elara's refusal becomes easy, Xion's conflict becomes abstract, and the downstream consequences feel arbitrary rather than inevitable. The offer must land as the reader knows it would land on a competent advisor's desk: this is the correct move. It just requires something she cannot give.

**Xion's divided self:** This chapter is where the reader sees, most clearly, that Xion's two natures — the healer who reads systems and the man who loves Elara — are not always compatible. For most of the series, those two things have pointed in the same direction. Here they don't. He cannot resolve this. The reader should not resolve it for him. The tension is the point.

**What Kalden's motives do and don't matter:** The skeleton notes that Kalden has terrible motives. The reader may or may not know this at this point — what matters is that his motives are structurally irrelevant to the offer's validity. A genuine solution offered by a bad actor is still a genuine solution. Elara's refusal is not because she has seen through a trick. The trick, if there is one, hasn't changed the math. This is harder and more honest than a villainous proposal whose flaws justify an easy no.

**Sets up the love triangle resolution:** Whatever comes next — whatever Xion and Elara become to each other, or don't — this is the moment the reader will return to. Not as the moment they got together, but as the moment they chose each other over thousands of lives and had to know, clearly, that they were making that choice. The catastrophic consequences the skeleton names don't feel catastrophic unless the reader understands, here, what was on the other side of the scale.
