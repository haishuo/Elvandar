# Chapter 8: Inhabitants

**Timeline:** The first two days inside Lathion, opening the morning after Chapter 7 (Book 3, weeks 15–17)
**Characters:** Xion, Elara, Farleen, Brennek (the crown surveyor), Ormuth (the Urathan lead scholar), the Urathan contingent, the Arol Batae detail, the three-mage Tor'anar detachment
**Emotional work:** Wonder becoming habitable, and then wonder acquiring a date. The chapter's first movement is the pleasure of a functioning ancient city that rewards looking — corridors, systems, automatons, the small daily business of learning your way around the impossible. Its second movement is what the looking turns up. The expedition came in expecting a ruin five thousand years dead. What it finds is a house that was closed, deliberately, by people who had time to close it, and closed within living memory. The chapter ends on a question with a number in it and no mechanism to explain the number.
**POV:** Xion, close third-person limited

---

## Beats

1. Waking, and the absence of morning. The lights have not changed. The pulse has not changed. The rotation has not changed. Xion surfaces in a camp thrown together in a hurry the night before, in an alcove off one of the Crown's boulevards, and for a few seconds does not know where he is. Then he does, and the knowing is stranger than the forgetting was. He slept here. It is all still true.

2. The camp stirs. People are quiet in the particular way of people who have not settled on a register for the impossible, and fill the silence with small practical business — packs redone, water counted, lamp oil checked. Somebody points out that the lamps are not needed here, and laughs, and the laugh opens something. The wonder of the night before has not faded; it has become the medium they are moving through, which is its own kind of extraordinary. A junior scholar eats standing up, staring down the boulevard, with a face Xion last saw on a child at a festival.

3. Elara at the railing above the central shaft, where she has been for some time. Something has settled in her overnight that was not there. Not peace — resolution. She has been carrying pieces of this place her whole life without knowing what they were, and here the pieces fit. She does not say any of it and does not appear to have the words. Xion reads it off her shoulders and the stillness of her hands and the way she finally touches the railing: lightly, deliberately, the way you touch something you have come back to. *Standing here was not discovery. It was return.* He registers the change and cannot account for it, and the narration does not account for it either.

4. The first practical problem is not the mystery. It is that they have no names for anything. Chapter 7 ended on somebody whispering the city's name; the expedition has a word for the whole and no word for any part of it. Conversations take twice as long as they should — *the big chamber*, *the upper level*, *the room with the brass things* — and Xion watches three men spend five minutes failing to establish which corridor they each mean.

5. So the naming starts, the way language always starts: somebody says a word, somebody repeats it, and it exists. The top level becomes **the Crown** because it sits at the top, and the word is in three separate mouths inside an hour, each of them believing they coined it.

6. Other names accumulate over the day like a dialect. *The Gatehouse* — the vaulted threshold at the conveyor's foot, where the plain passage turns ornamental. *The Compass* — a circular plaza where six boulevards meet around a fountain still running water so clear the basin looks empty, and by which you can orient. *The Gallery of Unknowns*, which makes the junior scholars laugh every time. None of these are what the Lathionese called them; those names died with the city. Xion finds himself moved by the smallness of it — improvised human sounds pressed against something enormous — and it is the only response available.

    *(No Lathionese proper name appears in narration or dialogue, here or in any Xion-POV chapter.)*

7. The automatons, met properly. Not humanoid: purposeful shapes in brass and crystal, sized to their work, moving through corridors polishing surfaces nobody has dirtied and adjusting fixtures nobody has disturbed. They go around the living the way water goes around stones — no acknowledgment, no pause. The first one startles the camp. The second is remarked on. By the third, people have learned to step aside. Xion watches one work and thinks about building something that outlasts its makers, and about a machine that does not know its people are gone. **That thought is his day-one assumption and the chapter is going to take it away from him** — the narration carries it as his, not as fact.

8. Brennek. The crown surveyor who spent four days building a plan for a door that would not open, produced eleven pages on it, and looked four hundred minutes for a seam he never found — and who now has an entire city in front of him. He is the same man he was in that chamber: unexcitable, methodical, and quietly in the state Xion recognizes from his own worst nights, which is a phenomenon and no framework. He finds the Crown's first ventilation node by following the draught with a dampened cloth. He works out the water distribution by tasting the output of three fixtures and naming the mineral differences. He brings what he finds to Xion rather than to the scholars, which is a small thing and changes nothing practical, and Xion notices it anyway.

9. Xion and Brennek walk the infrastructure while the scholars work the inscriptions — two relationships with the same city, running in parallel. Water first: still cycling, still clean, drawn from somewhere hot below. Then the climate, which never varies, and which turns out to be not luck but an exchange system elegant enough that Brennek stands in front of it for ten minutes without speaking. Then the growth terraces, and the plants they saw from the Gatehouse the night before, which are not survivors clinging on but the output of a working agricultural system — light, water, enrichment, all of it automated and all of it running.

10. **The room.** This is the chapter's hinge and it belongs to Xion, not to the surveyor. Off one of the Crown boulevards, a dwelling. He goes in because the door is open and because he has spent his adult life walking into rooms uninvited to see what is wrong in them. What is wrong is that nothing is. There is no disaster signature anywhere in it. Nothing broken, nothing on the floor, nothing dropped where a hand let go of it. And the tell is not the cleanliness, because the automatons account for cleanliness — a machine polishes; it does not **stack**. The vessels are stacked. The furnishings are against the walls rather than where people were using them. Things have been set down where a person coming in afterward would find them. Xion has been in rooms where somebody fled and rooms where somebody was turned out, and he knows the difference in his hands, and this is neither. **This is a room somebody closed.** He does not say the rest of it aloud, to Brennek or to anyone: they were not surprised, they had time, they chose, they expected somebody to come — and they knew they were not coming back.

11. What the room cannot tell him is *when*, and **nothing in Lathion is decaying, so nothing can be dated by decay.** Brennek's answer is the composting beds at the end of the terraces. The agricultural automatons have gone on farming for twenty years because farming is what they were built to do; the crop is harvested and stored, and what turns before it can be stored is carried to the beds and put back into the soil — brought, covered, left, and covered again by the next year's lot, with nothing to mix it because nothing down here does anything it was not told to. The bank cuts like a riverbank and it is **banded**. Every other surface in the city is brass, and brass looks identical after twenty years and after two thousand; the garden is the one thing in Lathion that ever got older. He counts the layers. Twenty, and not nineteen, and not thirty. *Somebody was eating this. Right up until they weren't.*

12. And then one step further, which neither of them wants to take and Xion takes anyway: **twenty years is also the Rending.** Brennek rejects it, on the only ground available and correctly — the Rending was a surface event, it scoured the province above, and there is no mechanism by which it reaches down through stone and a sealed brass door. He is right. There is no mechanism. The number does not move.

    *(Beat 19 must not re-argue this. The argument runs once, here.)*

13. Elara, mid-afternoon, and the recalibration that comes out of her. Passing an inscription the scholars have been arguing over for two hours, she slows, looks, says quietly that this one matters, and keeps walking. She cannot say why when asked, tries briefly, gives up: the words she has are not the words she would need. Over the day the scholars stop asking her *what does this say* and start asking *is this important* — a question she can sometimes answer. She gives impressions and hedges; they catalogue the impressions like field notes and go back to translating by their own methods. She is not doing their work. She is telling them where to spend it.

    *(Her calibration is fixed and the rest of the trilogy runs on it — see the footer. She does not read.)*

14. A second, smaller, stranger beat with her. Crossing a plaza, she lays a hand on a waist-high console that nobody can identify, presses nothing, and after a moment lifts her hand and says, half to Xion and half to nobody, that this one is sleeping. Then walks on. Brennek writes it down. She looks faintly startled at herself, the way people do when something private gets out, and Xion does not ask, because months of this have taught him that asking produces nothing. Whatever she has is not available on demand.

15. Ormuth. The lead scholar has been watching her since the Door and comes to Xion privately in the late afternoon to say, in substance, that he does not know what she is, that he is quite certain she does not either, and that he would trade his whole published career for what she told him about the Gallery inscriptions this morning. It is not flattery and it is not offered as praise. It is a man saying aloud something he is having to teach himself to live with.

16. The dispatch, handled in one beat and without drama. Ormuth composes a preliminary report — the terms of access required regular communication with the towers and nobody objects. Xion reads it before it goes. It is factual, measured, appropriately astonished, and does not need to exaggerate, because the facts are sufficient to change the world. He seals it, hands it to the courier, watches the courier step onto the ascending conveyor, and has one moment of wanting to call it back — not because the report is wrong but because information of this size does not travel without consequences. He lets the consequences be later.

    *(No suspicion, no alarm, no bad faith. The scholars are scholars. The political move happens in Uratha, off-page, when leadership reads this.)*

17. Farleen, who has been here the whole time doing what Farleen does. She organizes the camp, manages the supplies, and makes people eat, which in a place like this is a full occupation. She does not explore and does not need to; the city interests her the way weather interests a sailor. She watches Xion — who he talks to, how he is carrying what he is carrying, whether his shoulders have started doing the thing they do when a problem stops being analytical. She contributes nothing to the naming. She does not need words for what she already understands.

18. Evening at the Compass, and the best meeting Xion has ever sat in — not because anything is solved but because everyone in it is working at the edge of what they know and saying so. Ormuth offers tentative readings of the Crown's foundational inscriptions, which may describe a founding or a renovation or a rededication; the grammar is ambiguous and the scholars enjoy the argument enormously. Brennek presents the infrastructure. The implications are enormous and everybody knows it, and tonight nobody is ready for enormous. Into a dispute about which of two passages is older Elara says *the second one* without looking up from her cup, offers nothing further, and both scholars turn and look at her and then go back to arguing on the ground she has just moved them to. The meeting runs long. Nobody wants to leave.

19. Late, walking the Crown alone, and this beat does **not** re-run beat 12. The rotation is perceptible to him now, not because it changed but because his body learned it. He passes buildings whose purposes he cannot guess and plazas built for gatherings that will not happen again, and what he has carried out here to look at is not the argument but its cost. Somebody designed that façade. Somebody walked this boulevard in the opposite direction and looked up at this same steady light — and did it inside his own lifetime. A Lathionese child could have been on this street while Xion was a child in the sun above it, four hundred meters up, neither knowing the other was there. **They were not a lost people. They were neighbours**, separated by a sealed door and some stone. And then they were gone, all of them, and the boulevard is not gone and the light is not and the automatons are not.

20. He finds Elara at the railing again — the third time in two days — and stops a respectful distance back before going to stand beside her. They look down together at the warm light far below and the pulse coming up out of it, patient beyond patience. The scholars have already found Crown-level cross-references to a chamber on a lower level where the Lathionese kept what they knew, and have started calling it *the Repository*, half certain and half hoping. Neither Xion nor Elara says that this is where the heart of the place is. They both know it. She does not move toward it, and he is sure without being able to say how that some part of her has already started down.

21. Closing image. The city turns, the lights hold, the water runs clear, and the small human words the expedition has made today sit on top of something enormous. *You cannot think about what you cannot name.* They can name it now. Now the thinking starts.

---

**Nothing here is damaged, and no beat may imply that it is.** The city was emptied by a prepared, consensual sacrifice — the Lathionese closed the house and walked out. Nothing broke, nothing was interrupted, and no system is failing. An earlier version of this draft had the farm *"running at a fraction of its capacity,"* which the prose then turned into a food shortfall, which then needed decline to explain it — three inventions stacked on one another, and all of them arguing quietly against the bequest framing the next chapter is built on. **The terraces fed roughly a hundred thousand people for the two thousand years of the seal and they are still producing at that rate for nobody.** That fact needs no explanation; it is the same fact as the lights being on.

**On the recency discovery, and why the room comes before the number.** The earlier version of this draft routed the whole thing through the surveyor reading wear-rates, which made the discovery an inference delivered as a figure and asked the reader to trust an expert's arithmetic. The room is better on both of the tests this project uses. It is **denser** — one closed house says at once that they were not surprised, that they had time, that they chose, that they expected somebody, and that they knew they were not returning, and no line of dialogue decodes any of it. And it is **Xion's instrument**: he is a healer, he reads rooms and bodies for what was done to them, and the Warrens taught him what a fled room looks like against a room somebody was turned out of. The automatons are what makes it work — they account for the cleanliness, so cleanliness proves nothing, and the tell has to be *arrangement*, which is a decision and not a mechanism. Then Brennek supplies the one thing the room cannot: a date. Order matters. He feels the wrongness, the surveyor times it, the timing turns out to be twenty years, and only then does the Rending come up.

**On Brennek, who is not a new character.** He is the senior surveyor of Chapter 2 — four days on a plan, eleven pages, four hundred minutes hunting a seam — and Chapter 3 opens on Kael reading his report aloud. Introducing a crown Engineer here would also have broken Chapter 3's settled position that engineering *"is neither us and neither will ever be us"*; Balisha has no such man to send. It has this one, who earned the trip by cataloguing the door. His register is unchanged: unexcitable, practical, oriented toward use where the scholars are oriented toward understanding. **His choice to bring findings to Xion rather than to Ormuth is noted and not interpreted.** It accrues later.

**On Elara's calibration, which this chapter sets for the trilogy.** She does not read Lathionese. She *recognizes* it, the way an English speaker recognizes Spanish without speaking it, and occasionally a passage carries an impression — warning, importance, age, connection to that other passage. She cannot translate, cannot explain, and often cannot articulate the impression without sounding strange to herself. The same holds for the machines: something is *sleeping*, something is *answering*, and she has no theory and no fluency. Her responses scale with what she is responding to — small on the Crown, larger in the Repository, largest at the Engine. **This chapter holds them small deliberately.** The scholars' shift from *what does this say* to *is this important* is the chapter's most consequential political seed: it establishes her as indispensable and un-replicable, a phenomenon they orbit rather than a colleague they work with, and Uratha's later interest in containing that standing starts here, in a week when nobody is acting in bad faith.

**On what the wonder is doing while all this happens.** The exploration beats should be a pleasure to read — each corridor a discovery, each system a question, the feeling of stepping into an ancient functioning place that rewards investigation. The chapter's turn does not cancel that. It complicates it. By the night walk the register has moved from awe to the first genuine unease, which is not Chapter 10's horror but the cold thread that leads to it, and the chapter closes on the question unanswered.

**On the Arol Batae and the Tor'anar.** Both are present and both are furniture, which is correct: Xion's attention is on the city, so the reader's is. One or two glancing observations is the entire budget — the Arol Batae taking the alcove mouth without being told to, a Tor'anar who never once looks at the boulevard because he is watching the Urathans look at it. **Do not give either institution a scene here.** But note what the detachment is for, because the book pays it without trying: the Tor'anar are embedded to supervise the Urathan delegation's magical conduct, which is the jurisdiction they claimed in Chapter 3 — and the Urathan delegation turns out to have an undisclosed interest of its own in Chapters 10 and 12. The corps that was ignored for twenty years and had to argue its way onto this expedition was **right about the specific risk it named.** Nobody says so and nobody needs to, provided the detachment has been visible enough in 7–9 to be remembered.

**On length and unnamed walk-ons.** Chapters 4–6 run 5,400–6,200 words and Chapter 7 is deliberately 3,050; this one covers two days of exploration and belongs with the former. Every unnamed walk-on — junior scholars, porters, the Arol Batae, the Tor'anar — is male by default under the standing rule.

**Sets up:** Chapter 9. The scholars have located the Repository from Crown-level references, the expedition has named the city and settled its working patterns, and the closed-house reading is what Chapter 9's bequest framing is built on — *nothing here was abandoned; everything was set down.* Elara's reactions, held small here, begin to grow.
