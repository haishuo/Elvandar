# Chapter 9: Neighbours

**Timeline:** The second day inside Lathion, continuous from Chapter 8 (Book 3, weeks 15–17)
**Characters:** Xion, Elara, Farleen, Brennek, Ormuth (the Urathan lead scholar), the Urathan contingent
**Emotional work:** Living with the number. `Inhabitants` ended on a date nobody can explain and nobody can use; this chapter is the two days of ordinary working life that happen on top of it. The expedition settles into a rhythm, the scholars recalibrate around Elara, a dispatch goes up to the surface, and the wonder holds — right up until Xion walks the Crown alone and understands what the date actually means, which is not a mystery but a proximity. The Lathionese were not a lost people. They were on the other side of a wall while he was a boy in the sun. The chapter turns from unease into grief without ever naming it as grief, and ends pointing downward.
**POV:** Xion, close third-person limited

## Beats

1. Elara, mid-afternoon, and the recalibration that comes out of her. Passing an inscription the scholars have been arguing over for two hours, she slows, looks, says quietly that this one matters, and keeps walking. She cannot say why when asked, tries briefly, gives up: the words she has are not the words she would need. Over the day the scholars stop asking her *what does this say* and start asking *is this important* — a question she can sometimes answer. She gives impressions and hedges; they catalogue the impressions like field notes and go back to translating by their own methods. She is not doing their work. She is telling them where to spend it.

    *(Her calibration is fixed and the rest of the trilogy runs on it — see the footer. She does not read.)*

2. A second, smaller, stranger beat with her. Crossing a plaza, she lays a hand on a waist-high console that nobody can identify, presses nothing, and after a moment lifts her hand and says, half to Xion and half to nobody, that this one is sleeping. Then walks on. Brennek writes it down. She looks faintly startled at herself, the way people do when something private gets out, and Xion does not ask, because months of this have taught him that asking produces nothing. Whatever she has is not available on demand.

3. Ormuth. The lead scholar has been watching her since the Door and comes to Xion privately in the late afternoon to say, in substance, that he does not know what she is, that he is quite certain she does not either, and that he would trade his whole published career for what she told him about the Gallery inscriptions this morning. It is not flattery and it is not offered as praise. It is a man saying aloud something he is having to teach himself to live with.

4. The dispatch, handled in one beat and without drama. Ormuth composes a preliminary report — the terms of access required regular communication with the towers and nobody objects. Xion reads it before it goes. It is factual, measured, appropriately astonished, and does not need to exaggerate, because the facts are sufficient to change the world. He seals it, hands it to the courier, watches the courier step onto the ascending conveyor, and has one moment of wanting to call it back — not because the report is wrong but because information of this size does not travel without consequences. He lets the consequences be later.

    *(No suspicion, no alarm, no bad faith. The scholars are scholars. The political move happens in Uratha, off-page, when leadership reads this.)*

5. Farleen, who has been here the whole time doing what Farleen does. She organizes the camp, manages the supplies, and makes people eat, which in a place like this is a full occupation. She does not explore and does not need to; the city interests her the way weather interests a sailor. She watches Xion — who he talks to, how he is carrying what he is carrying, whether his shoulders have started doing the thing they do when a problem stops being analytical. She contributes nothing to the naming. She does not need words for what she already understands.

6. Evening at the Compass, and the best meeting Xion has ever sat in — not because anything is solved but because everyone in it is working at the edge of what they know and saying so. Ormuth offers tentative readings of the Crown's foundational inscriptions, which may describe a founding or a renovation or a rededication; the grammar is ambiguous and the scholars enjoy the argument enormously. Brennek presents the infrastructure. The implications are enormous and everybody knows it, and tonight nobody is ready for enormous. Into a dispute about which of two passages is older Elara says *the second one* without looking up from her cup, offers nothing further, and both scholars turn and look at her and then go back to arguing on the ground she has just moved them to. The meeting runs long. Nobody wants to leave.

7. Late, walking the Crown alone, and this beat does **not** re-run beat 12. The rotation is perceptible to him now, not because it changed but because his body learned it. He passes buildings whose purposes he cannot guess and plazas built for gatherings that will not happen again, and what he has carried out here to look at is not the argument but its cost. Somebody designed that façade. Somebody walked this boulevard in the opposite direction and looked up at this same steady light — and did it inside his own lifetime. A Lathionese child could have been on this street while Xion was a child in the sun above it, four hundred meters up, neither knowing the other was there. **They were not a lost people. They were neighbours**, separated by a sealed door and some stone. And then they were gone, all of them, and the boulevard is not gone and the light is not and the automatons are not.

8. He finds Elara at the railing again — the third time in two days — and stops a respectful distance back before going to stand beside her. They look down together at the warm light far below and the pulse coming up out of it, patient beyond patience. The scholars have already found Crown-level cross-references to a chamber on a lower level where the Lathionese kept what they knew, and have started calling it *the Repository*, half certain and half hoping. Neither Xion nor Elara says that this is where the heart of the place is. They both know it. She does not move toward it, and he is sure without being able to say how that some part of her has already started down.

9. Closing image. The city turns, the lights hold, the water runs clear, and the small human words the expedition has made today sit on top of something enormous. *You cannot think about what you cannot name.* They can name it now. Now the thinking starts.

---

**On Elara's calibration, which this chapter sets for the trilogy.** She does not read Lathionese. She *recognizes* it, the way an English speaker recognizes Spanish without speaking it, and occasionally a passage carries an impression — warning, importance, age, connection to that other passage. She cannot translate, cannot explain, and often cannot articulate the impression without sounding strange to herself. The same holds for the machines: something is *sleeping*, something is *answering*, and she has no theory and no fluency. Her responses scale with what she is responding to — small on the Crown, larger in the Repository, largest at the Engine. **This chapter holds them small deliberately.** The scholars' shift from *what does this say* to *is this important* is the chapter's most consequential political seed: it establishes her as indispensable and un-replicable, a phenomenon they orbit rather than a colleague they work with, and Uratha's later interest in containing that standing starts here, in a week when nobody is acting in bad faith.

**On the dispatch, which is a mechanism and not an alarm.** Ormuth composes it, the access terms required it, nobody objects, and it is factual and measured and does not exaggerate because the facts do not need it. **No suspicion, no bad faith, no narrative warning.** The scholars are scholars. Xion's one moment of wanting to call the courier back is his own, and it is about the size of the information rather than the honesty of the report. The political move happens off-page in Uratha when leadership reads it, and the reader should not be told that in advance.

**On the night walk, which is the reason the chapter exists.** The argument about the Rending ran once and finished at the end of `Inhabitants`; **it does not run again here.** What this beat carries is not the mystery but its cost. Somebody designed that façade. Somebody walked this road in the opposite direction and looked up at the same steady light, and did it inside Xion's own lifetime — a Lathionese child on this street while he was a boy on an estate wall in the sun four hundred meters up, neither knowing the other existed. **They were not a lost people. They were neighbours.** That is the whole beat and no line of dialogue may decode it.

**On Farleen.** Quiet, consistent, unchanged. She organizes the camp, manages supplies, and makes people eat, which here is a full occupation. She does not explore and does not need to; the city interests her the way weather interests a sailor. She reads Xion and says almost nothing. The love triangle is not advanced — it is maintained.

**On the Tor'anar and the Arol Batae.** Furniture, exactly as in `Inhabitants`, and for the same reason. One glancing observation is the whole budget. Do not give either institution a scene.

**Sets up:** `Bequest`. The scholars have located the Repository from Crown-level cross-references and have started calling it that, half certain and half hoping. Elara does not move toward it and some part of her has already started down. Her reactions, held small across these two chapters, begin to grow from here.
