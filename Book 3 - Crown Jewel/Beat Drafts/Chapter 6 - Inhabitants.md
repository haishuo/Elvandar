# Chapter 6: Inhabitants

**Timeline:** First days inside Lathion, immediately following Chapter 5
**Characters:** Xion, Elara, Farleen, Engineer Kaiden Voss, the Urathan scholars
**Emotional work:** Wonder becoming habitable. Chapter 5 was the single overwhelming moment; Chapter 6 is what it feels like to *live* inside that moment. The impossible has become the air they breathe. The expedition gets to work, and in getting to work, begins to give the city a vocabulary it can use. The register is not diminished awe but *inhabited* awe. The reader should feel the D'ni / Uru pleasure of exploring an ancient functioning city — each corridor a discovery, each system a question, the sense that years of work are visible from the doorway. But a second movement runs beneath the wonder. As the expedition settles in and truly begins to look, the signs accumulate that this is no five-thousand-year-old ruin: the city is ancient, but it was inhabited within living memory and emptied only twenty years ago. By its end the chapter turns, quietly, from wonder toward the first unease — the question of what happened here, and how recently.
**POV:** Xion, close third-person limited

---

## Beats

1. Waking. There is no morning. The lights have not changed, the pulse has not changed, the slow rotation has not changed, and Xion wakes in a camp that was set up in a hurry the night before, in a broad alcove off one of the upper-level boulevards, and for three full seconds he does not know where he is. Then he does, and the knowing is stranger than the forgetting. He is inside a city that has been sealed underground for as long as anyone can plausibly remember. The air is fresh. The lights are on. The plants are growing. He slept here. Now he is awake, and it is all still true.

2. The expedition stirs. People are quiet in the particular way of people who have not yet decided what register to use for the impossible. Small practical actions fill the silence: packs reorganized, water counted, lamp oil checked. Then someone remembers the lamps are unnecessary here, and laughs, and the laughter breaks something open. People start talking. The wonder of the previous night has not faded. It has become the medium they move through, which is its own kind of extraordinary. One of the junior scholars eats breakfast staring out at the boulevard with an expression Xion last saw on a child at a festival.

3. Elara is already up. She has been up for some time. Xion finds her at the railing of the upper level, where it overlooks the central shaft and the warm light rising from far below and the other levels of the city turning slowly in their separate rotations. She does not turn when he arrives. She has been doing the same thing she has been doing since the Brass Door opened — the almost-reaching, the hand that lifts toward a surface and then stops. But this morning something has settled in her that was not there last night. Not peace exactly. Resolution. Whatever she has been carrying — the fragments, the inexplicable recognition, the knowledge that arrives without source — it has found its frame. She has been carrying pieces of this place her whole life without knowing what they were. Standing here, the pieces fit. *Standing here was not discovery. It was return.* She does not say any of this. She does not have language for it. Xion reads it in the line of her shoulders, in the stillness of her hands, in the way she finally does touch the railing — lightly, deliberately, the way you touch something you have come back to. Something has changed in her. He does not understand what.

4. The first practical problem of the day is mundane and immediate: they have no names for anything. The scholars keep saying *the large chamber* and *the upper level* and *the room with the — you know, the brass things*, and every conversation takes twice as long as it should, because no one can refer to anything with precision. Xion watches three scholars spend five minutes trying to explain to each other which corridor they mean and thinks: this is the first thing that needs solving. Not the mystery. The vocabulary.

5. It starts organically, the way language always does. Someone uses a word, someone else repeats it, and suddenly it exists. The uppermost level — where the conveyor deposits them, where the expedition has made camp among the grand boulevards and the buildings whose windows still glow with warm interior light — someone calls it *the Crown*, because it sits at the top. The word sticks immediately. It has the rightness of a name that had been waiting to be said. Xion hears three different people use it within an hour, each as though they had coined it themselves. Other names follow over the course of the day, accumulating like a new dialect: the threshold space at the conveyor's base, where the utilitarian passage gives way to vaulted ceilings and brass ornament — *the Gatehouse*, because it marks the crossing from one world into another. A circular plaza on the Crown where six boulevards converge around a fountain that still runs with water so clear it appears empty — *the Compass*, because you can orient yourself by it. A long curving gallery lined with alcoves containing objects no one can identify yet — *the Gallery of Unknowns*, which makes the junior scholars laugh every time they say it. Each name is improvised, human, coined from function and first impression. None of them are what the Lathionese called these places. Those names died with the city. The expedition is giving Lathion a language it can use, and Xion finds himself moved by the smallness of it — tiny human words pressed against something vast. It is the right response. It is the only response available.

6. The automatons. The expedition encounters them properly on the first day. They are mechanical figures moving through corridors, maintaining surfaces, performing functions that have continued without interruption for fifty centuries. They are not humanoid. They are purposeful shapes — brass and crystal, sized for the work they do — and they ignore the expedition entirely. They clean surfaces no one has dirtied. They adjust fixtures no one has disturbed. They move around the living the way water moves around stones, without acknowledgment, without pause. The first encounter startles people. The second is noted. By the third, the expedition has learned to step aside and let them pass. Xion watches one polish a section of wall with mechanical patience and thinks about what it means to build something that outlasts its makers. The automaton does not know the Lathionese are gone. It does not know anything. It is doing what it was made to do, and it will do it until its mechanisms fail — and its mechanisms, five thousand years old, have not failed yet.

7. Elara, walking through the Crown with the lead scholar's contingent, passes an inscription the scholars have been arguing about for two hours. She slows, looks at it, and says only — quietly, almost to herself — *this one matters*. Then she keeps walking. The scholars stop arguing. Two of them write down what she said. One of them looks at her retreating back the way someone looks at a barometer that has just dropped. She cannot tell them why it matters. She tries, briefly, when asked, and gives up — the words she has are not the words she would need. She does not know what the inscription says. She knows only that it has weight: the way certain phrases in a half-remembered song carry weight even when you cannot recall the lyric. The scholars treat the remark as data. They do not press her for more. They have already learned, in the weeks at the Brass Door, that pressing produces nothing. What she gives, she gives. What she does not give, she does not have.

8. Engineer Kaiden Voss emerges as a presence in these first days. Where the scholars are drawn to text and theory, Voss is drawn to systems. He walks corridors the way a surgeon reads a body — looking for how things connect, what feeds what, where the logic of the infrastructure reveals itself. He locates the first ventilation node on the Crown by following the airflow with a dampened cloth. He maps the water distribution by *tasting* — actually tasting — the output from three different fixtures and identifying mineral signatures the others miss. He is methodical, unglamorous, and delighted in the specific way of someone whose professional skills have suddenly become relevant to the most interesting problem in the world. He brings his findings to Xion rather than to the scholars. This is a small choice, practically insignificant — the information would reach everyone eventually. But Xion notices it. Voss is oriented toward use. The scholars are oriented toward understanding. Both are necessary. The distinction matters.

9. Xion and Voss begin a parallel track. While the scholars document and debate and follow Elara's intuitive direction deeper into the Crown's inscriptions, Xion and Voss walk the infrastructure. They find the water systems first — still running, still clean, still cycling drinkable water from geothermal sources through processes Voss can describe only in pieces. Then climate regulation: the reason the temperature in Lathion never varies is not magic or luck but engineering, a system of thermal exchange so elegant that Voss stands in front of it for ten minutes without speaking, working through the implications. Then the growth terraces. The plants the expedition saw from the threshold of the Gatehouse the night before are not survivors clinging to life. They are the products of a functioning agricultural system — light, water, soil enrichment, all automated, all operational. Food is growing in Lathion. Not enough to feed a city — the system is failing, slowly, running now at a fraction of its capacity. But the thing Voss cannot stop turning over is *when* the failing began. He reads it in the wear, in the way the imbalances have propagated, and the machines tell him a clear and impossible story: this system was maintained, fully and expertly, until roughly two decades ago — and has been declining, untended, only since. Not five thousand years. Twenty. Voss says, very quietly, almost to himself: *Someone could live here.* And then, quieter: *Someone did. Not long ago at all.* He does not elaborate. He does not need to. Xion has already followed the same thought to the same place. And then one step further — the kind of step neither of them quite wants to take. Xion says it aloud, looking at Voss: *Twenty years. Same as the Rending.* Voss meets his eyes. Neither speaks for a moment. Then Voss answers it the way an engineer answers anything, with the careful flatness of a man rejecting a hypothesis on principle: *The Rending was a surface event. It scoured the province above. It would not have reached down here — sealed, underground, behind a brass door shut for millennia. There is no mechanism.* He is right. There is no mechanism. The numbers stay where they are anyway.

10. The second beat with Elara comes mid-afternoon and is smaller and stranger. They are crossing a plaza on the way to a Crown-level chamber the scholars want to investigate — the one with the panels of interlocking brass that catch the sourceless light in a way that makes the surface look liquid. There is a console in the plaza, low, waist-high, unattended by any hand the expedition could name. Elara passes it, slows, and lays her hand on it. She does not press anything. There is nothing to press. The console does not respond — there is nothing visible for it to respond *with* — and after a moment she lifts her hand and says, half to Xion and half to no one, *this one is sleeping*. Then she keeps walking. Voss, two steps behind, writes it down. Xion watches her go and notices that she looks slightly startled at herself, as people sometimes do when they have spoken aloud something they meant to keep private. He does not ask her about it. He has learned, in the months since this began, that asking does not help. Whatever she has access to is not available to her on demand.

11. Over the course of the day a working pattern establishes itself. The scholars stop asking Elara *what does this say* and start asking *is this important*. The new question is one she can sometimes answer. She gives impressions: *that one is older than the others*, *those two are connected*, *this is a warning of some kind, I think*, *that one I can't feel at all*. The scholars catalogue the impressions like field notes, each entry tagged with its source and its hedges, and then they go back to the actual work of translation with their existing methods. Elara is not doing their job. She is pointing them toward what to spend their time on. The lead scholar, who has been watching her for weeks, comes to Xion privately in the late afternoon and says: *I do not know what she is. I am quite certain she does not either. But I have published twenty-seven papers in my career, and I would trade all of them for what she just told me about the Gallery inscriptions.* He does not say it as flattery. He says it the way a man says a thing he is having to teach himself to live with.

12. By the second day the wonder has not faded but it has *settled*. The festival-child face on the junior scholar at breakfast persists. People are still finding things that stop them in their tracks. But the tracks themselves have become familiar. They know which corridor leads to the Compass. They know to step aside for the automatons. They know that the alcove off the third boulevard is where Voss has set up his tools. The city is still impossible. They have begun to be able to *find* their way through the impossible, which is its own small daily miracle and the chapter's actual emotional through-line: not the discovery of wonder but the inhabitation of it.

13. Farleen. She has been here the entire time, doing what Farleen does: being present without demanding attention, useful without being conspicuous. She organizes the camp. She manages the supplies. She ensures people eat when they forget to eat, which in a place like this is constantly. She does not explore. She does not need to. The city interests her the way weather interests a sailor — it is the environment in which the thing she actually cares about is happening. She watches Xion. She watches who he talks to, how he carries what he carries, whether his shoulders are tightening in the particular way they tighten when the weight of a problem has shifted from analytical to personal. She has been reading him for longer than anyone in this expedition. She does not contribute to the naming of Lathion's spaces. She does not need words for what she already understands.

14. The dispatch. On the second day, the lead Urathan scholar composes a preliminary report. This is expected — the terms of access included regular communication with the university towers, and no one objects. The report goes up through the conveyor system to the surface tunnels, then overland by courier. Xion reviews it before it leaves. It is factual, measured, appropriately astonished. It describes the city's preservation, the functioning systems, the scale. It does not exaggerate. It does not need to: the facts are sufficient to change the world. Xion seals the dispatch pouch and hands it to the courier and watches the courier step onto the ascending conveyor platform, and for one moment he has the impulse to call it back — not because the report is wrong, but because he understands, in the way he understands systems, that information of this magnitude does not travel without consequences. The courier disappears upward. The consequences will arrive later. He lets them be later.

15. Evening at the Compass. The expedition gathers around the fountain that runs with empty-looking water and shares what they have found. It is the best meeting Xion has attended in his professional life, not because anyone has solved anything, but because everyone is operating at the limit of what they know and is honest about it. The lead scholar presents tentative readings of the Crown's foundational inscriptions, which may describe a founding, or may describe a renovation, or a rededication — the grammar is ambiguous and the scholars argue about it with evident pleasure. Voss presents his infrastructure findings. The implications are enormous, and everyone in the plaza knows it, but tonight no one is ready for the enormous. Tonight they are still in the wonder. At one point, in the middle of a dispute between two scholars about which of two passages is older, Elara says quietly, without looking up from her cup, *the second one*, and offers nothing more. Both scholars turn to look at her. She does not elaborate. They turn back to their argument and continue it on the new ground she has just moved them to. The meeting runs long. No one wants to leave.

16. Late, walking the Crown alone. The rotation is perceptible to Xion now — not because it has changed, but because his body has learned the rhythm. He passes buildings whose purposes he cannot guess, plazas designed for gatherings that will never occur again, a structure with a façade of interlocking brass panels that catches the sourceless light and holds it differently. He thinks about the people who built this. Not abstractly — specifically. Someone designed that façade. And someone walked this boulevard, in the opposite direction, and looked up at the same steady light he is looking at now — and did it not in some unreachable antiquity but within his own lifetime. That is what the day has slowly handed him, and what he has carried out here into the quiet to finally look at. The city is ancient — five thousand years of it, true in every brass seam. But the people were here twenty years ago. A Lathionese child could have walked this boulevard while Xion was a child himself, above, in the sun, neither of them knowing the other existed. They were not a lost people. They were *neighbors* — separated by a sealed door and a few meters of stone — and then, twenty years ago, they were gone. All of them. The boulevard is not gone. The light is not. The automatons are not. He is walking through a city its people emptied within living memory, and the mystery has sharpened from the soft distance of legend into something with a date on it: the door was sealed long before any account he has read of it — why? — and the people were alive twenty years ago, and then they were not — what happened? It is everywhere around him, and answered by nothing — except, and this is the thing he could not push back from in the workshop and cannot push back from now, by a number. Twenty years. The year his mother died. The year Elara was orphaned. The year the desert came. And, somehow, also the year a sealed city beneath Kaha'an quietly emptied itself behind a door no living memory could place. Voss had the right of it. There is no mechanism. The Rending was a surface catastrophe; there is no pathway by which a wave on the world above could have reached down through brass and stone into Lathion. The two facts cannot be the same fact. They share only a year. *Only.* He stands on the boulevard and lets the word sit. *Only.* And the year remains twenty, and the number does not stop being twenty, and the question stays unanswered because the only answer he can imagine has no mechanism to it.

17. He finds Elara at the railing again, looking down the central shaft, and he stops a respectful distance away before he approaches. He has watched her come back to this railing three times in two days. Whatever pulls her here is older than he is and not something he can interrupt. After a moment she half-turns and acknowledges him without speaking, and he comes to stand beside her. They look down together. The pulse rises from below: one beat every three seconds, felt in the sternum, patient beyond patience. The lowest visible level glows the color of the earth's own heat. *That is where the heart of this place is.* Neither of them says it. They both know it. The scholars have already identified, from Crown-level cross-references, that there is a vast chamber on a lower level where the Lathionese stored their accumulated knowledge — *the Repository*, they have started calling it, half certain and half hoping. They have not gone there yet. They will. Elara stands at the railing and does not move toward it, but Xion can feel — he is sure of this in a way he cannot articulate — that part of her has already begun the descent.

18. Closing image. The city turns. The lights hold. The water runs clear. The names the expedition has given this place — *the Crown, the Gatehouse, the Compass, the Gallery of Unknowns* — are small human sounds in something ancient and enormous, and they are the first foothold. *You cannot think about what you cannot name.* Now they can name it. Now the thinking begins.

---

**The chapter's structural function:** Chapter 5 delivered the wonder. Chapter 6 makes it habitable. The reader needs to feel what it is like to *live* inside the impossible — to eat breakfast in a city that should not exist, to argue translations while automatons clean the floors, to walk a boulevard at night and feel the rotation underfoot. The wonder is not less here than in Chapter 5; it is differently shaped. No longer a single overwhelming moment but a sustained condition the characters must learn to operate within. That transition — from revelation to inhabitation — is the chapter's core emotional work, and the reason for its title.

**The naming as worldbuilding method:** The expedition's working names are also the *reader's* vocabulary for the rest of the book. By having the characters coin them in-scene, out of practical necessity, the names arrive with the naturalness of use rather than the weight of lore. *The Crown* is not a title bestowed; it is what someone called the top level because it was at the top, and it stuck. This grounds the reader in the characters' experience rather than the author's knowledge. Critically: *no Lathionese proper names appear in narration or dialogue.* Those names died with the civilization.

**Elara's connection — calibration:** This chapter is the first sustained portrait of Elara's connection to Lathion, and it must establish the rules the rest of the trilogy will operate by. She does *not* read Lathionese. She *recognizes* the language as Lathionese, the way an English speaker recognizes Spanish without speaking it. Occasionally a passage carries an *impression* — a feeling of *warning*, *importance*, *age*, *connection to that other passage*. She cannot translate. She cannot explain. She often cannot even articulate the impression without sounding strange to herself. The same applies to the city's machines: she sometimes feels that a fixture is *sleeping*, or that something is *answering*, but she has no theory of the technology and no fluency. Her reactions are stronger near concentrated meaning — and weaker on the Crown level than they will be in the Repository, which will be weaker than they will be at the Engine. The principle is metaphysical resonance, not scholarship: the Lathion essence in her responds in proportion to what it is responding to. On the Crown, that pull is small. The chapter holds it small on purpose.

**Elara's "return":** This is the line moved out of Chapter 5 ("Standing here was not discovery. It was return."). It lands here, not there, because the processing has had time to happen. In Chapter 5 she was overwhelmed. In Chapter 6 she has settled into something quieter and more profound — not the shock of recognition but the resolution of it. Xion sees the change in her but cannot fully account for it, which is correct: her connection is metaphysical, and his POV should register the change without explaining it. The phrase appears in narration as Xion's read of her interior, not as Elara's spoken thought.

**The scholars' adjustment:** The chapter establishes, without comment, the working dynamic that holds for the rest of the exploration phase: the scholars stop asking Elara *what does this say* and start asking *is this important*. That recalibration is the chapter's most important political seed. It establishes Elara as invaluable but un-replicable — a phenomenon they orbit, not a colleague they collaborate with. The scholars are not yet acting in bad faith. They are scholars doing what scholars do. But the seed of her exceptional standing — and of Uratha's later interest in *containing* that standing — is planted here, in their first week of working with her.

**The Uratha dispatch:** Handled in a single beat, without drama, without suspicion. The report is honest, measured, appropriate. The consequences of sending it are Xion's private thought, not a narrative alarm. The scholars are not acting politically here. The political move happens in Uratha, off-screen, when leadership receives the dispatch and grasps the magnitude. This chapter plants the mechanism — the information has left the city — without signalling that the mechanism is a threat.

**The turn from wonder to unease.** For most of its length Chapter 6 is still exploration — naming, wonder-becoming-habitable, the D'ni/Uru pleasure of an ancient functioning city. But the recency discovery changes its final register. As the signs accumulate — Voss reading recent failure in the machines, the mounting wrongness of a "ruin" that was tended within living memory — the chapter turns. By Xion's night walk it has reached the first genuine unease: not the horror of Chapter 8, but the cold first thread of it — the question of what happened here, and how recently. Chapter 6 ends on that question, unanswered. The wonder is not cancelled. It is complicated.

**D'ni / Uru energy:** The exploration beats should feel exciting and curious. Each discovery — the automatons, the ventilation, the water systems, the growth terraces, the sleeping console — is a room in an ancient city that rewards investigation. The reader should want to explore alongside the characters. The feeling is Atrus opening a new book, the player stepping into Kadish Tolesa for the first time: *what is this place, and what did the people who built it think was important?*

**Farleen's beat:** Quiet, consistent, structurally identical to her Chapter 5 role. She is here because Xion is here. She does not need to be integrated into the exploration narrative. Her contribution is presence and care — she watches him, manages the camp, ensures people function. The love triangle is not advanced here. It is simply maintained: she is present, she is attentive, she is not going anywhere.

**Engineer Voss — established, not resolved:** Voss is introduced as a working presence, not a character with an arc in this chapter. His function is to give Xion's practical orientation a partner and to make the scholarly/practical distinction visible in action before it becomes politically significant. His choice to bring findings to Xion rather than the scholars is noted but not interpreted — the significance accrues later.

**Sets up:** Chapter 7 (The Repository). The scholars have identified the Repository from Crown-level references. The expedition has named the city, established working patterns, and begun to understand what they are dealing with. Now they go deeper. The discoveries become more specific and more consequential. Elara's reactions — held small here — begin to grow.
