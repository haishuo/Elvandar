## How to write beat drafts

**The body is the page. This is the one hard rule, and it is the author's.** The beats that appear in the body of a beat draft are what appears **on the page**, and nothing that does not appear on the page may appear there. Instructions to the drafter — negative constraints, guardrails, rule citations, correction notes, provenance — are permitted **around** the body: above it in the header block, below it in the footer, in their own sections, or in a bracketed aside indented under the beat they attach to. They are **never** inside a numbered beat's own sentences.

The failure this prevents is specific and has happened. B3 ch4's beat A4 read *"She has been living two streets from her own shop. **Not from a ruin** — from the building itself, which is standing."* The *not from a ruin* was a guard against a drafter still carrying a retired error, and it was correct as an instruction. It went onto the page verbatim as prose, where it denied a ruin no reader had any reason to expect, and by denying it implied it had been in play.

The mechanism generalises. **A beat draft carries two kinds of sentence: what happens, and what a drafter must not get wrong.** The second kind is usually phrased as a negation, because that is the natural shape of a correction — *not a ruin, not a mistake, not dramatically, this is not about X* — and **those negations transcribe.**

So, in the body: what happens, what a character notices, what changes, and what a beat communicates. In an aside or the footer: *do not*, *must not*, *the prose should*, *the reader should feel*, *keep this short*, *corrected on [date]*, *an earlier version had*, *rule 2*, *see `People/X.md`*.

A constraint whose *content* is an event stays in the body, restated positively. *"…and it must stay unresolved: he still does not know whether he decided or whether he could not move. Do not let him arrive at an answer"* becomes, in the body, *he still does not know whether he decided or whether he could not move, and he does not arrive at an answer* — and the *do not let him* framing moves to the footer. **The relocation must not lose information.** If a guardrail cannot be restated without losing something, move it verbatim rather than compressing it. An ugly footer is cheap; a lost guardrail is not.

Two things that are **not** violations, and should not be hunted:
- **Negative choices.** What a character deliberately does *not* do is an event on the page. *"What Elara does not do in this moment: raise her voice"* stays.
- **Effect analysis.** *"This is the beat that does the most damage and it contains no misfortune at all"* is a statement about what the beat achieves, not an order to the drafter. It stays.

The test, applied to any sentence in a beat body: **could this be transcribed, more or less as written, into narration?** If yes, it belongs. If it could only be obeyed, it belongs somewhere else.

**Format:** Present tense throughout. Each beat is a paragraph (or a few sentences) describing what happens in that segment of the chapter and why it matters.

**Analytical, not prose:** A beat draft is a blueprint, not a draft of the actual text. The goal is to think through the chapter's structure and meaning so that the prose draft can do the work of feeling. Write clearly about what's happening and what it communicates — but don't write the scene itself.

**Dialogue:** Describe conversations rather than scripting them. "Xion and Melina discuss the state of the Coterie — she's exhausted, he's frustrated, neither has answers" is the right register. Specific lines can be noted if a particular phrase must land, but the beat draft is not the place to write dialogue.

**Texture and specificity:** Some beats will name a specific detail — a location, a gesture, a character choice — when that detail does real narrative work. Include it when it matters; don't pad when it doesn't.

**Negative choices:** It's often worth noting what a character deliberately does NOT do in a scene, and why. These absences are frequently as revealing as actions. They belong in the body — a refusal is a thing that happens. Do not confuse them with instructions to the drafter, which do not.

**Beat count:** There's no required number. Chapters in this series typically run 12–20 beats. The right number is however many it takes to map the chapter without padding or skipping.

**Footer notes:** After the beats, add bold-header notes for anything worth flagging explicitly — structural function, character attachment work, what must land for a later chapter to pay off, and **every guardrail and correction note the beats would otherwise have swallowed.** Not every chapter needs them for the first three; most chapters need them for the last. House measurement is 400–750 words of footer written as prose paragraphs, and a draft carrying a lot of accumulated corrections will run past it. That is acceptable. Deleting a guardrail to hit a word count is not.
