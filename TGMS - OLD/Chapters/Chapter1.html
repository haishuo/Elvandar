Chapter 1: The Healer’s Hands

The morning heat pressed down on Xion Kemvimore’s shoulders as he carefully measured dried herbroot into a small ceramic bowl. He’d arrived before dawn to prepare the day’s remedies, grinding seeds and mixing tinctures in the cramped back room of Mistress Thera’s weaving shop. The space wasn’t much—a rickety table, shelves lined with jars of varying sizes, and a narrow window that let in just enough light to work by—but it served his purpose. Here, in this small clinic hidden behind bolts of fabric and spindles of thread, he could escape being the heir to House Kemvimore, if only for a few hours.

“Master Fen? Are you there?” A voice called from the front room, using the name he’d adopted for these excursions.

Xion placed the bowl beside four others, each containing a different remedy. He brushed herb dust from his simple brown tunic—nothing like the fine clothes that hung in his wardrobe back at the Kemvimore estate—and pushed aside the curtain that separated his workspace from the small waiting area.

Five people sat on worn benches along the walls. An old man with a persistent cough who’d been coming weekly for a month. A young mother cradling an infant with a rash spreading across its plump cheeks. Two laborers with what looked like minor injuries—probably from the construction site in the merchant quarter. And finally, a thin boy clutching his right hand, wrapped in a makeshift bandage already soaked through with blood.

The boy’s face was pinched with pain, yet he sat quietly, eyes downcast, trying not to draw attention. That alone told Xion everything he needed to know about the child’s life.

“You first,” Xion said, gesturing to the boy.

The boy looked up, surprise flitting across his face. He’d clearly expected to wait while those with coin or status were treated first. “But I—I can’t pay much, sir.”

“Today’s treatments are free,” Xion replied with a smile that felt both genuine and fraudulent. Free, because he subsidized this clinic with funds skimmed from his own allowance. Funds that ultimately came from the very system forcing this child and countless others to scavenge for survival.

The boy hesitated, then rose and followed Xion back into the treatment room. Up close, Xion could see the grime embedded in the creases of the boy’s neck, the way his tunic hung from bony shoulders.

“What’s your name?” Xion asked, gesturing for the boy to sit on a stool.

“Tam.” The boy’s voice was barely audible.

“Let me see your hand, Tam.”

Gently, Xion unwrapped the filthy cloth. The cut was deep, running diagonally across the palm from thumb to little finger. The edges were jagged, and bits of something glinted in the wound.

“Glass,” Xion murmured. “What happened?”

Tam’s eyes remained fixed on the floor. “Was looking for scraps in the refuse behind the Silver Rose tavern. Found a broken bottle, didn’t see it proper in the dark.”

The irony wasn’t lost on Xion. His father, Rosik Kemvimore, Lord of the Grain, controlled every morsel of food that entered Kaha’an. Meanwhile, children like Tam cut themselves on broken glass while searching for discarded crusts.

“This might sting,” Xion warned, reaching for a bottle of clear liquid.

He worked methodically, cleaning the wound with an alcohol solution, using tweezers to extract glass fragments, then preparing a needle and thread. Tam didn’t cry out, though his free hand gripped the edge of the stool until his knuckles turned white.

“You’re brave,” Xion observed, carefully placing each stitch.

Tam shrugged one thin shoulder. “Can’t work if I cry.”

The statement, delivered with such matter-of-fact resignation, made something twist in Xion’s chest. While he spent his days playing at being a healer, children like Tam couldn’t afford the luxury of pain.

“How old are you, Tam?”

“Twelve, I think. Maybe thirteen.”

The same age Xion had been when his healing mentor was executed. Another memory he pushed aside daily.

“There,” Xion said, tying off the final stitch. “I’m going to apply a salve and wrap it properly. When can you come back to have the stitches removed?”

Tam’s expression grew anxious. “Come back? How soon can I work again? My mother—we need—”

“Three days,” Xion interrupted, more firmly than he intended. “No work for three days, or the wound will reopen and might fester. After that, light duties only, and keep it clean. Come back in ten days for the stitches.”

Fear flashed across Tam’s face. “Three days with no coin, sir? We can’t—”

Xion reached into his pocket and pressed a silver piece into Tam’s good hand. A week’s wages for a laborer, a fortune to a boy like this.

“For the lost work,” he said quietly. “Our secret.”

Tam stared at the coin, then quickly tucked it into his ragged shoe. “Thank you, Master Fen,” he whispered, a shadow of suspicion crossing his features. In Kaha’an, generosity always came with hidden costs.

After applying salve and wrapping Tam’s hand, Xion sent him on his way and attended to his remaining patients. The old man’s cough was improving with the herbal mixture Xion had provided last week. The infant’s rash responded well to a gentle cream of aloe and lavender. The laborers had simple injuries that required little more than cleaning and bandaging.

None could pay more than a few copper pieces, which Xion accepted with practiced reluctance—maintaining the fiction that Master Fen was a struggling healer who occasionally offered charity, not the heir to one of Kaha’an’s four ruling cartels.

After the last patient left, Xion began tidying his supplies, lost in thought. The silver piece he’d given Tam represented nothing to him—pocket change, a trivial expenditure. To Tam, it meant three days of survival. Such was the grotesque imbalance of Kaha’an.

“Playing the benevolent healer again, I see.”

Xion didn’t need to turn to recognize the cultured voice of Silvanno Larannas. He continued arranging his herbs. “I didn’t hear you come in.”

“That was rather the point.” Silvanno leaned against the doorframe, his blue silk tunic a stark contrast to the humble surroundings. As the son and heir of Tania Larannas, leader of the Water cartel, Silvanno’s presence in this district would raise eyebrows—if anyone recognized him. Like Xion, he’d learned the art of blending in when necessary.

“I enjoy helping people,” Xion said, corking a bottle of tincture. “Is that so difficult to understand?”

Silvanno’s lips quirked. “Not at all. I enjoy it myself, when the opportunity arises. I simply prefer methods with broader impact than bandaging one cut hand at a time.”

There was no malice in his words, only the gentle prodding of an old debate between them. Silvanno had always been the more politically minded of their small group, focused on systemic solutions rather than individual aid.

“The others are waiting,” Silvanno added. “Farleen’s already growing impatient.”

Xion’s heart quickened at her name, a reaction he immediately chastised himself for. Farleen Darfi, from a lesser family aligned with the Water cartel, was as passionate about reform as any of them—and increasingly, the object of Xion’s less political thoughts.

“Let me just finish here,” he said, placing the last of his supplies on the shelf.

Ten minutes later, they were walking through Kaha’an’s middle district, navigating the narrow streets that separated the grandiose nobility of the upper city from the desperate squalor of the lower. Here, merchants and craftspeople maintained a precarious existence—not wealthy enough for luxury, not poor enough for complete desperation. The harsh desert sun beat down mercilessly, though the streets were designed with overhanging balconies and awnings that provided strips of blessed shade.

“My mother’s making another play for the Ruby Throne,” Silvanno said quietly as they walked. “Nothing overt, just positioning. Meetings with key nobles, subtle reminders of how the Water cartel saved the city after The Rending.”

Xion nodded. It was an old dance. For twenty years, the four cartels—Grain, Water, Iron, and Slavers—had maintained a delicate balance of power, each preventing the others from claiming the empty imperial throne. His own father, Rosik, had similar ambitions, though he pursued them with characteristic cold calculation rather than Tania Larannas’s diplomatic maneuvering.

“My father would restrict her well access if she moved openly,” Xion observed.

Silvanno chuckled without humor. “And my mother would redirect the aqueducts away from your father’s warehouses. The same deadlock as always.”

They reached The Amber Leaf, a teahouse favored by merchants and minor officials—neutral ground where their meeting wouldn’t attract undue attention. The proprietor, a shrewd woman who recognized them despite their plain clothing, led them to a secluded alcove without comment. She knew better than to refuse the heirs to the Grain and Water cartels, regardless of their attire.

Farleen and Kael were already seated, heads bent in conversation. Farleen looked up as they approached, her amber eyes brightening. Xion felt that now-familiar flutter in his chest.

“Finally,” she said, though her smile took any sting from the word. “We were about to start without you.”

“My fault,” Xion admitted, sliding onto the bench beside Kael. “The clinic was busy today.”

Kael Issamil nodded in greeting, his expression characteristically reserved. From a minor Iron clan, Kael was the cautious counterweight to Farleen’s passion, always considering angles and consequences before committing to action.

“Three families from my district,” Farleen said immediately, leaning forward. “All taken by the Slavers this morning for failure to pay water taxes. Not criminals, not debtors—just families who fell behind on payments during the dry season.”

The news landed like a stone in Xion’s stomach. This was becoming more common—the Slavers cartel extending beyond their traditional hunting grounds in the Warrens, now collecting debts for the other cartels and converting minor infractions into opportunities for enslavement.

“The Slavers are becoming bolder,” Silvanno observed, accepting a cup of steaming tea from the server who briefly appeared and then discreetly withdrew. “My mother’s lieutenants are increasingly using them to enforce collection.”

“The Iron clans too,” Kael added. “It’s efficient. The other cartels maintain clean hands while the Slavers do the dirty work.”

Farleen’s mouth tightened in frustration. “And we continue our little charity projects while the Slavers take entire families. What difference are we really making? A few meals distributed here, a free clinic there—” She glanced apologetically at Xion. “Sorry, but you know what I mean.”

Xion did know. For nearly a year, their small group had been doing what they could to ease suffering in Kaha’an—each in their own way, each risking their families’ disapproval. But lately, it had begun to feel like bailing water from a sinking ship with a thimble.

“Perhaps we need to think differently,” Xion said slowly. An idea had been forming in his mind for weeks, one he’d hesitated to share. “What if there was another way? Not just treating the symptoms of Kaha’an’s disease, but actually curing it?”

The others looked at him expectantly.

“The cartels maintain power because there’s no legitimate alternative,” he continued. “After The Rending, when the emperor and empress were lost, the Ruby Throne remained empty because no single cartel could claim it without the others opposing them. But what if there was someone with a more legitimate claim than any cartel?”

Kael’s eyebrows rose. “You’re talking about restoring the imperial line? The entire Valanar family died in The Rending.”

“Did they?” Xion leaned closer, lowering his voice. “I’ve heard rumors—reliable ones—that a child of the imperial family survived. The princess was an infant during The Rending, safely within the city walls. She would be about our age now.”

Silence fell over the table. The idea was explosive—the existence of a legitimate imperial heir would upend the entire power structure of Kaha’an.

“That’s… significant if true,” Silvanno said carefully. “But ‘rumors’ aren’t exactly solid ground for action.”

“Who’s protecting her?” Farleen asked, immediately cutting to the practical question. “She couldn’t have survived this long without powerful allies, especially as a child.”

“The Arol Batae,” Xion replied. “The former imperial guard. They went into seclusion after The Rending, supposedly out of loyalty to the lost emperor. But what if their withdrawal was actually to protect his heir?”

Kael frowned. “The Arol Batae compound in the Noble District? They’re practically hermits. Barely interact with the outside world except for essential supplies.”

“Exactly,” Xion said. “Perfect circumstances for hiding a princess.”

A commotion from the front of the teahouse interrupted their conversation. The proprietor was speaking with three city guards in the distinctive bronze-trimmed uniforms of the Noble District patrol.

“Security check,” one guard announced loudly. “Everyone remain seated while we verify identities.”

Silvanno muttered a curse. “Noble District patrols don’t usually come this far into the middle district.”

“Someone’s watching the borders more carefully,” Kael observed. “Interesting timing.”

Farleen shot Xion a pointed look. “Very interesting, given what we’re discussing.”

They quickly shifted postures, adopting the air of bored noble youth slumming in the middle district for exotic tea—a common enough occurrence to avoid suspicion. Silvanno tilted his head with practiced aristocratic disdain, while Farleen giggled at nothing as if slightly intoxicated.

One guard approached their alcove, hand resting casually on the hilt of his sword. His eyes narrowed as he took in their deliberately shabby clothing, clearly noting the quality of the fabric beneath the artificial wear.

“Identifications,” he demanded.

Silvanno looked up, allowing his hood to fall back slightly. The guard’s eyes widened in recognition.

“Lord Larannas,” he said, straightening immediately. His gaze shifted to Xion, recognition dawning there as well. “And Lord Kemvimore. I… apologize for the intrusion.”

“No intrusion at all,” Silvanno replied smoothly. “Simply enjoying some authentic middle district tea with friends. I find the Noble District blends terribly bland, don’t you?”

The guard bowed slightly. “Of course, my lord. Please continue your… tea tasting.” He backed away, signaling to his companions to leave the alcove alone.

After the guards departed, Kael let out a slow breath. “That was closer than I’d like.”

“They recognized you both immediately,” Farleen noted. “Our disguises need work.”

“Or perhaps we need better allies,” Xion suggested, returning to their interrupted discussion. “If the princess exists, and if she could be convinced to claim the Ruby Throne with our support—”

“That’s a lot of ‘ifs,’” Kael interrupted. “We need more information before committing to anything so radical.”

Silvanno nodded. “Agreed. Where did you hear these rumors, Xion?”

“Various sources, but they all point to the same conclusion. And I believe we can get confirmation.” Xion hesitated, then continued. “There’s an information broker named Arlo Berakailen. They say he knows every secret in Kaha’an.”

“The Shadow Broker?” Farleen’s eyes widened. “I thought he was a myth.”

“Very real,” Kael said. “And very expensive.”

“I’m willing to pay,” Xion said. “This could change everything.”

A thoughtful silence fell over the group. The weight of what they were considering—potentially undermining their own families’ power—wasn’t lost on any of them.

“We should meet again next week,” Silvanno finally said. “After we’ve had time to consider this properly. And perhaps gather any information we can through our usual channels.”

They agreed to depart separately to avoid drawing further attention. Kael left first, then Silvanno, both taking different routes back toward the Noble District.

Outside the teahouse, Farleen caught Xion’s arm as he prepared to leave. The evening air had cooled slightly, bringing relief from the day’s relentless heat. Lanterns were being lit along the street, casting pools of golden light that caught in Farleen’s dark hair.

“Be careful, Xion,” she said, her voice lower than usual. “This isn’t distributing food or treating cuts. If the cartels learn we’re seeking the princess—if she even exists—”

“I know the risks,” he assured her, though he wasn’t entirely sure he did. The full implications of what he’d proposed were still unfolding in his mind.

Farleen’s gaze held his for a long moment. Then, with a quickness that surprised him, she leaned forward and pressed her lips briefly against his cheek.

“Just make sure you do,” she whispered, then turned and walked away, disappearing into the deepening shadows.

Xion stood motionless, fingers touching the spot where her lips had been. The kiss had been brief, probably meaningless—a gesture between friends—yet his heart raced as if he’d run the length of the Noble District.

As he finally turned toward home, Xion found his thoughts returning to Tam and his injured hand. As a healer, Xion knew the difference between treating symptoms and curing disease. The free clinic, the secret charity—these were bandages on wounds that would never heal while the current system remained.

The princess, if she existed, might be the cure Kaha’an needed. Or she might be another symptom of a deeper affliction. Either way, Xion was committed to finding out, whatever the cost.

The winding streets of Kaha’an stretched before him, a testament to the city’s fragmented nature—noble mansions looming above cramped middle district homes, which in turn overlooked the desperate Warrens below. Somewhere in this layered metropolis, perhaps a princess waited, unaware that her very existence might rewrite the future of a city defined by greed.
