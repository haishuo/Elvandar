Chapter 14 · Chapter 19 · Chapter 13

Rule Zero did not exist, and you can watch the book discover it

The rule the whole series now runs on — the camera stays on Xion's shoulder; what he attends to is what the reader attends to — was written down this month. The old draft had never heard of it, and broke it three times in twenty chapters.

Chapter 14 is a Commander Vesk chapter. Chapter 19 leaves Xion for Elara and switches into present tense to do it. Chapter 13 stays nominally with Xion and then reaches into Elara's head anyway, explaining that her royal upbringing had never prepared her for the Warrens.

The finished book has none. Twenty-five chapters, one consciousness, and two deliberate widenings of the lens, both signed off as exceptions: the last two pages, when the bells go up tower by tower to the harbour — and the last line of chapter 12, “Somewhere far below all of it, a twelve-year-old boy was working in the dark.” An earlier draft of this page claimed there was only one, which was wrong on the day it was written.

Old draft, chapter 14, first line:
“Commander Vesk paced the length of the Arol Batae's inner ward, his shadow stretching and compressing with each turn.”

“He was not pacing. Xion had expected pacing and got instead a man standing perfectly still in the middle of a swept expanse of stone.”

The book, chapter 23 — same man, same courtyard, from behind Xion's shoulder

Nobody planned that. The canon line was written to characterise Vesk, not to answer a manuscript nobody was going to read again. But it is the whole argument in two sentences: the old draft told you what Vesk was doing, and the book tells you what Xion found when he got there.

11 of 20 → 5 of 25

Eleven chapters opened on the weather

Dawn crept, dawn broke, morning light filtered, the dusk painted Kaha'an in hues of amber. More than half the old draft began by describing the time of day to a reader who had not yet been given anyone to be.

The book does it five times in twenty-five — chapters 6, 12, 15 and 23, plus chapter 8, where the subject is a fever that broke in the night rather than the night itself. A sixth, chapter 17, uses the morning to say that this one sounded exactly like the last one and nothing else about the day would, which is the construction doing work instead of filling space.

An earlier draft of this page said four. It was counting chapter 8 twice over, in the sense of counting it as the fever and not as a fifth.

Chapter 1, closing paragraph

The ending problem, in its original form

The audit of all twenty-five chapter endings turned up thirteen that needed cutting, and a fourteenth surfaced later — chapter 22 was still walking its own ending back nine lines after it landed. This is where the habit came from.

Old draft ends chapter 1

“The princess, if she existed, might be the cure Kaha'an needed. Or she might be another symptom of a deeper affliction. Either way, Xion was committed to finding out, whatever the cost.

The winding streets of Kaha'an stretched before him… Somewhere in this layered metropolis, perhaps a princess waited, unaware that her very existence might rewrite the future.”

The book ends chapter 1

“Two impossible things in one afternoon…

Tomorrow, he would act.”

The old version leaves Xion entirely for its last sentence and forecasts, on the narrator's authority, about a woman who has not yet appeared. It is the purest specimen of the thing the ending pass was built to hunt, and it is sitting in the first chapter.

Tam Corris — 1 chapter of 20

The boy is never taken

Tam exists in the old draft. He comes in with a cut hand, he doesn't cry because he can't work if he cries, Xion stitches him — and then Xion presses a silver piece into his good hand, a week's wages, and sends him home, and the manuscript never mentions him again across nineteen further chapters.

The narrator explains the scene three times on the way out. While he spent his days playing at being a healer, children like Tam couldn't afford the luxury of pain. The silver piece represented nothing to him. Such was the grotesque imbalance of Kaha'an. These were bandages on wounds that would not close.

What Xion does about it

Reaches into his pocket. The problem is money, he has money, and the scene resolves.

Then the narration tells you it was ironic.

What Xion does about it

Nothing. Two enforcers invent a debt in an open square and take the boy, and acting would mean saying his own name aloud, and he cannot.

Nobody explains anything. Nine chapters later he is carrying the same child up out of the dark with a fever in his blood.

This is the single largest difference between the two manuscripts, and it is the book's whole argument. The old draft has a rich man solve a poor boy's problem with pocket change. The book has a man who cannot help because helping requires a name he has spent eight years hiding — and then spends its entire middle act proving that the only thing that ever worked for him was the unglamorous work he did for no reason at all.

Xion never rescues Tam. Korvin does, and a food vendor does, and a Slaver's cousin does, and a guard named Varris does, because Master Fen patched them up once and never asked what they did for a living. None of that exists in the old draft, because the boy walks out of chapter one with a coin and is not thought of again.

“Mistress Thera” — 1 mention, ever

Janice does not exist either

The weaver whose back room hides the clinic is named once in the old draft, in a subordinate clause, and never appears again. The copper argument, the coded ledgers, the eight years of cover, the woman who told a fifteen-year-old he could not keep his own name and who later burned the patient records rather than let them be read — all of it is invention.

And the division inside that scene is exact, which is the thing worth noticing. Janice supplies the instruction: you can't keep that name, not if you want to keep doing this. Xion supplies the name — Fen. Master Fen. Told he has to stop being himself, he reaches for the surname of the woman his father had executed, and wears it for eight years. She gave him the cover. Mira gave him everything the cover was protecting.

Which makes her arc across this year's work a three-stage one, and the middle stage was a mistake worth recording: absent in the old draft, then over-weighted in the rewrite — 275 words of her dialogue against Xion's 90, in the opening scene of the book he is named after, a narrator-surrogate standing in the protagonist's light — and now sound before sight, a needle that stops and starts behind him while his hands are busy, and one look he never asks about.

Iron cartel, chapter 16 → chapter 24

The Iron Lady used to be won with a speech

Old draft

Sa Ko Ren pours a dark liquid from a simple carafe and asks the princess for her vision of governance. Elara delivers it — artificial scarcity, exploitation, lungs and limbs — and Sa Ko says “You've studied us well.”

Xion is in the room. He has nothing to do in it.

The book

Sa Ko waves the bloodline and the divine sanction away in six lines — a pretty mirror proves the gods like you, it doesn't prove you can hold a throne — and offers single combat instead.

Xion cannot follow the fight. So he watches the only thing he has ever known how to watch, and is the first person in the hall to work out that she isn't fighting back.

The old scene is competent and the new one is a different order of thing, for a reason that has nothing to do with sentences: the book asks what this specific man can perceive in a room, and then refuses to give the reader anything else. He is a healer, so he reads a rib strike that should have cost her something and did not. The chapter's discovery belongs to him because it is the only discovery he is equipped to make.

Dynamic range 1.9× → 3.1×

The book learned to change speed

This turned out to be the year's most useful discovery and the one that saved the most good material. Three separate proposals to cut “talky” chapters were made and withdrawn once the length profile was checked — including two attempts on chapter 8, which sits dead centre of its own movement and is the last quiet chapter before the disaster in chapter 10.

“This is talky” is not a finding. It is now written into the rulebook that way.

And then chapter 8 was rewritten anyway, which is the part worth having. Withdrawing a cut is not the same as concluding a chapter is finished. The reviewer's case had been that it duplicated chapter 6 — walk, witness, explain, vow — and that framing was simply wrong: the two do different jobs, chapter 6 makes Elara the signatory and chapter 8 carries the injustice specific to a desert city. What was actually wrong with it was delivery. It walked past three fountains and put a statistic in Xion's mouth at each one.

It now has an arched gate with a ledger at it, a man ahead of them written down and turned away, and Xion saying “Kemvimore, and a guest” to save himself a walk — and the guard's hand never going near the book. Elara watches, and convicts him for it. One fountain, no statistics. Children in the basin, unexplained. 1,906 → 1,214 words, which makes it the shortest chapter in the novel and moves the whole dynamic range from 2.6× to 3.1×.

So the rhythm rule saved the chapter and did not settle it. Both of those are the finding.

Two sessions, run in parallel

What actually changed, including the wrong turns

The retrospective was the fun part. The work was elsewhere — and, it turned out, in two places at once. This page originally described only one of them.

Two lines of editing ran against Book 1 over the same forty-eight hours without either seeing the other, and were merged on 2026-07-29. One did the passes below. The other rewrote four chapters outright. Both are in the book now, and the table has been extended to say so.

Things I got wrong

Two structural cuts proposed and both wrong in the same direction, which is what produced the rhythm rule. An alibi accidentally written into Xion's ch1 ending that converted a freeze into a constraint — the exact thing his character cannot be given. A ribs scene read as a diagnosis when it was built as a romance that gets ruined. A whole Warrens economy read as a ledger when the point is that only Xion keeps ledgers, and everyone else is just frightened and helping anyway.

And, at the very end, reporting an open question about Janice's eyesight that had been settled and written into Book 3 — the precise failure the session had spent all day correcting in other people's flags, committed by the person correcting them. (The eyesight has since been vetoed outright and removed from the series. Janice's sight is fine.)

And two more, found afterward

The beat draft for chapter 22 contradicted itself. Beat 25 planned the nine lines that walked the chapter's ending back, and called them “the repair, such as it is… End there.” Four paragraphs below, its own footer said the absolution failing to land “is the real ending and must not be fixed.” The footer was right. This is the retro-draft failure mode in its purest recorded form — a draft written by summarising finished prose inherits the prose's flaw and launders it into the plan, and the blind read exists to catch exactly that and here did not.

And this page. It shipped with a dynamic range that was wrong by twenty per cent, a chapter-length floor that belonged to a chapter that had since been rewritten, a count of one deliberate lens-widening where there are two, and a “what actually changed” table missing four rewritten chapters. A document that looks authoritative and is quietly out of date is the same problem as a craft flag that outlives its problem, which is the thing this page spends a whole section congratulating itself for catching.

Verdict

What the old draft was actually good at

It covered more ground. Twenty chapters reach the coronation by chapter 17 and then keep going — three further chapters of aftermath, a Remembrance ceremony, a cartel regrouping. That material is not lost; it became Book 2, and Book 2 is the better half of the series.

And it knew what the story was about. The imbalance, the manufactured scarcity, the healer who is also the son of the man causing the wounds — all of that is on the first page of the old chapter one. What it did not yet know was that a book has to be somebody. It kept stepping back to explain what it had just shown, which is a form of not trusting the reader, and it kept leaving Xion to go and look at something more interesting.

Nothing more interesting than Xion was ever found. That is the whole finding, and it took twenty chapters and seventy-one thousand words to establish it, which is exactly what a discarded draft is for.
