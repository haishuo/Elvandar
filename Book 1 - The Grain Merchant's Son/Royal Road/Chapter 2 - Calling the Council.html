He wrote the notes before the light came up. Four of them, identical, on parchment torn small, and four runners who did not know about one another. Urgent. Meet today. Something has happened.

Then a fifth, which took longer than the other four together, and which went no further than the steward's tray at the foot of the stairs. It said he was unwell and would not be at the table, and it was the only one of the five that was a lie, and he read it over twice before he left it.

In five years he had never called them together. They called him — came to the clinic, stood in the doorway until he put something down, walked him to the Amber Leaf whether he had the afternoon or not. He thought about that on the way and it did not slow him down.

His hands were not steady. He had not slept, and every time he had shut his eyes it had been there again, and it had been the eyes and never the boy. Blue, then amber, then violet, in a light that was going.

The Amber Leaf at that hour held four merchants and a girl sweeping between the tables. He took the corner booth and laid his hands flat on the wood until they stopped.

Silvanno came first, the way he always did, dropping into the seat opposite with his coat half off.

"Well. This is new." Then he got a proper look at Xion's face, and the rest of it went out of his own. "What's happened?"

"Wait for Farleen."

"Xion—"

"Wait for Farleen."

Kael arrived a few minutes later, which for Kael was late, and did not take his coat off at all. "Your message said nothing."

"I know."

"Then say something."

"Let's wait for—"

"I'm here." Farleen came through from the front room, and Xion's chest did the thing it always did. She slid onto the bench beside him. "Sorry. My father had guests this morning. Unexpected ones."

Xion put both hands round his cup and did not drink out of it.

"All right," he said, and then nothing came, because he had spent the whole walk choosing how to start and every version of it had sounded like a man asking for a physician.

"Start at the beginning," Farleen said.

"I saw her."

Three faces failed to change in three different ways.

"Her," he said. "The heir. I know what it sounds like. I've been telling myself what it sounds like since about the fourth hour of last night." He set the cup down. "There was a boy taken in the market square yesterday evening. Tam. The Slavers made a debt out of nothing and took him for it, and I was there, and there was a woman at the edge of the crowd who watched them do it. Her eyes changed. Blue to amber to violet, and then again, and then again after that."

Nobody said anything.

"It's the trait. It doesn't come out of a jar and it doesn't wipe off." He leaned in over the table. "Do you understand what that would mean? Not one more family fed. Not one more winter of deciding which street gets the medicine. The whole rotten—"

He stopped, because none of them had moved.

Farleen put her hand over his on the table. "Xion. You watched a child taken yesterday."

"Not another one." Silvanno said it tired rather than unkind, which was worse. "The last Princess Elara had coloured glass in her eyes. It fooled nine people for about a week, and one of them was a magistrate, and he is still hearing about it."

"Six that we know of, in five years," Kael said. "And a man who has had a night like that sees things. That isn't an insult. It's only what people are."

"That's exactly why," Xion said.

Kael's fingers stopped on the table. "Why what?"

"Why I'm not letting go of it." He turned the cup a half circle and left it. "I sat up trying to work out how to get that boy back. There isn't a how. I don't know where he is. I don't know one person I could ask who wouldn't cost me more than the asking is worth. He's gone, and I did that, and it's done." He looked up. "This one isn't done."

"And when you find her," Kael said, "you'll ask her about the boy."

Xion opened his mouth.

"You will," Kael said.

"She might know something." Nobody said anything at all. Xion set the cup down. "All right. No. She wouldn't. But look at me and tell me you wouldn't ask."

Silvanno rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. "You want it too much, is all. That's all I'm saying to you."

"I know what I saw." It came out of him louder than the room allowed. Two of the merchants looked over, and the girl with the broom stopped, and Xion held up a hand to her and lowered his voice. "Sorry. Not at you."

"You can shout at me. I've had worse off you." Silvanno spread his hands on the table, and when he went on he was careful with it, which was the thing Xion had been dreading. "But think about where you were standing. A boy you'd stitched that morning, being dragged off in front of you, and nothing you could do about it. Light going. Forty people in the way. If a man's mind was ever going to hand him something he needed, that's the hour it would pick."

"Nobody's saying you're lying," Kael said.

"No," Silvanno agreed. "I'm saying you might have filled in a piece that wasn't there, and never known you did it."

Xion sat with that.

"I know," he said. "I know that's what I'm supposed to think. I spent most of last night trying to talk myself into it. Why do you imagine I look like this."

"Or she might not have been there at all," Silvanno said.

"Or she might not have been there at all." Xion said it evenly, and it took something to do it. "Fine. It was dark, the light was going, and I want it to be true. All of that has been put to me already, by me, most of the night." He laid his hands flat. "So say you're right. Say I've got to where I see what I need to see. That isn't something I get to leave alone either, is it. Either she's out there or there's something wrong with me, and I would like to know which one I'm living with."

Kael started drumming again, four fingers, in order.

"Suppose you're certain," he said.

"Which we're not," Silvanno said.

"Which we're not. Suppose it anyway. What changes? If she's been kept out of sight this long, there are people who arranged that, and those people don't stop existing because you've had a look at her."

"The historians at Uratha wrote the trait down four hundred years ago," Xion said. "It runs in the Valanar blood and it isn't a story—"

"I'm not arguing about her eyes." Kael's voice did not rise. "I'm asking what you would do on the day after you found her."

That was the rub, and Xion had known it would be, and he had nothing for it that was worth the parchment he had torn.

"I'll save you the rest," he said. "What if she isn't what you're imagining. What if she won't help. I can't answer either of those and I'm not going to pretend to." He looked round at them. "But how long have we been at this, Sil? Five years. Say it out loud and hear it. Five years, and what do we have — a few families going to bed less hungry than they'd have been, and some people who got medicine instead of dying in an alley. That's not nothing. I'll take it to my grave doing it. But we are bailing a ship with a cup and we all know it, and if I'm wrong here it costs us a few days and we go back to the cup."

Silvanno was looking at his own hands.

"Five years," he said. "Those people deserve better than what we give them. They deserve something that doesn't grind them up to start with." He looked up. "Damn you for being right about that part."

Kael's jaw set. "It isn't nothing, what we do."

"I didn't say it was nothing. I said it wasn't enough, and you've thought so longer than I have."

Farleen leaned forward.

"Even if she's real," she said. "I'm not saying she is. But if she is — she's been hidden twenty years, and somebody has been doing the hiding, and they've been doing it well enough that nobody in this city has found her. Those are not gentle people, Xion. That's what I'd want you to think about. Not whether you saw it. What happens to a man who goes looking."

"So we don't get caught looking."

"That isn't—" She stopped, and started again lower. "It's dangerous. That's all. It's dangerous."

He could see that she meant it. It was in her face and in the way she had gone still, and part of him wanted to give her something back, wanted to promise he would be careful, wanted to leave the whole thing on the table and walk out with her into an ordinary morning.

"You're proposing to turn the city over," Kael said, "on the strength of a glance."

"I'm proposing that the woman this city belongs to might be alive. That's all I've said."

"Two days," Xion said, with more certainty in it than he had. "Two days, quietly. I saw her by the market square. We start there."

"Two days," Silvanno repeated, tasting them.

"Quietly," Kael said. "Not carefully. Quietly. If it gets out that four of us are hunting a pretender, we won't be four people with an odd hobby."

"Then quietly."

Farleen was chewing the inside of her lip. When she spoke it was smaller than before. "Where would you even begin? The square is a dozen streets and hundreds of people an hour."

She had asked it as a question. Xion took the breath he had been holding since before dawn.

"We ask," he said. "Carefully. Whether anyone else saw a woman with strange eyes. Whether anyone saw somebody who didn't fit."

Silvanno sat back and studied him for a while.

"You're going to do this whether we come or not."

"Yes."

Nobody argued with it. Kael let out a breath through his nose and stopped drumming. Silvanno shook his head once, at nothing.

Farleen had gone somewhere past Xion's shoulder. Whatever she was looking at was not in the room, and she stayed there long enough that he almost said her name — and then she came back, and there was something in her face he could not put a word to. It was not fear. It was nearer to the look of a woman reading a bill she already knew the total of.

"We'll help you," she said.
