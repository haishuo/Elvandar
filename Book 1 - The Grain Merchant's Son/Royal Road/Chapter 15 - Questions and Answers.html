Morning came too early, announced by the sounds of the Warrens waking—vendors calling out their wares, the clatter of carts on stone, children's voices echoing through narrow streets. Xion had barely slept, his mind churning through everything that had happened, his shoulder a constant dull ache.

Korvin was already awake, preparing a thin porridge over his small stove. He glanced at them as they stirred. "You three look like you could use some food before you venture out."

"We've imposed enough," Xion said, but his stomach betrayed him with an audible growl.

"It's just porridge. Won't set me back." Korvin ladled the watery mixture into bowls. "But I'll be honest with you, Master Fen—whatever you're planning to do today, be careful. Word spreads fast down here, and after last night..."

"After last night what?" Farleen asked, her voice sharp with alertness despite the early hour.

Korvin's good eye moved to Elara, who was already dressed and checking the window. "After three outsiders arrived and one of them put down three armed men in about ten heartbeats, people are going to be curious. Or cautious. Or both."

They ate quickly, the porridge bland but filling. When they were ready to leave, Korvin walked them to the door.

"The Crossroads is maybe twenty minutes from here if you stick to the main paths," he said. "That's where people go when they need to know things—information brokers operate there. It's called that for a reason." He looked at Elara specifically. "But the Warrens aren't like the surface. Don't wander into areas you don't recognize, and if someone tells you to turn back, listen to them. Even if you can handle yourself, there's always someone tougher, or smarter, or more connected."

"We'll be careful," Xion promised.

"See that you are." Korvin flexed his scarred hand one more time. "I'd hate for my debt to you to end with you getting yourself killed down here."

---

The morning Warrens were different from the night version—louder, more chaotic, but somehow less menacing in the daylight filtering through gaps in the ceiling far above. People moved with purpose through the streets, conducting business, hauling goods, living lives that happened entirely outside the notice of the surface world.

But Xion noticed something odd almost immediately.

People were watching them. Not hostile, exactly. Just... watchful. A woman selling vegetables tracked their movement past her stall. A group of men lounging against a wall went quiet as they approached, then resumed talking once they'd passed. Children playing in an alley scattered when they came near, not in fear but in wariness.

"We're being noticed," Farleen murmured.

"Of course we are," Elara said. "We're outsiders."

"No." Farleen's voice was tight. "It's more than that. Look at them."

Xion did, and saw what she meant. The attention wasn't predatory. It was... evaluative. Like people trying to decide something about them.

They turned a corner and nearly walked into a group of four men—big, rough-looking, the kind who made their living through intimidation. The kind who would have seen three outsiders as easy marks.

The lead man's eyes swept over them, lingering on Xion's medical bag, on Farleen's fine clothes beneath the borrowed cloak. Then his gaze found Elara.

Something changed in his expression. Recognition, maybe. Or caution.

"Morning," he said, his tone carefully neutral.

"Morning," Xion replied, confused.

The man stepped aside. His companions followed suit, clearing the path.

They walked past in silence. Xion glanced back once and saw the group watching them go, talking among themselves in low voices.

"That was strange," he said.

"That was impossible." Farleen's voice was low and urgent. "They had numbers. We're obviously outsiders. There's no guard down here to stop them, and they stepped aside."

"Maybe word spread that we're dangerous," Elara offered.

"Dangerous means worth testing. Worth taking down to prove you're tougher." Farleen's hand had drifted to her belt, where she'd hidden a knife, and her eyes hadn't stopped moving since the corner. "This isn't fear of you. It's something else. Like they're waiting for something."

Farleen had gotten them into the Warrens. She knew how to move through hostile ground. If she was confused, they were in deeper water than Xion had realized.

They reached a small marketplace—just a widening in the street where vendors had set up makeshift stalls. The smell of cooking food mixed with less pleasant odors, and the press of humanity made the air thick and close.

Farleen was already scanning the area with tactical precision. "See that corner? Those three men have been watching us since we arrived. Long Knives, probably—they're stationed here to keep track of who comes and goes. If we start asking obvious questions, they'll report it up the chain."

"So what do we do?" Xion asked.

"We look for someone who has less to lose by talking to us." Farleen's eyes swept the marketplace, taking in positions, escape routes, vulnerabilities. Her jaw was tight, but her voice was professional. "That stall. He's positioned at the edge of the market—can see trouble coming from multiple directions. And he's alone, no family nearby to endanger. If we're going to get anything, it'll be from someone like him."

She led them toward a food cart operated by a middle-aged man with scarred hands, her movements confident despite the tension Xion could see in her shoulders.

The man looked up as they approached. His eyes widened slightly as they settled on Xion.

"Master Fen?" he said, keeping his voice low.

Xion blinked. "Yes?"

"Thought that was you." The man glanced around nervously, then gestured them closer to the cart, away from the open market. "You stitched me up after I got caught in some cartel business about six months back. Told me to stay off my feet for a week." He touched his side. "Still hurts when it rains, but I'm alive. That's more than most can say."

"I'm glad you're well," Xion said.

"Well enough." The man's voice dropped further. "What are you doing down here? This isn't your territory."

"We're looking for someone. A boy named Tam, taken by Slavers a few weeks ago."

The vendor's expression tightened. "That's dangerous talk, Master Fen. The Slavers don't like people asking questions about their business."

"I know. But I have to try."

The man was quiet for a long moment, clearly wrestling with something. His eyes kept darting to the three men Farleen had identified, to other watchers in the crowd. When he spoke again, his voice was barely above a whisper.

"I can't be seen helping you. You understand? I've got a wife, kids. If the wrong people think I'm working against them..." He didn't finish the sentence.

"I understand," Xion said. "I won't put you at risk."

The vendor pretended to show them his wares, gesturing at the food on his cart as if they were discussing a purchase. To anyone watching, it would look like normal business.

"The Crossroads," he said quietly. "That's where information gets traded. There's a man there—Arlo Berakailen. Best information broker in the Warrens. If anyone can tell you about one specific kid, it's him." He paused. "But Master Fen... it won't be free. Information down here costs. And Arlo don't take coin."

"What does he take?" Farleen asked, her voice carefully neutral.

"Other information. Favors. Whatever he thinks is worth the trade." The vendor handed Xion a piece of dried meat, as if completing a sale. "That's all I can tell you. And you didn't hear it from me."

"Thank you," Xion said sincerely. "For taking the risk."

"You saved my life. Figure I owe you that much." The man's eyes were hard. "But after this, we're even. You understand? I can't help you again. Can't even acknowledge knowing you if we cross paths."

"I understand."

They moved away from the cart, and Xion noticed the vendor immediately turned his attention to other customers, making a point of not watching them leave.

"That was it?" Elara asked. "That's all we get?"

"That was more than we should have gotten," Farleen said quietly. "Did you see how nervous he was? That man took a real risk talking to us. If the wrong people noticed..." She shook her head, then added with an edge to her voice, "Of course, we wouldn't be in this position if we'd stayed in the Tiers like I suggested."

Xion didn't respond to the barb.

They continued through the marketplace more cautiously now. A woman loading a cart caught Xion's eye and he saw recognition there—but she quickly looked away. Another man, younger, actually started toward them before his companion grabbed his arm and pulled him back.

"They know you," Farleen observed, her tone professionally detached. "But they're afraid."

"Of what?" Xion asked.

"Of being seen helping outsiders. Of drawing attention from the factions. Of making themselves targets." She navigated them around a group of hard-looking men, her movements precise. "Your reputation protects you from being robbed or attacked. But it doesn't make people brave enough to openly help you. Not down here." A pause. "Which makes this whole endeavor that much more difficult. As I predicted."

The Crossroads turned out to be a large open area where five different streets met, creating a natural gathering point. Unlike the cramped quarters of the Tiers, this space had room to breathe—which meant it also had room for commerce, negotiation, and the kind of business that required witnesses.

People clustered in groups, talking, trading, conducting deals that seemed to involve more conversation than actual exchange of goods. In the center of the space, elevated slightly on a makeshift platform, sat a middle-aged man with sharp eyes and an air of comfortable authority.

"That'll be Arlo," Farleen murmured, her voice tight.

But before they could approach, a group of men moved to intercept them. There were six of them, dressed in mismatched leather armor that bore the marks of the Slavers—not official colors, but the kind of gear that marked them as part of that organization's lower ranks.

Xion's heart sank. This was it—the confrontation Farleen had been expecting, just delayed.

The lead man was tall and scarred, his face weathered by years of violence. He looked them over with calculating eyes, and Xion saw him notice Elara specifically, saw recognition flicker across his features.

"Well now," the man said, his voice carrying across the sudden quiet of The Crossroads. "Heard there were some interesting newcomers asking questions about our business."

"We're not looking for trouble," Xion said, keeping his voice steady.

"Course not. Nobody ever is." The Slaver stepped closer. "But trouble finds people anyway, especially when they start poking into things that don't concern them."

The other Slavers had spread out slightly, not quite surrounding them but positioned to cut off easy escape routes. Xion felt Elara tense beside him, and Farleen's hand drifted toward her concealed knife.

Then the lead Slaver's eyes focused on Xion's face, really seeing him for the first time.

"Hang on." The man's aggressive posture shifted, uncertainty creeping in. "You Master Fen?"

Xion hesitated, then nodded. "Yes."

The change was immediate and dramatic. The Slaver's whole demeanor shifted from threatening to... complicated. He glanced at his companions, then back to Xion.

"You stitched up my cousin last month," he said. "Rykan. Idiot got himself cut in a territory dispute, came to your clinic bleeding all over the place. You patched him up, didn't ask for nothing. Didn't even ask what happened."

"I remember," Xion said, though he wasn't entirely sure he did.

"Yeah." The Slaver was quiet for a moment, clearly wrestling with something. Then he made a visible decision and stepped back, gesturing for his companions to do the same. "Look, I ain't seen the kid you're looking for. That's above my pay grade—you'd have to talk to the boss about specifics like that."

"Can you arrange that?" Xion asked.

"Maybe. But..." The man paused, choosing his words carefully. "Here's the thing, Master Fen. We grunts, we don't got much say in who gets taken, where they go. That's all handled higher up. And some of the guys who run the operations, they got... reputations. Even among us."

"What kind of reputations?" Farleen asked.

The Slaver's jaw tightened. "The kind that makes you glad you're just a grunt and not one of the people they take." He looked at Xion directly. "I'm telling you this because Rykan says you saved his life. Says you treated him like he mattered, even though you probably knew what he does for work. That's... that's not common."

He turned to address the watching crowd at The Crossroads. "Alright everyone, show's over. These three are just asking questions. Nobody needs to make this into something bigger."

The crowd didn't exactly disperse, but the tension eased. People went back to their own business, though Xion noticed they were still being watched.

The Slaver turned back to them, his voice lower now. "Listen, Master Fen. I don't know what you're doing down here, but it ain't safe. Not all of us are like me. Not everyone's gonna care that you helped their cousin or their neighbor or whatever." He glanced at Elara. "And after last night, people are talking. About the girl who put down three armed men without breaking a sweat. That's the kind of thing that makes people nervous."

"Or careful," Farleen observed quietly.

"Yeah, that too." The Slaver nodded. "But careful only lasts so long. Eventually someone's gonna decide you're worth the risk, or that taking you down is worth the reputation. You understand?"

"We understand," Xion said. "Thank you. For the warning, and for—"

"Don't thank me." The man's voice was gruff. "I'm just... returning a favor. Rykan's family, and family matters down here. You helped him, so I'm helping you. But after this, we're even."

He and his companions walked away, leaving the three of them standing in The Crossroads with everyone studiously pretending not to watch.

"That," Farleen said quietly, "should not have happened."

"Why not?" Xion asked.

"Because Slavers don't just walk away from potential targets. They don't care about gratitude or favors. They care about profit and power." She was watching the Slavers disappear into the crowd. "That man just passed up an opportunity to either shake us down or turn us over to his bosses. For what? Because you stitched up his cousin?"

"Family matters to people," Xion said. "Even here."

"Maybe." Farleen didn't sound convinced. "But something else is happening. Something we're not seeing." Her jaw tightened. "Which makes this even more dangerous than I thought. And I already thought it was suicidal."

Elara had been quiet through the whole exchange. Now she spoke, her voice tight with something Xion couldn't quite identify.

"You patched up slavers."

It wasn't a question.

"I patch up anyone who comes to my clinic," Xion said carefully.

"These are the people who took Tam." Elara's voice was getting louder. "The people who kidnap children and sell them. And you helped them?"

"I helped injured people," Xion corrected. "I don't ask what they do for work. I don't make moral judgments about who deserves treatment."

"That's not making a moral judgment—that's avoiding one!" Elara's eyes were bright with something fierce. "These people are evil. They destroy lives. And you just... what, stitch them up so they can go back to ruining more lives?"

"I'm a healer," Xion said quietly. "That's what healers do. We treat the sick and injured. All of them."

"Even the ones who don't deserve it?"

"I don't get to decide who deserves healing." Xion met her eyes steadily. "Nobody does. The moment I start making those judgments, the moment I say 'you're too evil to deserve my help,' I stop being a healer and become something else. Something I don't want to be."

"You become someone with principles," Elara shot back.

"No. I become someone who decides the value of human life based on my own moral framework. And that's a power nobody should have."

"So you just help everyone? Slavers, murderers, anyone who walks through your door?"

"Yes." Xion's voice was calm but firm. "Because in my clinic, everyone is equal. The merchant and the thief. The guard and the criminal. They all bleed the same, they all hurt the same, and they all deserve to be treated with the same care."

"That's insane," Elara said flatly.

"That's the job." Xion looked at her, trying to make her understand. "If I start choosing who to help based on whether I approve of them, where does it end? Do I refuse to treat guards because I don't like the city's laws? Do I turn away merchants because I think they charge too much? Do I let someone die because I heard a rumor they did something bad?"

"Those aren't the same thing," Elara argued.

"Aren't they? They're all judgment calls about who deserves help and who doesn't. And the moment I start making those calls, I've betrayed what it means to be a healer."

It was true. He had believed every word of it for eight years and would go on believing it.

But she hadn't asked him whether slavers deserved care. She had said these are the people who took Tam — and he had answered a different question instead, cleanly and well, the way a man reaches for the tool nearest his hand.

He put the thought where he kept the others.

Elara looked like she wanted to argue further, but Farleen interrupted.

"As philosophically fascinating as this is, we're standing in the middle of The Crossroads having a very public disagreement. Can we table this until we're somewhere less exposed?"

She was right. People were definitely watching now, and not bothering to hide it.

"Fine," Elara said tightly. "But this conversation isn't over."

"I didn't expect it to be," Xion replied.

They approached Arlo's platform, where the information broker waited with an expression of amused interest. He was younger than Xion had expected—maybe forty, with clever eyes and the kind of face that gave nothing away.

"Master Fen," Arlo said as they climbed the steps to his platform. "I've been expecting you. The Warrens have been absolutely buzzing about your arrival." His gaze moved to Elara. "And your very impressive companion."

"You know who I am?" Xion asked.

"I make it my business to know everyone who matters in the Warrens. And you, Master Fen, matter quite a bit. Even if you don't realize it." Arlo gestured for them to sit on low stools arranged around his platform. "Now. I understand you're looking for information about a boy. Tam, I believe?"

"Yes. Do you know where he is?"

"I might." Arlo's smile was pleasant and completely unreadable. "But first, we should discuss what that information is worth to you, and what you're willing to trade for it."

Xion felt his heart sink. This was the part he'd been dreading—the negotiation, the price, the inevitable moment when his resources ran up against reality.

"What do you want?" he asked.

"Now that," Arlo said, leaning back with evident satisfaction, "is exactly the right question."
