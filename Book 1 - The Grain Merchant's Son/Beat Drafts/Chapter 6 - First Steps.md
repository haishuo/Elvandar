# Chapter 6: First Steps

**Timeline:** Day 7. Morning to midday, the day after ch5.
**Characters:** Xion (POV), Elara, a Water cartel token-checker, a mother and her small daughter, a woman reading a notice, a boy chasing an orange
**Emotional work:** Elara has spent twenty years being told the cartels are weather. This chapter replaces that with something far worse — they are administration, conducted in writing, under her name, with her seal. The reader must finish it understanding that her problem is not that she has to defeat the cartels. It is that on paper she already *is* them.
**POV:** Xion — close third-person limited. He is the guide, so the chapter's method is that he shows her things and she sees more in them than he ever has.

---

## Beats

1. Morning, out of a rented cooper's back building where they passed the night — the establishing detail is that the man who took the money counted it and asked nothing, which is the kind of arrangement Xion has been maintaining for eight years. His shoulder aches with every step, and the pain reads as distant against the fact that he is walking beside the heir to the Ruby Throne.

2. He gives her fieldcraft — hood up, eyes down until they clear the merchant district — and she notes drily that she is not completely helpless at subterfuge. He is sharper with her than he means to be, and it is stress rather than temper. She asks after the shoulder with something close to guilt, and he softens. Establish early that she feels responsible for the wound she did not cause, because it is the same reflex he has toward Farleen and it makes them legible to each other.

3. Walking the transition between Noble and Middle districts, Xion sees a route he has taken hundreds of times differently because she is beside him — the subtle hierarchies, the careful distances people keep, prosperity fading by degrees. His expertise here is not knowledge but familiarity, and her presence is what makes it visible to him.

4. The public well. He tells her to watch and says nothing else. At first glance it is an ordinary morning; then the structure surfaces. A man in Water cartel colours with a ledger, checking papers. A young woman's token examined before she may draw. An older man turned away entirely because his token is insufficient.

5. Xion explains the mechanism flatly: water rights are hereditary, those families do not qualify at this well, they will walk another half-mile to the next one if they have tokens for that. If they do not, they buy from private sellers at premium or they go without.

6. The detail that does the work is a small girl tugging her mother's skirt and asking for a drink. The mother shakes her head and leads her away without filling the jug. Elara's eyes flash amber before she catches them. No commentary follows.

7. She asks how long this has been policy, and the answer requires Xion to discuss her parents' deaths as a matter of administrative history — since The Rending, a vacuum, the cartels filling it by controlling essentials. He notes how strange the words feel in his mouth. Then the fiction: they rule in her family's name, maintaining order until the imperial line returns. She says it back to him with the part that matters restored — *ruling in my family's name while people die of thirst.*

---

8. He takes her deeper into the Middle District, and it is a public notice board that stops her. Establish it as nothing to look at: a stretch of boarded wall, papered in layers, old proclamations gone soft and grey beneath the new. People pass without turning their heads. Xion has walked past it ten thousand times.

9. He tells her to read the top of them. She reads one, then the second, then the third, and he watches her go still in a way he has not seen before.

10. What is on the wall. Every sheet opens the same way — by order of the Imperial House of Valanar — and beneath that the seal, a good enough copy that nobody in this street would ever have cause to test it. And beneath that, the business: taxation schedules, licensing requirements, amended procedures for the collection of debt, the terms of indenture available to those who cannot satisfy a collection, and the schedule by which such indenture may be sold. The horror is administrative and it is complete.

11. Xion supplies the causal link he has never said aloud: the paper is what puts the men in brown leather on the corner. They do not invent debts out of cruelty; they invent them because the sheet sets a quota they must reach by the quarter, and it is easier to write a debt than to find one. Everything she has watched this morning was authorised, in writing, publicly, and somebody stood exactly here and read the authorisation and went to work.

12. Her response, after a long silence, is the chapter's line and must land verbatim: they have been signing with her name, for twenty years. Then she takes it apart — not invoking, not claiming descent from, not in the tradition of. *Signing. As her.* Every schedule, every quota, every indenture, and the woman standing in front of it has never held a pen over any of it. Her voice goes very level, which Xion is learning is her dangerous register.

13. He offers the obvious consolation — she can repudiate all of it, start fresh — and she closes the trap on him. Or she is the puppet who signed them. What they will say is not *the empress has returned but *the empress has changed her mind. Twenty years of this and her first act is to disown her own signature. There is nothing bitter in it, which is what makes it unanswerable: it is only accurate. She finishes with the question the chapter exists to ask and does not answer — how does she prove she was never here.

14. Xion has no answer. He has known the wall was there for years and has never once thought of it as a thing that had been done to a specific person. This is the chapter's actual subject and it belongs to him as much as to her.

15. They stand with it. A woman comes, reads the second sheet with her lips moving, and goes away with the particular set to the shoulders of someone who has just learned a thing she cannot afford.

16. Then a boy of about eight comes past at a dead run, chasing an orange down the gutter, and catches it, and holds it up in triumph to nobody at all. He is thin enough that his ribs show. He is laughing. Elara watches him go and asks Xion to make sure she remembers it — that it is not only about stopping the bad things, it is about the ones already going on despite them. This is the only warmth in the chapter and it must not be developed or discussed.

17. They walk on and she is different in a way Xion could not have written down: fewer questions and better ones, and she notices things he has trained himself to overlook — people stepping aside before a cartel man is close enough to require it, the careful neutrality of shopkeepers' faces, children who watch from doorways and never come near a uniform. By midday she looks older than her twenty years, and none of it came from the wells or the enforcers. It came from a wall of paper with her name at the bottom.

---

18. A temple courtyard, resting. He says she needs time to process; she asks whether she has time, since every moment she delays, more people suffer under laws signed with her name. He argues for preparation over speed. She watches pigeons, and when she speaks again there is a new hardness: she is beginning to understand what she is up against, and it is not corruption or incompetence but deliberate cruelty systematically applied. He asks what she plans to do. Her answer is one word, flat and final: everything.

19. Leaving the courtyard, Xion catches himself stealing glances at her profile. The woman who came out of the compound yesterday was uncertainty wrapped in royal authority. The woman walking beside him now carries something harder.

---

**On the chapter's single idea, and why the wells are only a runway:** the first third does what the reader expects — a guide shows a sheltered heir a visible injustice and she is appropriately angry. That is a scene the book could have run at any point and it is deliberately the *weaker* half. The notice board is the chapter, and the reason it lands is that it converts the cartels from antagonists into **administrators acting under her authority.** An enemy can be defeated. A signature cannot be un-signed. Her final question — how does she prove she was never here — is the strategic problem of the entire ascension plot, arrived at by a twenty-year-old reading a wall.

**On what this costs Xion, which the chapter should not underline:** he has passed that board ten thousand times. He is the character defined by noticing things — the shape of a crowd, a face going from surprise to confirmation, seven stitches under a bandage — and he has walked past the mechanism of the whole system every week for eight years without once reading it as a document with a victim. His answer to her, that he never thought of it as a thing done to a specific person, is the most damning line he speaks in Act II and nobody remarks on it.

**On the orange, which is the chapter's rule 3 protection:** ch6 is built for one feeling and the feeling is *administrative dread*. The boy is the only warmth in it and his entire function is that Elara chooses to notice him — she is not being consoled, she is issuing herself an instruction about what to hold on to. It works because it is brief, unearned, and immediately dropped. Any expansion of it, any answering line from Xion, any callback later in the chapter, converts a survival instinct into a moral and kills it.

**On the temple courtyard, which should stay.** A reviewer reading this chapter alone will notice that it lands its transformation twice — once in narration at beat 17, where she is different in a way Xion could not have written down and it came from a wall of paper, and again in the courtyard where she says something similar aloud — and will propose cutting the second. Do not. **This is the shortest chapter in the book at roughly 1,450 words**, it sits inside the compressed ch1–8 movement, and the next chapter opens on Xion collapsing with a fever. The courtyard is the breath between the notice board's blow and an illness, and removing it puts those two things adjacent. It also carries her first statement of intent, which the ascension plot spends seventeen chapters paying off.

The one thing worth a line-level look, independent of the scene: *deliberate cruelty, systematically applied* is a character summarising her own arc in the abstract, which is the register the house style avoids, and the beat around it is stronger than the phrase. That is a phrase question, not a structural one, and cutting the scene to solve it would be using a hammer on a splinter.

**On the plant that matters most:** the notice board is what makes ch12's dock worker, ch13's quotas and the whole indenture apparatus legible without further explanation, and it is the origin of Elara's interest in what the cartels write down — which she first tested on Xion in ch5's ledger question and will pursue for the rest of the series.

**On rhythm:** 1,453 words, **the shortest chapter in the book**, third of the four-chapter quiet stretch, and the courtyard note above is the operative form of this. The chapter is small because ch7 opens on Xion collapsing with a fever; the notice board's blow and an illness must not be adjacent. Check this profile before proposing any cut here.
