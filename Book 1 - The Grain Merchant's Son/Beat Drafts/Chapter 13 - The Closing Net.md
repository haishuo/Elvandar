# Chapter 13: The Closing Net

**Timeline:** Day 15. Morning through night.
**Characters:** Xion (POV), Elara, Mistress Janice, Farleen Darfi, watchers
**Emotional work:** A slow tightening that becomes a loss, and then an astonishment. The reader should spend the first half watching a net close, spend the middle having something taken from them that they did not know was at risk, and end the chapter looking at a thing so much larger than the book has prepared them for that the loss briefly stops hurting. Three registers in one chapter, in that order.
**POV:** Xion — close third-person limited.

---

## Beats

1. Morning, and the first sign is not an event but a wrongness. Xion notices it while organising supplies: the street's usual rhythm has shifted by degrees too small to name. He goes to the window carefully and watches a man loitering at a produce stall whose attention keeps drifting — not to the shop's front entrance where customers come and go, but to the side alley where the clinic's back door is.

2. Twenty minutes later a second man takes position by the baker's. Then a woman browsing fabric at Janice's counter whose eyes sweep the layout, memorising exits. Three that he can see and probably more that he cannot. The surveillance is professional, patient and methodical, and Xion's read of it is that they are not yet certain he is here — they are waiting for confirmation.

3. Janice puts her head through the beaded curtain once, between patients, to report the alley is covered too: a woman in grey who has been mending the same basket since noon. She withdraws before he can answer. She counted a watcher he had missed, delivered it through a curtain, and did not wait for a response.

4. The afternoon spent preparing while performing normality. She sees three routine patients through the connecting door. Elara packs two travelling loads. Xion watches the street.

5. A fourth watcher, and this one he recognises immediately — Grain cartel livery, his father's colours. They leave tonight, as soon as it is fully dark.

6. Where. Noble and Middle districts belong to the cartels; the Merchant Quarter has more chaos but more cartel presence; the docks are Water's entirely. Which leaves one place they will not immediately follow, and Xion says the Warrens with the words tasting like defeat. His argument to Elara is not that it is safe but that it is danger they choose over danger that has already found them — here they are trapped, there they at least control their own movement.

7. Full darkness. The watchers have been joined by a fifth, and the alley is held. He says they cannot leave easily and then explains why they must anyway: if the surveillance is this organised now, by morning there will be enough force to simply take the building.

8. Footsteps in the alley, approaching with purpose rather than caution, and then a knock — three taps, pause, two more. Silvanno's signal. Xion puts himself between Elara and the door with a knife meant for medical work.

9. It is Farleen's voice. Elara's eyes flash amber and she steps forward; Xion holds up a hand. The last time they saw this woman she put a blade in his shoulder.

10. The argument through a closed door, and it is all she gets. Grain cartel forces are coming tonight, an hour at most. Asked why they should believe her: because they asked her directly where the clinic was, which they would not have done unless they already suspected, and she lied and said she did not know the exact location, and they are not stupid. She came as soon as she could get away.

11. Xion notes that the desperation sounds real, and so had her confession of love before she drove a blade into him. She swears she did not lead them. Then she gives him the only argument that works, and it is evidence rather than emotion: look outside and count the watchers and see how they are positioned. He checks. She is right — the positions have tightened.

12. She asks to help, to do something right. Elara says do not open the door. Xion's hand is already on the latch. He opens it.

---

13. Farleen in the alley, hair disheveled, amber eyes red-rimmed, looking like she has been crying or running or both, and something breaking in her face when she sees him. She moves immediately to the practical: the watcher is still there, they go through the service passages, stay close and stay quiet.

14. Xion has one foot in the alley when he remembers the logs, and is back through the door before Elara can catch his sleeve — to the shelf where eight years of coded notation stands in six bound journals. Every patient, every injury, every district and date and name, in a script two people in Kaha'an can read.

15. The shelf is empty. Janice is at the front counter with all six of them squared edge to edge the way she squares everything, and the lamp lit beside them with the glass chimney already off. She has been ahead of him since the curtain.

16. She asks how long he has got. Minutes. Then he is wasting them. He crosses anyway and asks for the journals so he can carry them, and she gives the operational answer: he will be caught with them, and then it is not him they hang — it is Korvin, it is the Corris family, it is every name in the book. Eight years of people who came to a door with nothing, in a cipher two people can read. One of us is leaving.

17. He says they will know it was her. She corrects him the way she would correct a figure: they will know it was her shop, which has been true since he was fifteen, and she has had a long time to be ready for it and he has had a long time not to think about it, and this is where that stops mattering.

18. He says he cannot let her do it, and she asks why not, and what comes out of him is guilt rather than authority: because then it is his, whatever happens to her, and he is already carrying more than he can hold. He stops before finishing the sentence.

19. The copper argument, resolved after eight years, and it is resolved by concession. She has asked him for one copper for eight years and never got a number, because the whole point of what he does is that people take it and walk out owing him nothing — so he does not get to stand in her shop and tell her she cannot do it too. He says it is not the same. She says it is exactly the same: she is giving him something, and he does not get to write it down. He has no answer, and the narration notes there never was one, at this part.

20. Her hand closes on the lamp. He says her name. She says *my choice.* From the alley, Farleen says his. He promises to come back for her and she tells him he will not, and that he will go and find the boy because it is the only thing he has ever been able to do with any of this. He does not move. She looks at him a moment longer and whatever is in her face then, he will spend a long time failing to name. Then: go, Master Fen. He goes.

---

21. The flight. Farleen sets a punishing pace through back passages she plainly knows, raised voices erupting behind them as the raid begins. Four streets on, Elara catches his sleeve and makes him look back — above the weavers' rooflines, against the low orange of the lamps, a thin column of smoke going up perfectly straight in windless air. She says *the records.* He says yes. She says nothing else, and the narration supplies the reason: she held those journals two nights ago and called them proof.

22. Six blocks south, into the transition zone where the Middle District bleeds into the neighbourhoods bordering the Warrens — buildings more ramshackle, streets narrower, lighting sporadic. Farleen avoids the main descending ramp where Slavers watch the traffic and brings them to an abandoned granary with a collapsed floor.

23. The first Farleen–Elara collision, and both of them are right. Farleen has used this passage before, part of her training, and will not meet Xion's eyes saying so. Elara names it flatly as spy training; Farleen answers that not everyone gets to grow up in a compound, protected and safe. Elara says the cruellest true thing available — at least she has never stabbed him. Farleen's control cracks: she is trying to make it right and asks what more they want.

24. Xion ends it without resolving it, which is the correct move for him: he does not have to trust her, he needs her to get them into the Warrens, and after that they will see. Something flashes in Farleen's face at after that.

25. The descent. Rough-cut stairs spiralling down, water dripping, air cooling. He counts fifty, then a hundred. Farleen identifies the construction as pre-imperial — from whatever civilisation was here before Kaha'an — and notes the Warrens are built in and around the ruins. The counting is his instrument and the archaeology is the first hint of Lathion.

26. Sound arrives before sight: thousands of lives in close quarters, voices, movement, the particular echo of a vast enclosed space. Then they come out onto a ledge.

27. The reveal, and it must be wonder rather than squalor. An entire city beneath Kaha'an. Buildings clustered like mushrooms on a forest floor, built into and around massive pillars holding up a ceiling lost in the dark. Streets climbing the chamber walls on switchbacks or descending to deeper levels. Hanging lamps and cooking fires making islands of light, revealing markets, workshops, taverns, homes. Elara breathes one word. Farleen names it: the city beneath the city, sixty thousand people living in the bones of the old world. This is not the slum Xion imagined. It is a civilisation forced underground that refused to disappear.

28. He asks how they find Tam in this. Farleen's answer is specific and awful: if the Slavers took him he was probably sold to the salvage crews working the collapsed sections, because they use children for the tight spaces.

29. Elara tells Farleen she has fulfilled her obligation and can go. Farleen refuses — they do not know the Warrens, and Xion has been down here twice as Master Fen, and below here the surface rules do not apply. Elara objects again, hard, and Xion overrules her: they do not know this place, and if the boy is deep in Slaver territory they need someone who does. Three fugitives, bound by necessity and divided by everything else, and the chapter ends without any of that being fixed.

---

**On the three-part structure and the length:** 3,652 words, the longest chapter to this point, and it is carrying a siege, a bereavement and a world-reveal in sequence. Each needs its own tempo — the surveillance section is slow and observational, the Janice scene is close and almost entirely dialogue, and the descent accelerates into spectacle. **The chapter cannot be shortened by trimming any one of them**, because the reveal only lands as astonishment if the reader has just lost something, and the loss only lands because forty paragraphs of patient watching established that the danger was real.

**On the Janice scene, which is the chapter's centre and the resolution of a thread running since page one.** For eight years she has demanded a number in the book and he has refused, because his giving must never balance. So she concedes the entire argument and turns it around: she gives him something on *his* terms and cites his own practice as the precedent. It is not a debt, not a favour, and not a sacrifice to be repaid — it is a gift, the exact thing he has handed strangers for eight years and has never once been able to accept. **Do not add a warm or grateful beat after "my choice,"** which would convert an assertion into a sacrifice, and do not give him a second protest. She says it once and reaches for the lamp.

**On the line that must not be noticed yet:** *I can't let you do that* is the grammar of a man who decides for other people, which is precisely and exclusively what Rosik does. Xion does not notice, and must not. He means it innocently and it is innocent. The realisation belongs in Book 2, where it detonates properly; firing it here would make him self-aware nine chapters early and cost the later scene everything.

**On the woman who spent eight years demanding a ledger destroying the only one that exists:** she is one of two people alive who can read the cipher, so burning them is the only act in the building that only she can perform. Ch11 established the journals as political evidence; ch13 reveals the third reading, which is that they are a list of names that hangs everyone in them. **Her last three words to him are the same three she used the day he took the name.** She never says the other one.

**On Xion obeying, which is the damning part:** he offers to come back for her, she tells him he will not, and she is right. This is the third time in his life that someone he loves is destroyed by his father's power and he does not move — Mira at fifteen, Tam in a market square, Janice in a shop with minutes to spare. This time he is an adult, he has warning, and he leaves anyway because she told him to. Mira also chose. That is precisely what he has never been able to accept, and *my choice* does not absolve him — it replays the sentence he has spent eight years unable to hear. **None of this appears on the page.**

**On Farleen's return, which is built entirely out of evidence:** she gets no reunion and no forgiveness. What gets the door open is not her tears but a verifiable claim — count the watchers, look at the positions — and Xion checks before he moves. That is the correct way to readmit a character who has knifed the protagonist, and it also means her later usefulness is established as competence rather than as absolution.

**On the reveal:** the Warrens must not read as a slum. Sixty thousand people, markets, workshops, taverns, a civilisation in the bones of an older one. If the reader pities it, ch14 through ch21 do not work, because the Warrens have to be a place with its own powers and laws that the surface has failed rather than a hole the poor fell into.
