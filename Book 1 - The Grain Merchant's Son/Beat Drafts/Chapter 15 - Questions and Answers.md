# Chapter 15: Questions and Answers

**Timeline:** Day 16. Morning into midday, in the Tiers and at the Crossroads.
**Characters:** Xion (POV), Elara, Farleen, Korvin, a food vendor, a Slaver enforcer, Arlo Berakailen
**Emotional work:** Two discoveries running in opposite directions. Master Fen turns out to be currency down here — the thing that keeps opening doors — and Elara turns out to find that unbearable. The reader should end the chapter with the rescue newly possible and the partnership newly cracked, and should not be certain which of the two matters more.
**POV:** Xion — close third-person limited.

---

## Beats

1. Morning in Korvin's room, announced by the Warrens waking — vendors, carts on stone, children's voices in narrow streets. Xion has barely slept and his shoulder aches. Korvin is already up making thin porridge, and offers it before they can decline. Xion says they have imposed enough and his stomach contradicts him audibly.

2. Korvin's warning is specific rather than atmospheric: word spreads fast down here, and after last night people will be curious or cautious or both — three outsiders arrived and one of them put down three armed men in ten heartbeats.

3. His directions to the Crossroads, twenty minutes on the main paths, and the rule that matters: do not wander into areas you do not recognise, and if someone tells you to turn back, listen — because however well you handle yourself there is always someone tougher, smarter or more connected. He addresses that last part to Elara specifically.

4. His parting line is the chapter's first statement of the debt theme and it is delivered lightly: he would hate for his debt to Xion to end with Xion getting himself killed down here. He flexes the scarred hand as he says it.

---

5. The morning Warrens are louder and more chaotic than the night version and somehow less menacing, daylight filtering through gaps in the ceiling far above. People move with purpose, hauling goods, conducting business, living lives entirely outside the notice of the surface.

6. Xion notices almost immediately that they are being watched — a vegetable seller tracking them past her stall, a group of men going quiet as they approach and resuming after, children scattering not in fear but in wariness. Farleen names it: not hostile, evaluative. Like people trying to decide something about them.

7. Four rough men at a corner, exactly the sort who would treat three outsiders as easy marks. The leader's eyes go over the medical bag and Farleen's good clothes under the borrowed cloak — and then find Elara, and something changes. He says good morning. He steps aside, and his companions clear the path.

8. Farleen's reaction is the important one, because she is the only person present qualified to say it: that was not strange, it was impossible. They had numbers, the three of them are obviously outsiders, and there is no guard down here to stop anything. Elara suggests word spread that they are dangerous; Farleen corrects her — dangerous means worth testing, worth taking down to prove you are tougher. This is not fear. It is something else, like they are waiting for something. Her hand has not left her belt and her eyes have not stopped moving.

9. Xion's assessment of Farleen's assessment: she got them in, she knows how to move through hostile ground, and if she is confused then they are in deeper water than he understood.

10. A small marketplace, and Farleen works it professionally — three men in a corner watching since they arrived, probably Long Knives stationed to track who comes and goes, and if the three of them start asking obvious questions it goes up a chain. Her solution is to find someone with less to lose: a stall at the market's edge, sightlines in several directions, the man alone with no family nearby to endanger.

11. The vendor recognises him before they speak. Xion stitched him up after cartel business six months back and told him to stay off his feet for a week; it still hurts when it rains and he is alive, which is more than most can say.

12. He will help and he cannot be seen helping — he has a wife and children — so he pretends to show them his wares while he talks, gesturing at food as if they are haggling. The tradecraft is his, invented on the spot, because people who live here know how to do this.

13. What he gives them: the Crossroads is where information trades, and the man to see is Arlo Berakailen, the best broker in the Warrens. But it will not be free, and Arlo does not take coin — he takes other information, favours, whatever he judges worth the trade.

14. He hands Xion dried meat as though completing a sale, says that is all he can tell them and they did not hear it from him. Xion thanks him for the risk and the man's answer is the chapter's motif stated plainly: *you saved my life, figure I owe you that much* — and after this we are even, and I cannot acknowledge knowing you if we cross paths. He turns to other customers immediately and makes a point of not watching them go.

---

15. The Slavers, and this is the chapter's hinge. Enforcers intercept them at the Crossroads, spreading to cut off easy exits, the lead man delivering the standard warning about people poking into things that do not concern them. Elara tenses; Farleen's hand drifts to her hidden knife.

16. Then the lead Slaver actually looks at Xion's face and asks if he is Master Fen. Xion hesitates and says yes. The change is immediate and total. The man's cousin Rykan got cut in a territory dispute last month and came to the clinic bleeding, and Xion patched him up, did not ask for payment, and did not ask what happened.

17. He steps his men back. He has not seen the boy — that is above his pay grade — but he offers what he can: some of the people who run the operations have reputations, the kind that make you glad you are only a grunt and not one of the people they take. He says why he is telling them: Rykan says Xion treated him like he mattered, even though Xion probably knew what he does for work, and that is not common.

18. He turns and disperses the watching crowd — show's over, these three are just asking questions, nobody needs to make it bigger — and then gives them a real warning: not everyone down here is like him, and after last night people are talking about the girl who put down three armed men without breaking a sweat, and careful only lasts so long. Eventually somebody decides you are worth the risk. He refuses thanks: he is returning a favour, Rykan is family, family matters down here, and after this we are even.

19. Farleen's verdict, and she is unsettled rather than relieved: that should not have happened. Slavers do not walk away from potential targets, they do not care about gratitude, they care about profit and power. That man passed up a shakedown or a bounty — for what, because Xion stitched up his cousin? Xion offers that family matters to people even here. She is not convinced, and says something else is happening that they are not seeing, which makes this more dangerous than she already thought it was, and she already thought it was suicidal.

20. Elara has been silent through the whole exchange, and now she says it: you patched up slavers. Not a question.

21. The argument, and it is the book's central moral collision arriving three chapters early. She names them as the people who took Tam, the people who kidnap children, and he *helped them. He says he helps injured people and does not ask what they do for work. She calls that avoiding a moral judgment rather than making one. He answers with the principle he has held for eight years: he does not get to decide who deserves healing, and nobody does, because the moment he says *you are too evil to deserve my help he stops being a healer and becomes something else. She says he becomes someone with principles. He says no — he becomes someone who decides the value of a human life by his own moral framework, and that is a power nobody should have.

22. He escalates to the slope: if he chooses by approval, where does it end — guards whose laws he dislikes, merchants who overcharge, someone he heard a rumour about. She says those are not the same thing. He says they are all judgment calls about who deserves help.

23. The narration then does the thing the whole book turns on, in two sentences and without either character noticing. It confirms he believes every word and has for eight years and will go on believing it. And then: she had not asked him whether slavers deserve care. She had said these are the people who took Tam — and he answered a different question instead, cleanly and well, the way a man reaches for the tool nearest his hand. He puts the thought where he keeps the others.

24. Farleen ends it on tactics rather than merit — they are having a very public disagreement in the middle of the Crossroads and can table it somewhere less exposed. Elara agrees tightly and says the conversation is not over. Xion says he did not expect it to be.

25. Arlo Berakailen, younger than expected, maybe forty, clever eyes and a face that gives nothing away. He has been expecting them; the Warrens have been buzzing about Xion's arrival and his very impressive companion. He knows who Xion is because he makes it his business to know everyone who matters in the Warrens — and Xion, he says, matters quite a bit, even if he does not realise it.

26. He might know where the boy is. But first they should discuss what the information is worth and what they are willing to trade. Xion's heart sinks at the negotiation he has been dreading — the moment his resources run up against reality — and he asks what Arlo wants. Arlo leans back with evident satisfaction and tells him that is exactly the right question. End there.

---

**On the chapter's real discovery, which is not the information:** the plot business is that they learn Arlo's name and reach his platform. What actually happens is that Master Fen turns out to be **currency**. Three separate people — Korvin, the vendor, the Slaver — open doors that should not open, and every one of them says a version of *after this we are even.* Xion has spent eight years building something whose entire design is that nobody owes him anything, and down here it is the only thing he has that works. He does not notice. Farleen, who is a professional, notices and is frightened by it, because she cannot model a system where gratitude is a currency.

**On the Slaver, and why he must not be redeemed:** he is not a good man having a change of heart. He is a man discharging a specific debt on behalf of a specific cousin, he says so plainly, and he tells them the arrangement expires immediately. The same structure as the vendor and as Korvin. **Do not let any of the three become an ally**, and do not let the chapter suggest Xion's kindness is converting anyone. It is buying passage, once, from people who keep accounts.

**On the argument, which is the seed of ch22 and must be planted exactly here:** Xion wins it. His position is coherent, he has held it for eight years, and every word of it is true. The chapter then quietly registers that **he answered a question she did not ask** — she said *these are the people who took Tam*, which is grief rather than ethics, and he reached for the nearest tool and gave her a beautifully constructed defence of universal care. That is why ch22 detonates: Elara has been carrying an unanswered sentence since this moment, and when she finally uses it she reaches for the same weapon off the same shelf **because the first time it went in.** The ch22 prose says so explicitly, so ch15 must not.

**On Elara's position, which is not wrong:** she is twenty, she watched a child taken, and she has just learned the man beside her has been repairing the people who do the taking. The chapter gives her the weaker argument and the better grievance, and the imbalance is deliberate — she loses on logic and is not answered on substance. **Do not let a revision give her a better case here.** The point is that she cannot argue it and cannot let it go.

**On rhythm:** 3,186 words, the third of four consecutive chapters over 3,100. This is the deepest part of the descent and the chapters are meant to run long and continuous. The chapter also has almost no physical action after the Slaver stand-down, which is correct — ch14 was violence, ch16 is a heist, and ch15 is the one where the danger is entirely social.
