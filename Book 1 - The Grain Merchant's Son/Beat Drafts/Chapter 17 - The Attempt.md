# Chapter 17: The Attempt

**Timeline:** Day 17. Morning into early afternoon, the Deep Warrens.
**Characters:** Xion (POV), Farleen, Elara, six Slavers, Korvin (asleep)
**Emotional work:** The rescue fails, and it fails without Xion in the room. He is told — correctly, by the two people qualified to say it — that he is the reason the attempt cannot be made, and then he stands in a corridor and counts. The chapter ends by revealing that none of it ever mattered: they were being watched from the beginning, and the day stopped being his some hours before he noticed.
**POV:** Xion — close third-person limited, and this chapter is the strictest application of the rule in Book 1. The action happens twice, offstage, both times.

---

## Beats

1. Morning in the Warrens sounds exactly like the morning before, and nothing else about the day is the same. Arlo's information spread across Korvin's table one more time — routes, schedules, guard patterns, everything about where Tam is and how the crews move. Everything except how to get him out. Farleen has been dressed and ready for an hour. They leave Korvin sleeping.

2. The descent into levels Xion has never seen. Air cooling, habitation thinning out behind them, and ahead the sounds of the scavenger operations — metal on stone, voices calling instructions, something heavy on rollers. Farleen follows Arlo's directions with something that reads as work rather than confidence.

3. She stops them and stands a long moment looking at the passage ahead without moving, and says something is wrong and that she does not yet know what.

4. She goes ahead to scout alone, and shuts the objection down by categorising all three of them accurately and without cruelty: she is the only one trained to move quietly; Xion is neither fighter nor scout and would be seen inside a minute; and Elara is neither of those things either — she is a weapon, which is a different job, and this is not the moment for it. Elara's jaw sets and she does not argue, which is its own answer.

5. Her instruction if she is not back inside the hour: she has been caught or killed, do not come after her, go back to the Tiers and think again. She delivers it the way another person reads out a supply list, and then goes into the dark making no sound.

6. The hour, and Xion cannot remember it afterward — only the shape it left, which is a man with nothing to do standing in a corridor while somebody else spends herself on his behalf. Elara paces. He offers that Farleen will come back and Elara agrees without looking away from the dark, and says that is not the part she is worried about.

7. Farleen returns at forty minutes, and Xion knows from how she is standing before she opens her mouth. Heavier than Arlo had it — more men, better placed, tighter rotation — and that is *not* the problem.

8. The problem, and it is the chapter's best piece of expertise: the position is **good**. The gaps are in the wrong places. There are two ways in that somebody like her finds and both leave you visible for about nine seconds, and nine seconds is a strange length of time for a gap to be — too long to be an oversight, too short to be a wall. Either bad luck, or a room built to catch somebody, and she cannot tell him which. She says that last part explicitly rather than guessing, which is the difference between her and everyone else in the book.

9. Asked whether they can get past, she says probably — once — and then they are inside carrying a child who cannot walk, with six men between them and the way out.

10. Her recommendation: walk away. They tried, they bought the information, they scouted the ground, and this is the point at which people who intend to survive recognise they are outmatched. Xion refuses in two words: *he's forty feet away.*

11. Her control cracks, and this is the first time in the book it does. She has been trained for this exact thing since she was fourteen. She knows what an impossible room looks like. And then the part that is not tactics at all — if he dies down here, everything she threw away to get him out of that clinic was thrown away for nothing. Her voice goes on the last of it and she stops.

12. Xion has no answer, and the narration says so and then says what is on the other side of the scale: a promise made in a clinic doorway to two people who owned nothing, a boy who has never once been anybody's priority, and against that a woman who has already lost her family to keep him breathing. He offers not to make her come; she tells him he is not going at all, because the moment he is seen the two of them stop being a rescue and start being two people deciding whether to leave him behind.

13. He looks to Elara and she takes a long moment and says Farleen is right. **This is the only time in Book 1 that Elara sides with Farleen against him.** It ends the argument in a way Farleen alone never could.

14. The offer, which is genuinely the best available: he stays, they go as close as it can be gone, and if there is a real opening they take it and if there is not they come back — no heroics, no improvising. Take it or everyone leaves now. The helplessness comes down on him like water closing over his head and he agrees.

15. Thirty-one minutes. He knows because he counted, and counting was the only thing left he was any use for. That is the entire scene.

---

16. They come back filthy and scraped and without a boy. Farleen's voice has gone flat and professionally empty, which is how Xion understands what it cost her. They got close enough to see the choke point: six men on it and more behind, and in a corridor that width six is the same as sixty.

17. Blood on Elara's sleeve that is not hers — one of them came round a corner faster than he should have. She says it with no satisfaction at all: he will live, and he will remember her. Then, a moment later, that they could have fought through. Farleen's answer prices it exactly: three of them, possibly four, and then Xion would have had a princess and a spy dead in a tunnel and a boy still down there, and nothing about tomorrow one scrap better.

18. Xion looks at the passage where his sight ends, and at the forty feet beyond it, and cannot make himself say the thing he is thinking, which is *that's it, then.*

19. Footsteps behind them. Several sets, unhurried, coming down the way they came in.

---

20. Six Slavers, and the distinction from the opportunists of the first night is the whole point — equipped, arranged, entirely unhurried, and coming down a corridor Farleen chose precisely because nobody uses it. Nobody runs; Farleen understands before Xion does and puts a flat hand on Elara's arm.

21. The lead man's eyes stop on Xion and he says the name, not as a question. The summons is delivered as neither threat nor arrest — a man conveying something decided elsewhere, by somebody senior, some time ago. Asked by whom, he says they will find out when they get there. Asked what happens if they refuse, he considers Elara without a trace of hostility and says: *you won't.*

22. And it is not a threat, and that is the whole of it — a statement of fact from a man who has already run the arithmetic and knows they will run it too and arrive where he did. Standing there, far too late, Xion sees the shape of the entire day: the heavier guard, the rotation tightened in the last few hours, the gaps that were almost gaps, the route that felt like a room built to catch somebody. It had been.

23. The recognition, which is retroactive and total: they were watched not since this morning but since the beginning. Every question at the Crossroads, every door Farleen counted — observed by people who let it run because letting it run cost them nothing.

24. He agrees to go, Elara's head turns sharply, and he says aloud that they do not have a choice and makes the lead man confirm it. There is something almost like respect in the confirmation.

25. Up out of the corridor surrounded but not held, in a formation that is as much escort as custody, people in the passages drawing their own conclusions. Farleen gives him the smallest shake of the head — not *don't resist*, something nearer to *this was always coming.* He does not know where they are being taken or who decided it or whether any of them come back up. He knows only that it stopped being his day some hours ago and that he did not notice when. End there.

---

**On the chapter being the strictest POV test in the book, and why that is the reason it works.** The rescue attempt happens twice and Xion is present for neither. The scout is offstage; the attempt is offstage; what the reader gets is an hour he cannot afterward remember and thirty-one minutes he counted. A version that follows Farleen into the dark would be a better *action* chapter and a catastrophically worse one for this novel, because the subject is not whether they reach Tam. It is what it does to a man to be told, accurately, by people who like him, that his presence is the thing that makes the attempt impossible. Everything the chapter has is in the waiting.

**On Farleen finally winning, and what it costs to let her.** She has argued this three times — ch14, ch16, and here — and been correct every time and overridden every time. Here she wins, and the chapter should be clear that she does not win on argument. She wins because Elara backs her, and Elara backs her because Elara has now seen the ground. That is the only moment in Book 1 where those two are on the same side of anything, and it should be given exactly one line and no acknowledgement from either of them.

Her crack is also not tactical. *Everything I threw away to get you out of that clinic was thrown away for nothing* is not a risk assessment — it is the first time she says out loud what she is actually protecting, and her voice goes on it. She has spent three chapters being right about danger; this is the chapter that says why she cares. **Do not let a revision move this line into the calmer register she uses everywhere else.**

**On the nine seconds, which is the chapter's one piece of real craft in the professional-expertise line.** Farleen does not say *it's a trap.* She says the gap is a strange length — too long to be an oversight, too short to be a wall — and then says she cannot tell him which it is. That is what genuine expertise sounds like: it produces a precise anomaly and refuses to over-read it. It also means the reader is holding the answer for six beats before Xion is, which is why the summons lands as confirmation rather than surprise.

**On the recognition at the end, which is the ch6 faculty firing late again.** Xion assembles the day only once it is over — the heavier guard, the tightened rotation, the almost-gaps. He has all the pieces and does not put them together until a man says *you won't* to him in a corridor. This is the third instance of the pattern: the notice board he walked past ten thousand times, the years of Farleen's plausible excuses, and now a day he spent inside a room built to catch him. **The narration must not editorialise it**; the sequence of noticings is the whole statement.

**On Elara not killing this time, which is deliberate and unremarked.** She killed a guard in ch16 and said it was necessary. Here she cuts a man and reports that he will live and will remember her, with no satisfaction in it, and then argues that they could have fought through and is refused. Nobody connects the two, and nobody should. It is there for the reader who is keeping count.

**On rhythm:** 1,680 words, dropping from four consecutive chapters over 3,100 — the sharpest contraction in the novel. The descent has been long and heavy and continuous since ch13, and this snaps it short. The chapter is *supposed* to feel like it ends before it has finished; that is what a failure feels like from inside, and ch18 opens with the consequence. Anyone tempted to expand the offstage attempt into a scene should note that the contraction is doing structural work the added words would destroy.

**On the plant this collects:** ch16 planted, in four words, that Vex's hired muscle is Dermir's. The summons is the collection, and ch18 is the invoice. Nothing in this chapter should name Ronas or gesture at him; the lead Slaver's refusal to say who sent them is the correct amount of information.
