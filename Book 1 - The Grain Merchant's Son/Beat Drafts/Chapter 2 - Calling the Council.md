# Chapter 2: Calling the Council

**Timeline:** Day 2. Before dawn to mid-morning. Immediately following ch1.
**Characters:** Xion (POV), Silvanno Larannas, Kael Issamil, Farleen Darfi
**Emotional work:** The reader has to want him to be right while watching three reasonable people give three reasonable arguments that he is not. And Farleen has to agree to help in a way that reads, on a first pass, as love — and on a reread, as the worst moment in the chapter.
**POV:** Xion — close third-person limited.

---

## Beats

1. He sends the messages before dawn by separate runners. Identical notes, torn parchment: *Urgent. Meet today. Something has happened.* He has never called a meeting in six months of the group's existence — he has always been the one dragged away from the clinic. **The inversion is the chapter's premise and should be stated once, flatly, and not returned to.**

2. He arrives at The Amber Leaf with his hands shaking on no sleep, having spent the night replaying blue-to-amber-to-violet every time he closed his eyes. Note that the recall is of **the eyes**, not of the boy — and do not comment on it. Ch12 will collect on this when Tam's parents arrive and he has to be reminded the boy exists.

3. Silvanno first, easy and confident, and his smile goes when he gets a proper look at Xion's face. Xion cannot manage a smile back. Silvanno's register drops immediately from banter to concern; this establishes that whatever is wrong is visible, and that these people know him.

4. Kael, economical, no preamble: *your message was cryptic, what happened.* Xion stalls for Farleen. The stalling matters — he wants all three, which means he wants a decision, which means he has already decided.

5. Farleen arrives late with the excuse she will use again in ch3 — *my father had unexpected guests this morning.* **Plant it and do not have Xion notice it.** In the old draft he clocked the repetition and grew suspicious, which quietly wrecked the ch4 betrayal, because a man who has been suspicious for two chapters cannot be blindsided.

6. He tells them. *I think I saw her* — then the whole thing in a rush: the boy, the false debt, the woman in the crowd, blue to amber to violet, the royal trait. His excitement breaks through the exhaustion at the end of it: not patching the boat one leak at a time — *we can upturn the whole damn rotten edifice.*

7. **The silence.** Then three refusals, and they must differ by temperament, not volume.

8. **Farleen's is care.** *You've been through something traumatic. Watching that boy get taken—* She reaches for his hand on the table. Her concern is genuine and the reader should feel it as genuine, because it is. It is also the only one of the three refusals that has a second reason underneath it, and the chapter must not hint at that.

9. **Silvanno's is exhaustion.** *Another one?* — the last Princess Elara had coloured lenses in her eyes and fooled nobody for long. He is not being cruel; he is tired of this specific hope.

10. **Kael's is method.** Emotional distress produces false perception; six fake princesses in five years. He is applying a rule, and the rule is correct.

11. Xion loses his temper — *I know what I saw* — loudly enough that patrons look. He apologises for the volume immediately, which is characteristic: he will fight for the claim and not for the room.

12. Silvanno makes the strongest version of the counter-argument, gently: your emotions were high, the light was strange, maybe someone had coloured glass drops, **maybe your mind filled in details that weren't there.** He explicitly does not accuse Xion of lying. This must be genuinely persuasive or the chapter has no tension.

13. Xion concedes the whole frame and holds anyway: *I know that's what I'm supposed to think, and I spent all last night trying to convince myself of exactly that.* The concession is what makes him credible. He is not defending a certainty; he is reporting a failure to talk himself out of one.

14. Kael moves from *did you see it* to *what would it change*, which is the real question and a harder one. If she has been hidden this long there are reasons, and reasons don't evaporate.

15. Xion pre-empts their objections aloud — *what if she's not what you think, what if she won't help* — and grants he cannot answer either. Then the argument he actually wins on, and it is not about the princess: **six months of the group's work has not moved anything.** Bailing a sinking ship with a thimble. If he's wrong they lose a few days; if he's right, it is the first real change since The Rending.

16. **Silvanno turns first, and against his own position** — *those people deserve better than charity. They deserve a system that doesn't grind them up in the first place. Damn you for being right about that.* He is conceding the premise while still refusing the conclusion, which is the honest shape of being persuaded.

17. Farleen presses once more and harder, and her argument shifts from *you were traumatised* to *this could be dangerous, really dangerous.* Note the change: she has stopped disputing what he saw and started trying to stop him anyway. **The reader gets the tell here and Xion does not.**

18. Kael names the scale — overturning the balance of power on a moment's glimpse. Xion reframes it as possibility rather than certainty, and proposes the smallest version that could work: **two days, quietly, starting at the market square.**

19. Silvanno asks the only question left: *you're really going to do this, aren't you. With or without us.* Yes. No hesitation.

20. They fold, one at a time, and the chapter's last beat belongs to Farleen. Her expression goes distant — she is looking at something past his shoulder — and when she comes back there is something in it he cannot name. **Sadness, maybe. Resignation.** Then: *"We'll help you."*

21. End on that line. Nothing after it.

---

**The chapter's real subject is not whether they believe him.** It is that he calls, and they come, and it costs them — which is the thing ch3 will collect on when two of the three walk away. Ch2 and ch3 were once flagged as running the same argument twice; they no longer do, because ch3 reverses it. Keep them distinct: **ch2 is "you're mad," ch3 is "you're right, and that's worse."**

**Farleen's agreement is the chapter's engine and it must play twice.** First pass: the woman who loves him is the only one who doesn't push back hard, and agrees when it matters. Reread: she is the only one who never once tried to talk him out of it, because she already knew what she would have to do, and she volunteered to be close enough to do it. **Nothing on the page may nudge the reader toward the second reading.** The distant look is the whole permitted signal.

**Xion is fluent here, and that is deliberate.** He has six speeches over fifty words in this chapter and none over fifteen in ch1's copper argument. A man with no language for his own compulsion and plenty for hope. Do not "balance" this.

**The "tomorrow" handoff.** Ch1 ends on *tomorrow, he would act.* Ch2 opens with him acting — for the princess, not the boy. The irony is structural and nobody remarks on it.

**Do not let the ending forecast.** The chapter previously closed on *"Xion felt something release in his chest—relief, gratitude, hope… That mattered more than he wanted to admit."* It now ends on Farleen's four words, which is the same information with the interpretation removed and the reread intact.
