# Chapter 2: Calling the Council

**Timeline:** Day 2. Before dawn to mid-morning, immediately following ch1.
**Characters:** Xion (POV), Silvanno Larannas, Kael Issamil, Farleen Darfi
**Emotional work:** The reader has to want him to be right while watching three reasonable people give three reasonable arguments that he is not. And Farleen's agreement has to read as love on the first pass and as the worst moment in the chapter on a reread.
**POV:** Xion — close third-person limited.

---

## Beats

1. He sends the messages before dawn by separate runners, identical notes on torn parchment, saying only that something has happened and he needs them today. He has never called a meeting in six months of the group's existence; he has always been the one dragged away from the clinic. The inversion is the chapter's premise, stated once and flatly, and not returned to.

2. He arrives at the teahouse on no sleep with his hands shaking, having spent the night replaying the colour change every time he closed his eyes. Note that what he replays is the eyes and not the boy, and let it pass without comment. Chapter 12 collects on this when Tam's parents arrive and he has to be reminded the boy exists at all.

3. Silvanno arrives first, easy and confident, and his smile goes when he gets a proper look at Xion's face. Xion cannot manage one back. Silvanno drops immediately from banter into concern, which establishes both that something is visibly wrong and that these people know him well enough to see it.

4. Kael next, economical, no preamble, wanting to know what happened. Xion stalls for Farleen. The stalling matters: he wants all three present, which means he wants a decision, which means he has already made his.

5. Farleen arrives late with an excuse about her father having unexpected guests. Plant it and do not let Xion react to it. In an earlier version he noticed the repetition and grew suspicious, which quietly wrecked the ch4 betrayal, because a man who has been suspicious for two chapters cannot be blindsided by one.

6. He tells them, and it comes out in a rush once it starts — the boy, the fabricated debt, the woman watching from the crowd, the colour change, the royal trait. His excitement breaks through the exhaustion at the end of it: not patching one leak at a time but overturning the whole arrangement.

7. Silence, and then three refusals, which must differ by temperament rather than volume. Farleen's is care: he has been through something traumatic, he watched a child taken, and she reaches for his hand across the table. Her concern is genuine and the reader should register it as genuine, because it is. It is also the only one of the three with a second reason underneath it, and nothing in the chapter may hint at that.

8. Silvanno's refusal is exhaustion rather than doubt. There was a Princess Elara last year with coloured lenses who fooled nobody for long. He is not being cruel; he is tired of this specific hope, which is a different and more useful objection.

9. Kael's is method. People see things in emotional distress; the group has watched something like six impostors in five years. He is applying a rule, and the rule is correct.

10. Xion loses his temper loudly enough that other patrons look, and apologises for the volume immediately. The pattern is characteristic and worth preserving: he will fight for the claim and not for the room.

11. Silvanno makes the strongest version of the counter-argument, gently and without accusing him of lying — high emotion, strange light, coloured glass drops, a mind filling in details that were not there. This has to be genuinely persuasive or the chapter has no tension in it.

12. Xion concedes the entire frame and holds anyway. He knows what he is supposed to think and spent the whole night trying to talk himself into it. The concession is what makes him credible: he is not defending a certainty, he is reporting a failure to argue himself out of one.

13. Kael shifts the question from whether he saw it to what it would change, which is harder. If she has stayed hidden this long there are reasons, and reasons do not evaporate because someone finds her.

14. Xion pre-empts their remaining objections aloud — what if she is not what he imagines, what if she refuses — and grants he cannot answer either one. Then he wins on a different argument entirely, and it is not about the princess: six months of the group's work has moved nothing. If he is wrong they lose a few days. If he is right it is the first real change since The Rending.

15. Silvanno turns first, and against his own position, conceding that the people they help deserve a system that does not grind them up rather than charity that softens it. He is granting the premise while still refusing the conclusion, which is the honest shape of being persuaded.

16. Farleen presses once more and harder, and her argument changes underneath her: she stops disputing what he saw and starts arguing that it is dangerous regardless. The reader gets the tell here. Xion does not.

17. Kael names the scale of it — overturning the balance of power on a moment's glimpse — and Xion reframes to possibility rather than certainty, then proposes the smallest version that could work: two days, quietly, starting where he saw her.

18. Silvanno asks the only question left, which is whether Xion is going to do this with or without them. The answer is immediate and has no hedge in it.

19. They fold one at a time, and the chapter's last beat belongs to Farleen. Her expression goes distant, focused past his shoulder on something he cannot see, and when it comes back there is something in it he cannot name — sadness, or resignation. Then she agrees to help. End on her four words with nothing after them.

---

**On what the chapter is actually about:** not whether they believe him. It is that he called and they came, and that coming will cost them, which is what ch3 collects when two of the three walk away. These two chapters were once flagged as running the same argument twice and no longer do, because ch3 reverses it. The distinction is worth protecting in any future revision: ch2 is *you are mad*, and ch3 is *you are right, and that is worse.*

**On Farleen's agreement, which is the chapter's engine and must play twice:** on a first pass, the woman who loves him is the only one who does not push back hard and the only one who agrees when it counts. On a reread, she is the only one who never once tried to talk him out of it, because she already knew what she would be required to do, and she volunteered in the same breath in order to be close enough to do it. Nothing on the page may nudge the reader toward the second reading. The distant look is the entire permitted signal, and it should be readable afterward as her seeing the shape of the rest of her life.

**On Xion's fluency here, which is deliberate:** he has six speeches over fifty words in this chapter and none over fifteen in ch1's copper argument, and the contrast is the character rather than an inconsistency. He has no language at all for his own compulsion and a great deal for hope. Any future pass that tries to balance his dialogue across the book will flatten something true.

**On the handoff from ch1:** the previous chapter closes on a promise to act tomorrow, and this one opens with him acting — for the princess, not for the boy. The irony is structural and nobody remarks on it, here or later. Chapter 12 is where it is paid, and it is paid by Tam's parents rather than by any narrator.

**On the ending:** the chapter previously closed on a paragraph naming his relief, gratitude and hope, and noting that Farleen's presence mattered more than he wanted to admit. It now ends on her line. The information is identical, the interpretation is gone, and the reread survives — which the old version did not, because it told the reader how to feel about a moment whose whole function is to be misread.

**On rhythm:** 1,913 words, dead centre of the compressed ch1–8 movement. This is the first chapter after the establishing one and it is deliberately smaller — one room, one conversation, three friends. The whole of Act I fits inside 8,500 words, and that compression is what makes ch9's jump to 3,695 read as the floor opening rather than as a long chapter.
