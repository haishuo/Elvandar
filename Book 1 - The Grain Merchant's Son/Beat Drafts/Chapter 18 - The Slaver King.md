# Chapter 18: The Slaver King

**Timeline:** Day 17, continuous with ch17. Night, the Midlevels down into the Depths.
**Characters:** Xion (POV), Farleen, Elara, Varris, Cullen, Ronas Dermir, Tam
**Emotional work:** Xion gets Tam back and it is not a rescue. It is handed to him by a man doing arithmetic, and the thing that tipped the arithmetic is five minutes of unglamorous work he did on the way in for reasons that had nothing to do with any of it. Then he opens the boy's hand, does the best work of his life on the floor of an enemy's hall, and finds out it does not matter. The chapter gives the reader the victory and takes it away with the back of two fingers against a forehead.
**POV:** Xion — close third-person limited.

---

## Beats

1. The Midlevels at night, shuttered and thinned out, the space feeling larger and emptier than in daylight. Six armed escorts in a loose perimeter, moving with quiet efficiency, nobody speaking. The silence reads as everyone saving energy for whatever comes next.

2. One of the escorts is limping, and Xion registers it as a hitch in the gait — a man managing his weight distribution, keeping his face neutral, failing by a fraction every few steps. Left calf. The binding is amateur work, too tight at the top and not enough support at the bottom. It will get infected.

3. His feet slow before he has consciously decided to stop, and the word comes out quieter than he meant it. The take-over, in the least survivable place in the book.

4. Farleen sees where he is looking, gives him the smallest shake of the head and his name barely above a whisper. He acknowledges it with the tiniest dip of the chin. Then does it anyway. Both halves matter — he registers the warning, agrees it is correct, and proceeds.

5. He tells the man he is injured and asks to look at it, which is the guardrail holding under maximum pressure: he asks permission of an armed stranger inside a Slaver escort. The escort leader interposes, not aggressively, and says they are on a schedule. Five minutes, Xion says. Less if the wound is clean. The guard refuses again, harder, and his hand drifts to the knife at his belt. Xion does not step back.

6. The escort leader glances at the injured man — and Xion understands, a beat late, exactly which corner this one came round too fast yesterday. He is offering to treat the man Elara cut. The guard says it out loud: Xion's *girl* is the one who got him. Elara straightens a fraction, chin up, a ghost of a smile. Satisfaction. Farleen's jaw tightens. Heat crawls up Xion's neck; he starts to correct the possessive, stops, and puts the sentence down. It does not matter how the man got it.

7. Then the question that is the chapter's hinge and looks like nothing — *why do you care.* Xion's answer is four words about it needing treating, and the simplicity is what unbalances the guard, who searches his face for the trick, the angle, the leverage. Xion waits. Farleen says nothing at all.

8. The treatment on a crate: a clean slice across the outer calf, dirty binding, early inflammation. He works quickly, without commentary or judgement. The guard notes that he is not asking for payment and asks why not, and is told he needs treatment rather than a bill. Aftercare delivered exactly as it would be in the back room — keep it clean, change it daily, find a healer if red streaks climb. The guard's expression never changes. He is still waiting for the other shoe. It does not drop.

9. The rest of the walk is heavier. Farleen's gaze on Xion periodically, assessing and silent. Elara quiet in a different register, pleased, like she has won something. He keeps his eyes forward and tries not to think about either of them.

10. The descent past the lower markets into the ancient tunnels themselves — smooth brass gleaming dully, proportions slightly wrong, too tall and too narrow, built by hands that were not human. Air cooler and drier, moving against his face, flowing from somewhere to somewhere for reasons nobody understands.

11. The Iron Hall: fifty metres across, ceiling eight or nine metres up, platforms and ramps and partitioned storage, guard posts on six or seven tunnel mouths at different heights, and a floor that is actually level in the way no modern stonemason can manage. Not territory — infrastructure, and it took discipline and resources.

12. The detail that carries the place is that the Slavers have built *around* the ancient space and not into it. The ramshackle human construction does not quite touch the brass walls, as though even they hesitate to mark something they cannot understand.

13. Ronas Dermir behind a desk on a raised platform, and Xion knows him — from a distance, at Coterie meetings he was forced to attend, mostly talking business with Rosik or Tania or Sa Ko. The hard eyes, the upturned lip, the look of a man who knew he was never truly accepted there. The reversal is the point: then Ronas was a thug among finery; now Xion is standing in Ronas's domain. Ronas does not stand. His voice is mild and conversational throughout and never once needs to be otherwise.

14. He names Xion in full, as a statement rather than a question. He met the father twice at cartel negotiations years back and Xion was there once — younger, quieter, but he remembers the face. And he has heard about Master Fen, patching people up, getting himself a name off it. The alias falls, out loud, in front of everyone, and Xion has nothing to say.

15. He turns to Farleen and calls her the princess. Farleen goes very still — then for one heartbeat straightens, chin lifting, a ghost of satisfaction crossing her face. She opens her mouth.

16. Elara steps forward, tight and irritated, and claims it. Ronas raises an eyebrow and says one word with an enormous amount of scepticism in it, and Elara deflates, visibly, the way a person does when even a brutal Slaver lord does not think they look the part. Farleen's face is carefully neutral and something crosses it that is not quite triumph. The moment stretches, is awkward, and is never mentioned again.

17. Ronas reassesses aloud: Water cartel, one of Tania's, and it does not much matter. Then Elara — the one who tore through his men, the *actual* princess. Kemvimore heir, imperial princess, Water spy. A real headache. And the sentence that is the chapter's problem statement: you made me a problem, boy.

18. Before the reasoning, the cause. Varris, the guard whose leg Xion patched, has never spoken up for anyone in the five years he has worked for Ronas. Never. Then five minutes of work and suddenly he is vouching. Xion glances back; Varris is standing near the entrance with an unreadable face.

19. The terms, flat: take the boy, leave the Warrens, straight up through the Market of Sighs, do not stop until the surface, we are done. Xion asks whether that is it and gets asked whether he is sure he wants to be questioning this.

20. The three fingers, and this is Ronas doing what Ronas does. They robbed a house he had men standing in — he does not care what Vex lost, he cares it went missing off his watch and every broker down here knows it. Then they came asking round the Crossroads after his business. Then she opened up one of his on the way out of a job they had no cause to be near. Three times in two days with Xion's name near every one. Not a run of luck: a pattern, and patterns are what he gets paid to notice. Nobody interrupts him and nobody argues.

21. And still he is not killing them, for two reasons and neither is mercy — killing a Kemvimore means a war with Rosik that does not go well for him, and half his crew uses Master Fen's services and word getting round that he put the man in the ground is not a good look. So: that's it.

22. Tam brought in from a side chamber. Alive, conscious, walking, and barely. Dangerously thin, clothes hanging loose, the pallid stretched skin of weeks without food, bruises fading yellow-green, marks on the wrists from bindings. And the eyes, which are what closes Xion's chest: hollow, the recognition arriving slowly, like something pulled up from somewhere deep and dark. The name, rough and uncertain. Xion catching him as he stumbles, the boy far lighter than he should be.

23. And then the other thing takes over, the way it always does, before Xion has decided anything at all. He gets the boy down — a body that empty goes over the moment it stops being held — and goes to one knee. He asks to see the hand and Tam gives it without being asked twice, the way he did in a back room in the Middle District a lifetime ago. Six weeks in a collapsed tunnel have made this of Xion's own work: stitches gone, some pulled and the rest given up on, the wound opened along most of its length and closed again badly on its own terms, edges thickened and shining, the whole hand fuller than its fellow. He asks who has looked at this. Nobody answers, and he had not expected anyone to.

24. Eight years of arithmetic, stated once and never again: you never had the thing you wanted, you had four things you did not, and the craft was knowing which four and in what order. It has to come open. He tells Tam so, and that it will hurt, and does *not* tell him it will be quick, because it will not be. He braces the wrist, works the wound apart along the line it has already chosen, presses either side, gets out what has been shut in there since the surface. Tam makes a sound. Xion keeps talking through it — the weather, the colour of a door, whatever comes — the way he always talks.

25. The invoice, itemised in the prose without a word of comment. The flask is light, because most of it went on Varris's leg an hour ago; he spends what is left flushing the wound rather than putting it in the boy, hates the trade, and makes it. The last of the verbana, ground weeks ago, nowhere near enough. Linen from the bottom of the bag, the last of what he took off Janice's shelves before the smoke. He binds the wrist and the two fingers either side, because the wound will open every time the boy closes his fist and the only way to stop that is to take the closing away from him, and knots a sling from the same cloth.

26. It is good work and he knows it is good work; he has done it a thousand times with less, in a back room, by the light of one lamp. Then he lays the backs of his fingers against Tam's forehead and the good work stops mattering. Warm — not hot yet — and going the wrong way. He has come too late. Whatever was in that hand is in the blood and there is no poultice for blood; he can open and clean and dress and splint it and the fever climbs anyway, because it stopped being about the hand days ago. What he carries can buy hours. It cannot buy the boy.

27. He says the boy needs to be topside tonight — to Cullen, to Ronas, to the room — and Ronas says that is the arrangement, with no particular interest in it. Orders given, Cullen professional and efficient. This was it. They were leaving, with Tam, just leaving. And Tam's head finds the hollow of his shoulder inside a dozen steps and stays there, and through two layers of cloth Xion can feel exactly how warm he is. He starts counting the stairs. End there.

---

**On the limp, which is the book's cleanest instance of the density standard.** One action — Xion stops a Slaver escort to treat a man's calf — carrying five things at once with no line of dialogue decoding any of them. It shows the faculty operating without consent, since his feet slow before he decides. It shows the guardrail holding at maximum pressure, since he still asks. It shows him overriding the person keeping him alive, having first acknowledged she is right. It establishes what Master Fen is worth down here, in the only currency the Warrens deals in. And it spends most of the flask, which is the medicine Tam does not get an hour later. This is the passage to point at when anyone asks what the standard means.

**On what saves them, and why it must never look like a plan.** Varris vouches, Ronas says so plainly, and that is the reason all three of them walk out. Xion does not know Varris matters, does not know who summoned them, does not know anyone is watching. The instant the reader can see him calculating, the chapter is dead — the whole point is that the only thing that has ever worked for this man is the thing he does for no reason.

And it is not a debt discharged. Ronas's own framing is that Varris never spoke up for anyone in five years, which makes it a man acting out of character rather than a man settling an account. Same footing as the vendor and the enforcer in ch15: what is rare is not the treatment, it is that nobody asked him anything.

**On the flask, which is the cruellest thing in Book 1 and is never said aloud.** He spends most of his water on the leg of a man he has no reason to help, and an hour later there is not enough for a dying child, and he spends what remains flushing a wound instead of putting it into the boy. The kindness that gets them out of the Warrens alive is paid for out of Tam's ration. Nobody in the chapter notices, and nobody ever should. Do not add a beat where Xion realises it, in this book or a later one.

**On the treatment, and rule 1.** The work is good and the work fails, and both halves are load-bearing. An older version of the ending contradicted this — Tam described as solid and alive, after the paragraph establishing the fever was already in the blood — and it was cut. Do not reintroduce anything that reads as reassurance between the forehead and the stairs.

**On Ronas, whose guardrails are absolute.** He does not explain himself and encounters no resistance. He counts a pattern out on three fingers and nobody interrupts. He spares them for two reasons and neither is mercy: a war with Rosik he would lose, and a crew that uses Master Fen. Do not soften him, do not give him an interlocutor, do not let anyone win a point off him. His dialect is locked — see `People/Ronas Dermir.md`; the marker list and the Book 6 warning both apply, and the temptation to let him speak properly when he turns serious is exactly backwards.

**On the princess exchange, the only comedy in Act III.** Ronas reads the room, picks Farleen, and is wrong; Farleen for one heartbeat wants it; Elara claims it, meets a single sceptical word, and visibly deflates. It is funny, and it is three characterisations at once, and it must stay brief and go entirely unremarked afterward. Ronas is a superb reader of people who has read rank instead of blood, which is precisely Elara's problem for the whole book.

**On the ending, which is a recurrence that moves.** Xion counted thirty-one minutes in ch17 because counting was all he was good for. He counts stairs here while carrying the person he was good for and cannot save. Same man, same gesture, different position — that is the difference between recurrence and repetition. There is no third instance and there must not be one.

**On rhythm:** 2,964 words after ch17's 1,670. The contraction snaps and the chapter re-expands into the longest single scene of consequence in Act III. Ch19 runs 2,427 and drops the floor out again, so this is the last chapter in the book where anything goes according to a plan.
