# Chapter 14: First Blood

**Timeline:** Day 15, continuous with ch13. Night, in the Tiers.
**Characters:** Xion (POV), Elara, Farleen, three opportunists, Korvin
**Emotional work:** The Warrens stop being a wonder and become a place with rules. Elara demonstrates what she actually is, in under ten heartbeats, in front of witnesses — and the reader should feel the awe and the catastrophe arrive together. By the end, three people who need each other are angry at each other for three incompatible reasons, and none of it is resolved.
**POV:** Xion — close third-person limited. He cannot follow the fight and the prose must not follow it either.

---

## Beats

1. Down from the ledge into the Tiers, and the air thickens with each step — sweat, cooking fires, too many bodies in too little space. Xion has been down here before but never this deep and never at night, when scattered lamps make more shadows than light.

2. Farleen leads with practised caution through narrow streets between ramshackle buildings. People watch them pass with calculating eyes, noting clean clothes, uncertain movements, packs that mark them as fresh arrivals or easy targets. Her instruction is to stay close and try not to look like they are carrying anything valuable, and Xion's observation is that it is already too late.

3. Texture in three strokes, all of it indifferent rather than threatening: a barefoot child darting past and gone before he registers it; an old woman in a doorway with an empty bowl who is neither begging nor selling and watches them the way a person watches weather; metal ringing on metal somewhere, smithwork or something worse, impossible to tell.

4. A square with a broken fountain that no longer runs and is a gathering point anyway — trading, talking, the business that happens after dark in places the surface pretends do not exist. The dead fountain is worth noting against ch8 and nobody remarks on it.

5. Three men positioned across their path. Not blocking exactly — present, watching. Mismatched clothes, scavenged weapons, no cartel colours. Opportunists. Farleen says keep walking and do not make eye contact.

6. The leader steps out anyway: large, scarred hands, comfortable with his own authority here. The shakedown is delivered as reasonable conversation — nobody just passes through the Warrens, you either have business or you become business. His companions flank them.

7. The price is sixty silver, more than most Warrens families see in a year, and it is not meant to be paid. It is meant to be impossible, to justify what comes next. Xion reads that immediately and the narration says so plainly.

8. The threat made explicit and casual: if they cannot afford it there are other arrangements, a strong girl would fetch good coin, and those soft hands and that pretty face would fetch a decent price too. The casualness is the horror — this is not organised crime, just three men who have decided the three of them are prey.

9. He reaches for Elara's arm. She moves.

10. The fight, rendered as Xion's failure to follow it. Later he will try to reconstruct what happened and find he cannot. The leader stumbling back with a broken nose before Xion registers the movement. The second man ducked under, elbowed in the solar plexus, kneed in the face on the way down — and Elara coming up with his knife. The third drawing a rusted blade and Elara already moving, using the falling body as a pivot, the stolen knife flashing twice, the man clutching a slashed wrist. The leader recovering enough to draw and Elara catching his arm, twisting, and driving him face-first into the broken fountain hard enough that the impact echoes.

11. Silence. Three armed men down in maybe ten heartbeats. She stands over them breathing hard but controlled, the stolen knife still in her hand, blood on her borrowed dress that is not hers. The whole square has gone still, and everyone who was conducting business has stopped to stare.

12. Farleen's fury arrives before Xion's shock has finished. She demands to know what Elara just did; Elara says she dealt with the problem; Farleen reaches for her arm — and Elara's hand snaps up and catches her wrist before contact, pure reflex, controlled and unmistakably a combat response. They freeze. Elara releases her and says do not grab me.

13. Farleen wins the argument by ending it: move, now, before every pair of eyes in the square decides they are worth investigating. The crowd parts warily as they go, people backing away from the young woman who just destroyed three grown men. Xion follows, still processing the speed and the total absence of hesitation.

14. Korvin. An older man in a doorway, weathered and scarred, one eye milky and the other sharp and fixed on Xion with recognition. He says the name — Master Fen — and Xion knows the face: a dockworker he treated two years ago for a crushed hand that most healers would have amputated, and he spent hours reconstructing the bones and saved most of the function.

15. Korvin takes in all three of them — Xion's distress, Elara's blood-spattered dress, Farleen's vigilance — and the fight everyone behind them is still murmuring about, and offers shelter anyway. The debt from ch1's establishment of who Master Fen is comes back as the only door in the Warrens that opens for them, and it opens because of work Xion did years ago for a man he had not thought about since.

---

16. Inside, safe for the moment, and the chapter's real event begins: Farleen learns for the first time that they are not simply hiding. They are hunting for a boy Xion has never met, going deeper, into Slaver territory.

17. Her argument, and it is not cowardice and the chapter must not let it read as cowardice. They are fugitives. They cannot afford this. He is playing hero because that is what he does — Master Fen, saving everyone, fixing everything — and he cannot fix this and trying might get all of them killed.

18. Xion asks whether she would have him abandon the boy and she says yes, exactly that, and then catches herself before finishing a sentence about bigger problems. He reaches for we're already here and she refuses the framing outright: the Tiers are safe enough, they do not have to go deeper, that is a choice, and it is a stupid dangerous choice he is making for all of them.

19. Elara attacks from the other side — if Farleen does not care about helping people, why follow them down at all — and Farleen draws the distinction precisely: she did not say she does not care, she said they cannot afford it right now, and there is a difference.

20. Elara calls it cowardice and Farleen detonates. She stabbed him to maintain her cover. She betrayed everything she believed in for cartel interests. And when she finally decided to do the right thing she threw away her entire life, her family, her position, everything. So do not dare call her a coward, princess. Elara's counter — what do you call someone who runs when things get difficult — gets the honest answer: smart, because you cannot help anyone if you are dead, and sometimes the smart choice is surviving today to fight tomorrow.

21. She forces herself calm and makes the real offer, which is the strongest argument anyone makes in the chapter: stay in the Tiers, safe and hidden, wait a few weeks or a month for the surface to cool. They would be alive. They would have options. But if they go deeper and start asking about Slaver operations after that — a gesture toward the square — they may not get to wait anything out.

22. Xion says he knows. She says but you are going anyway. Yes. She turns to Elara and asks, with something desperate under the anger, whether she is fine with this. Yes, simply. Farleen looks between them with something like disbelief — two people giving her the same answer for reasons that have nothing to do with each other. She hopes the boy is worth it, because if they die down there it had better be for a damn good reason.

23. The room afterward, arranged by the fight: Farleen in a corner with her back to the wall where she can watch both the door and the window, movements sharp and angry. Elara as far from her as the space allows. Elara offers Xion that she thinks he is right to look, and he snaps that he does not need her approval, and she falls silent. He is not grateful to anybody in this chapter.

24. Xion by the cold stove, his shoulder aching where Farleen stabbed him days ago. Outside, the Warrens carry on — but word is spreading. Three outsiders in the Tiers: one who fights like imperial guard, one who moves with purpose and knowledge, one carrying a healer's bag who was recognised by locals. People will be talking, making connections, drawing conclusions. He looks at his companions — Farleen rigid with controlled fury, Elara silent and alert — both of them here because of him, both angry for different reasons.

---

**On the fight, and why the POV rule is doing the work rather than constraining it:** Xion cannot follow it, and the prose reconstructs it the way he does — after the fact, in fragments, with the honest admission that he could not track the movement. This is the correct application of the rule that a character reads the world with his own instrument: he is a healer, so what he retains is the sequence of injuries rather than the choreography. A version narrated with clear tactical geography would be a different man watching. It also means the reader shares his astonishment rather than being invited to admire the fighting.

**On why the display is a catastrophe and not a triumph:** she wins, immediately and completely, and it is the worst thing that happens in the chapter. Farleen's objection is entirely correct — the square was full of people, and what they saw was not a woman defending herself but a woman with twenty years of formal training. Ch15 opens with the Warrens watching them, and that is caused here. The chapter must give the reader the thrill and then take it away inside two exchanges.

**On Farleen's argument, which is the best case anyone makes and loses anyway:** she is right about the tactics, right about the risk, and right that Xion is doing this because of who he is rather than because it is wise. She offers a concrete, survivable alternative with a timeline. The chapter's honesty depends on the reader being able to agree with her — this is the same structural move as ch3, where Silvanno and Kael are correct and refuse anyway, and the same as Janice for eight years. Everyone who tells Xion to stop is right, and none of them stop him. That is the pattern the whole book is built on, and it appears here in its clearest form.

**On the two yeses that mean different things:** Xion is going because he failed the boy once already and cannot carry another one. Elara is going because a child is enslaved in the city she intends to rule and she has just decided what kind of empress that makes her. Farleen registers only that she is outnumbered by people whose reasons she cannot argue with, and the narration says exactly that much and no more.

**On Korvin, who is not a debt and must never be written as one:** he is two years of backstory delivered in four lines, and his function is that he simply helps. He does not weigh it, does not price it, and does not mark a limit — he opens a door to three people covered in somebody else's blood, and in ch15 and ch16 he keeps doing it. He is the counter-example to Xion's whole way of understanding people. The only person in this book keeping accounts is Xion, and the Warrens do not work the way he thinks they do; Master Fen matters down here because he is the one thing in a factional place with no faction, not because anyone owes him. Korvin proves it by never once treating the thing as settled.

**On rhythm:** 3,282 words, sitting inside the longest sustained block in the novel (ch13 through ch16 all run over 3,100). This is the middle of the descent and the chapters are meant to feel heavy and continuous — the reader should lose track of how long they have been underground, because Xion has.
