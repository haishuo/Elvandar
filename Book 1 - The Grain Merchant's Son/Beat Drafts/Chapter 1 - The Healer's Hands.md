# Chapter 1: The Healer's Hands

**Timeline:** Day 1. Before dawn to late night.
**Characters:** Xion Kemvimore ("Master Fen"), Mistress Janice, Tam Corris, three slaver-enforcers, a hooded woman at the edge of a crowd (Elara — unnamed, unrecognised, never named on the page)
**Emotional work:** The reader must finish holding three things at once: a child has been taken, Xion caused it, and he was warned in the same room ninety minutes before he did it. Not tragedy that befalls him — consequence he authored. They must also have seen the impossible thing themselves, on exactly the evidence he has and no more.
**POV:** Xion — close third-person limited, no exceptions anywhere in the chapter.

---

## Beats

1. Before dawn, in the back room of a weaver's shop that is not his, Xion grinds verbana to a powder fine enough to pass through cloth. Everything establishing him is action: he does the menial work himself, at an hour nobody asked him to keep, in heat with no window. He could buy powders; he makes them. The work is the point and also the hiding place. Janice exists at this stage only as a sound — he knows she is in the doorway working at something and does not look, does not know what. This is the chapter's contract with the reader and it is not rudeness: while Xion works, what he attends to is all the reader gets.

2. She announces a boy at the front, twelve, hand wrapped in a rag she wouldn't clean a table with, and opens the copper argument in the same breath. She has asked what he can pay, as she asks everyone. The boy said he would pay *back*, and she notes that a twelve-year-old already knows the difference. She is not arguing about the boy and never argues about the boy.

3. Xion turns to the shelf while she talks because it is easier than facing her, and takes stock: liquor, verbana, needle, gut, tweezers. He opens the linen box, finds one roll, closes it, says nothing about it. His supply failure is his own discovery in his own hands. Nobody tells the reader he is bad at logistics.

4. The argument reaches its floor. She wants one copper from anyone who has one, handed straight back out the door if he likes, because what she wants is the ledger entry rather than the money. He answers that they come to him precisely because no ledger exists. Both are correct; neither can hear the other, because his primary drive is her secondary and hers is his third. She wins his attention exactly once in the scene and has to work for it — something in how she delivers her next line turns him round, and he sees her face, not her hands. The line itself is the one that must land verbatim: a man who charges nothing is a man somebody else is paying for.

5. One paragraph of interiority on the name Fen, and it is the only place in the chapter the reader learns who he is. Son of Rosik Kemvimore, master of the Grain; here a pair of hands and a box of tinctures, and that is enough. Delivered as relief, not burden.

6. She notes she has never asked where the money comes from, and that not asking is work. Then Mira, obliquely — the woman whose name he took would not have argued about the copper — and his reply establishes they knew her the same way, well enough to disagree about her temperament. Her closer is the coldest thing anyone says to him in the book, an execution used as a bookkeeping argument. He says nothing. Two words was all it took.

7. The staging beat that makes the relationship legible, entirely in sound: the needle stops behind him, he does not answer, he goes back to the pestle, and after a while it starts again. She waited, got nothing, did not ask twice, resumed. Eight years in four sentences, and nobody comments — not her, not the narrator, not Xion, who registers only a noise.

8. Tam, small for twelve. Xion asks permission before touching him and then waits, and the boy checks the door before nodding, which tells the reader nobody has ever asked him first. The permission question is the guardrail line and it is planted here for two books.

9. The treatment. Glass in the palm, cleaned with liquor, and the boy does not flinch. Xion remarks on the courage and gets back a rule of the world rather than a personal circumstance — the boy uses generic second person, the way everyone he knows talks. The narration does not explain this afterward.

10. Seven stitches, and he dresses the hand with the last roll of linen and uses all of it, without hesitating and without mentioning it. Then, when the boy says his mother is sick, Xion presses a silver coin into his good hand. This is the chapter's densest action and it must carry everything at once without a line of dialogue decoding any of it: generosity; a man healing one hand and destroying the boy with the other; compulsion ninety minutes after being warned; his father's money funding the charity that answers his father's policy; the murder weapon; and a warning attached to it that he knows is useless as he gives it.

11. Behind him the needle stops, and this time it does not start again. Beat 7 built the baseline; this is the break in it, and her entire reaction to the catastrophe is a sound ceasing. She then names the mechanism before it operates — nobody in the Warrens can break a silver, so the boy will be asked where he got it, and they will ask. Xion opens his mouth and finds nothing in it, and the narration notes there never is, at this part, because the honest answer is that the patients are not the point and he cannot say that to her or to himself.

12. He goes to the basin. Blood off his hands, taking longer than it needs to, because there is nothing else to do with them. Only now, with his attention free, does he look up — and she is looking at him, and has been on and off for eight years, and he has never once asked why. The emotion in that look is never named. Then she returns to her work and he sees, for the first time, what is in her lap: one of his own tunics, brown, cut for a man who wants to be looked past, which she made and never charged him for, with a bad row in the sleeve she is taking out backward to reach the place she went wrong.

---

13. The day passes in work. Three times he sits to write the morning into the coded patient log that two people in Kaha'an can read, and three times gets as far as the date. The journals are planted here and burn in ch13.

14. Late afternoon, the market square, and he reads the crowd with his instrument before he reads it with his eyes: too dense at the middle, too still at the edges, everyone facing in. He has seen that shape at bedsides and knows what it means — the thing has already happened and nobody will be the one to move.

15. Tam against a wall, three enforcers, a document, a fabricated debt that includes charges for the care Xion gave free. He shouts that Master Fen does not charge, before he knows he has chosen to, and hears his own voice come back off the far wall — and in the half second of its return understands what he has said in public and to how many people. He does the exact thing Janice warned him about, out loud, in front of witnesses, and nobody backs him up. Asked for proof, he has it, four streets away in a cipher, and producing it means producing himself. He keeps his mouth shut.

16. The enforcer takes the boy by the arm above the wrist, and Xion watches Tam fold around the hand, and sees his own seven stitches and the linen going dark across them. His work destroyed in front of him, legible only to him. His hands close and he takes a half step. Then he does the arithmetic — three men, a square emptying, a boy who can be hurt far worse in the time it would take anyone to reach him — and stops, and stays stopped, and it is the correct decision, and being correct does nothing for him.

17. The woman at the edge of the crowd. Brown cloak, hood up in weather that does not call for one, and still in the particular way a body goes still when it has decided to move and is waiting to be told it can. He knows that stillness; eight years taught him to read what people do with themselves in the moment before they do something. Their eyes meet, her irises cycle blue to amber to violet and keep going, and he watches an impossible thing happen twice more before the crowd takes her. He never sees her face, and the text says so explicitly, because ch4 collects on it.

18. He gets two steps after her before the boy cries out behind him, and the two steps are the whole of the argument, and he turns back. Then the slaver finds the silver, and Xion watches the man's face change and knows the change from a hundred bedsides — not surprise, confirmation. The understanding arrives the way a diagnosis arrives, as the last explanation standing once the others are gone: the coin was never evidence of a debt, it was evidence of a source, and the paper was drawn up to give them a reason to ask where. He put it in the boy's hand himself, after being told, that morning, in a room he can nearly see from where he stands.

19. They take Tam out of the square. He watches until the corner takes him and cannot afterward account for the time between the corner and the light going. Three concrete details close the scene — stalls coming down, a woman sweeping, a child called in to eat — and the narrator passes no verdict on any of them.

---

20. The shop at lamplight, Janice still at the same sleeve. He reports; she has already heard, and what she heard was a man shouting in a market square about Master Fen's billing practices. She corrects his account of the arrest, telling him they charged the boy because he had a silver and no story for it and invented the rest afterward. He cannot find the shape of the argument. He says he will get the boy back and she asks with what, not unkindly, which is the worst of it. Her verdict is the most accurate sentence anyone says about him in Act I and it turns on his inability to say his own name in public — a thing he has known for eight years and is somehow still surprised by. She holds the row up to the lamp, studies the gap where the bad thread was, and sends him home.

21. His rented room, the city quiet. Tam's face and a woman he cannot name. Two impossible things. Palms pressed together, knuckles white, and a promise to himself that tomorrow he will act.

---

**On the ending, which is not flat and must not be "fixed":** the closing promise is a man who did nothing telling himself he will do something tomorrow — presumably the same thing he has been telling himself since he was fifteen. Chapter 2 ironises it inside a page, because he does act, and it is for the princess rather than the boy. The general test for any forward-leaning last line in this series is whose sentence it is. If the character is lying to himself it stays. If the narrator is advertising the next chapter it goes.

**On Xion's paralysis, and why it must never be explained:** the freeze in the market is not cowardice. It is the same machinery that makes him a great healer, running correctly and returning the answer *no*. That reading belongs in the notes and nowhere on the page. Any sentence of the form *paying it would have required being somebody, and being somebody required an account of the money* converts a freeze into an alibi and tells the reader he had no choice. Janice's verdict is an accusation he cannot answer; it must never become an explanation he can.

**On Janice, who is a camera problem before she is anything else:** she has the best lines in the chapter and is not its subject. Everything she does arrives through his ears while his hands are busy, and through his eyes only once the patients are gone. An earlier version described her sewing in the second paragraph while he was grinding, which is a point-of-view error rather than a weighting one, and it is the reason the whole opening read as hers. The rule generalises: what Xion attends to is what the reader attends to, established here in the first three pages, before anyone knows it is a rule.

**On the plant list, all of it load-bearing downstream:** verbana ground by hand returns in ch18, carried for weeks and spent on the same wound. Tam Corris — twelve, left hand, glass, seven stitches — returns in ch12, ch18 and ch25. The coded journals burn in ch13. The permission question breaks for the first time in ch19 and runs through Book 2. The stillness and the phrase about a body deciding whether to move are collected in ch4, as is the fact that he never saw her face. The colour sequence and the brown cloak are both cited back in ch2 and ch3, so neither detail is free to drift.

**On what this chapter refuses to do:** it never moves the camera off Xion, not once, and the market sequence is where the old draft kept reaching for it — bloodstains drying in an alley he has walked away from, eyes watching somewhere in the darkness, the narrator grading the evening's meaning. All of that is gone and none of it should return.

**On rhythm:** 2,516 words — the longest chapter in the opening movement and the longest anywhere before ch9. Ch1–8 run 1,453–2,516 with a mean near 1,900, so this sits at the top of its own band, which is correct: an opening chapter is allowed to be the widest in its movement because everything after it is a narrowing. Four scenes, six plants and the book's POV contract have to fit here. Ch2 drops to 1,913 and the book does not exceed 2,516 again until ch9's 3,695.
