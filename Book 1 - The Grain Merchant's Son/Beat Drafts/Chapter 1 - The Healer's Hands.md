# Chapter 1: The Healer's Hands

**Timeline:** Day 1. Before dawn to late night.
**Characters:** Xion Kemvimore ("Master Fen"), Mistress Janice, Tam Corris, three slaver-enforcers, a hooded woman at the edge of a crowd (Elara — unnamed, unrecognised, never named on the page)
**Emotional work:** The reader must finish this chapter holding three things at once: that a child has been taken, that **Xion caused it**, and that he was warned in the same room ninety minutes before he did it. Not tragedy that befalls him — consequence he authored. And they must have seen the impossible thing with their own eyes, on exactly the evidence he has and no more, so that ch2's skeptics can be argued with.
**POV:** Xion — close third-person limited. **No exceptions in this chapter.** Rule 0 is absolute here because this is where the contract with the reader is signed.

---

## Beats

1. Before dawn, in the back room of a weaver's shop that is not his, Xion grinds verbana to a powder fine enough to pass through cloth. Establish by action only: he does the menial work himself, at an hour nobody asked him to keep, in heat with no window. He could buy powders. He grinds them. The work is the point and it is also the hiding place — the last clause should say the work is better than the world outside and stop there, without explaining what is wrong with the world outside.

2. Janice exists, at first, **as a sound.** He knows she is in the doorway working at something; he does not look and does not know what. This is not indifference and must not read as rudeness — it is the load-bearing fact of the character: **when Xion is working, what he attends to is all the reader gets.** Do not describe her hands, her lap, or her work anywhere in this scene.

3. She announces a boy at the front — twelve, left hand wrapped in a filthy rag — and the copper argument opens on the same breath. She has asked him what he can pay, as she asks everyone. The boy said he would pay *back*. She notes that a twelve-year-old already knows the difference. She is not arguing about the boy. She never argues about the boy.

4. Xion turns to the shelf while she talks — *because it is easier than facing her* — and takes stock. Liquor, verbana, needle, gut, tweezers. He opens the linen box and finds **one roll**, and closes it, and says nothing. His supply failure is his own discovery, in his own hands, unannounced. Nobody tells the reader he is bad at logistics.

5. The argument reaches its floor: she wants one copper from anyone who has one, handed straight back out the door if he likes, because what she wants is **a number in the book**. He answers that they come to him *because* there is no number in the book. Both are right. Neither can hear the other, because his primary is her secondary and her primary is his third.

6. She wins his attention exactly once, and has to work for it — something in how she says the next line turns him round. He sees her **face**, not her hands: eyes up, flat and patient. *A man who charges nothing is a man somebody else is paying for.* Then he goes back to the shelf.

7. *Fen.* One paragraph of interiority on the name, and it is the only place in the chapter where the reader learns who he really is. He is the son of Rosik Kemvimore, master of the Grain, and here he is a pair of hands and a box of tinctures, and that is enough. Delivered as relief, not as burden.

8. She notes she has never asked where the money comes from, and that not asking is work. Then Mira, obliquely — *the woman whose name you took wouldn't have argued with me about the copper* — and his answer establishes they knew her the same way, well enough to disagree about her temperament. Her closer is the coldest line anyone says to him in the book: *and look how that ended*. He says nothing. Two words was all it took.

9. **The staging beat that makes the whole relationship legible, and it is entirely in sound.** The needle stops somewhere behind him. He does not answer. He goes back to the pestle. After a while it starts again. She waited, she got nothing, she did not ask twice, she resumed. Eight years in four sentences and nobody comments — not her, not the narrator, not Xion, who registers only a noise.

10. Tam. Small for twelve. *"Can I have a look?"* — and then **he waits**, and the boy checks the door before nodding, which tells the reader nobody has ever asked him first. This is the guardrail line and it is planted here for two books.

11. The treatment. Glass in the palm, cleaned with liquor, and the boy does not flinch. Xion remarks on it and gets the chapter's flattest and worst sentence back: *"Can't work if you cry."* Generic second person — a rule of the world, not a personal circumstance. Do not explain it afterward.

12. Seven stitches. He dresses the hand with the linen from the box and uses **all of it**. He spends his last on the patient in front of him without hesitating and without mentioning it.

13. **The chapter's Tywin action.** Tam says his mother is sick; Xion presses a silver coin into the boy's **good hand**. One gesture carrying six things at once — generosity; he heals one hand and destroys the boy with the other; compulsion, ninety minutes after being warned; the money is his father's, so the charity is funded by the system that made the boy scavenge; it is the murder weapon; and the warning he attaches to it (*don't let anyone see it if you can help it*) is a useless hedge he knows is useless. **No line of dialogue decodes any of this.**

14. Behind him, the needle stops — and this time it does **not** start again. Beat 9 established the baseline; this is the break in it. Her whole reaction to the catastrophe is a sound ceasing.

15. Janice names the mechanism before it operates: nobody in the Warrens can break a silver, so where will the boy say he got it, and they will ask. Xion opens his mouth and finds nothing in it. *There never was, at this part* — because the honest answer is that the patients are not the point, and he cannot say that to her or to himself.

16. He goes to the basin. Blood off his hands, longer than it needs to take, because there is nothing else to do with them. **Only now, with his attention free, does he look up** — and she is looking at him, and has been on and off for eight years, and he has never once asked why. **Do not name the emotion in that look.** Then she goes back to her work and he sees, for the first time in the chapter, what is in her lap: one of his own tunics, brown, cut for a man who wants to be looked past, which she made and never charged him for, with a bad row in the sleeve she is taking out backward to reach the place she went wrong. Six meanings, one object, no dialogue.

---

17. The day passes in work. Three times he sits down to write the morning into the coded patient log two people in Kaha'an can read, and three times gets as far as the date. The journals are planted here for ch13.

18. Late afternoon, the market square. **He reads the crowd with his instrument before he reads it with his eyes** — too dense at the middle, too still at the edges, everyone facing in. He has seen that shape at bedsides. It means the thing has already happened and nobody is going to be the one to move.

19. Tam against a wall, three enforcers, a document, a debt of seven silver four copper for medical care he was given free. Xion shouts *Everyone knows Master Fen doesn't charge* before he knows he has chosen to, and hears his own voice come back off the far wall, and understands in the half second of its return exactly what he has said in public and to how many people. **He does the thing Janice warned him about, out loud, in front of witnesses.** Nobody backs him up.

20. Asked for proof, he has it — six journals four streets away — and producing it means producing himself. He keeps his mouth shut.

21. The enforcer takes Tam by the arm, above the wrist, and Xion watches the boy fold around the hand, and sees **the seven stitches he put in that palm that morning and the linen going dark across them.** His own work destroyed in front of him, legible only to him.

22. His hands close. He takes a half step. **Then he does the arithmetic** — three men, a square emptying, a boy who can be hurt far worse in the time it would take anyone to cross to him — and stops, and stays stopped, and it is **the correct decision**, and being correct does nothing for him. The freeze is not cowardice. It is the same machinery that makes him a great healer, returning *no*. **This reading stays off the page for the whole of Book 1.**

23. The woman at the edge. Brown cloak, hood up in weather that does not call for one, and **still** in the particular way a body goes still when it has decided to move and is waiting to be told it can. He knows that stillness; eight years taught him to read it. Their eyes meet. Blue, amber, violet, and it does not stop, and he watches it happen twice more before the crowd takes her. **He never sees her face** — plant this explicitly for ch4.

24. Two steps after her. Then Tam cries out, and the two steps are the whole of the argument, and he turns back. He chooses the boy over the miracle, but only just, and the reader has watched him try.

25. The slaver finds the silver. Xion watches the man's face change and knows the change from a hundred bedsides: **not surprise — confirmation.** And it arrives the way a diagnosis arrives, as the last explanation standing: the coin was never evidence of a debt, it was evidence of a **source**, and the paper was drawn up to give them a reason to ask where. He put it in the boy's hand himself, after being told, that morning, in a room he can nearly see from here.

26. They take Tam. He watches until the corner takes him and cannot afterward account for the time between the corner and the light going. Three concrete details close the scene — stalls, a woman sweeping, a child called in to eat — and **the narrator passes no verdict on them.**

---

27. The shop at lamplight. Janice still at the same sleeve. He reports; she has already heard, and what she heard was a man shouting about Master Fen's billing practices. She corrects his account of the arrest: they charged the boy because he had a silver and no story for it, and made the rest up after. He cannot find the shape of the argument.

28. *"I'll get him back."* — *"With what?"* Not unkindly; there is no cruelty in her at all, and that is the worst of it. **The most accurate sentence anyone says about him in Act I:** *you couldn't say your own name out there, and you've known it eight years, and you're going to stand in my shop and be surprised.* She holds the row up to the lamp and studies the gap where the bad thread was. *Go home, Fen.*

---

29. His rented room. Tam's face and a woman he cannot name. **Two impossible things.** Palms pressed together, knuckles white. *Tomorrow, he would act.*

---

**The ending is not flat and must not be "fixed."** *Tomorrow, he would act* is a man who did nothing promising himself tomorrow — the same evasion he has presumably been making since he was fifteen — and ch2 ironises it inside a page, because he does act and it is for the princess, not for the boy. The test for any "tomorrow" line in this series: if the character is lying to himself it stays; if the narrator is advertising the next chapter it goes.

**Do not give Xion a structural explanation for his own paralysis.** Not here and not anywhere in Book 1. Any sentence of the form *paying it would have required being somebody, and being somebody required an account of the money* converts a freeze into an alibi and tells the reader he had no choice. Janice's *you couldn't say your own name* is an accusation he cannot answer; it must never become an explanation he can.

**Janice is a camera problem before she is anything else.** She has the best lines in the chapter and she is not the subject of it. Everything she does must arrive through Xion's ears while his hands are busy, and through his eyes only once the patients are gone. The chapter previously described her sewing in paragraph two, while he was grinding — a POV error, not a weighting one, and the reason the whole opening read as hers.

**What Xion attends to is what the reader attends to.** B1 ch21 is the model: the Mistress walks into the chamber and he never notices, because he is treating Tam, and he shushes Farleen for trying to tell him. That rule is established here, in the first three pages, before anyone knows it is a rule.

**The plant list, all of it load-bearing downstream:** verbana ground by hand → ch18, carried for weeks and spent on the same wound. Tam Corris, twelve, left hand, glass, seven stitches → ch12, ch18, ch25. The coded journals → ch13, burned. The copper argument → ch13, conceded and inverted. *Can I have a look?* → ch19's first break, and Book 2. The stillness and *a body deciding whether to move* → ch4. *He never saw her face* → ch4. Blue → **amber** → violet, and a **brown** cloak → ch2 and ch3 both cite these.

**One deliberate rule 0 latitude, and it is small:** none. This chapter has no exceptions. The market sequence is the place the old draft kept reaching for the camera — bloodstains drying behind him, eyes watching in the darkness — and every one of those has been removed.
