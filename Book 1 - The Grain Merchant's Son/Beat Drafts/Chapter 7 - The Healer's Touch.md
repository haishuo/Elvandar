# Chapter 7: The Healer's Touch

**Timeline:** Day 8, the second day of their partnership. Afternoon into night.
**Characters:** Xion (POV), Elara, Mistress Janice (silent throughout)
**Emotional work:** The chapter inverts him. Xion is the healer, and here he is the patient, talked through his own treatment by someone using his own supplies in his own clinic while he pretends the place belongs to a friend. The reader should finish it with the lie feeling heavier than the wound, and with the first genuine warmth between these two arriving in a room where he is actively deceiving her.
**POV:** Xion — close third-person limited, and he is febrile for most of it, so the prose should be no more reliable than he is.

---

## Beats

1. By the second day the fever cannot be ignored. He has been pushing through it, explaining well permits and distribution quotas with sweat on his forehead, and the thing that ends the pretence is not a confrontation. Elara catches him swaying against a wall and simply stops walking — no question, no alarm — and waits for him to admit it. Her method throughout the chapter is refusal to perform concern.

2. She calls it: infected, and the bandage has not been changed. She asks when he last checked it. He does not answer, which is answer enough.

3. She proposes finding supplies, and Xion interrupts with an idea forming through the fever: he knows a place, a friend runs a clinic nearby, discreet about his patients. The narration lets the reader see the shape of the lie without letting Xion admit it — Master Fen was his friend once, the clinic is his, and he is being discreet about his patients by pretending to be one of them.

4. She studies him, sceptical, and asks whether the friend will ask questions. He says the friend will not mind. This is the chapter's engine: every true statement he makes is a lie by construction, and he has to keep producing them while running a fever.

5. The walk is longer than he remembers. His vision swims. His hands find the latch of the back entrance by muscle memory, which is a detail she is standing close enough to see.

6. Janice, and this is her whole appearance in the chapter. She is at the counter with a bolt of undyed linen half-measured across it. She looks up and takes in three things: Xion grey-faced and sweating, a strange young woman holding him upright, and a door he has opened without knocking. Xion says nothing at all. Her eyes go to Elara and stay a moment longer than politeness allows. Then she sets the linen down, picks up her shears, and moves to the front window, where she begins cutting a length she has no order for. She asks nothing and permits everything, and the reader who has read ch1 understands exactly what she is doing.

7. Through the beaded curtain. Elara observes that his friend's shopkeeper does not ask questions either. He agrees that she does not. This is as close as the chapter comes to letting him tell the truth.

8. The clinic itself, seen through her eyes and his fever: labelled tinctures, clean bandages in rows, dried herbs and antiseptic. Home in a way his rented room has never been — a fact he thinks and does not say.

9. She notes the friend is well-supplied; he says the friend is thorough. He moves to the cabinet and his hands find the right bottles without looking. She notes he seems very familiar with the system. He says he has helped before, that the friend showed him where everything is, and the narration observes that the lie came easier than it should have.

10. He directs her to sit, on the grounds that he cannot see his own shoulder, and adds that anyone travelling with a man who gets stabbed should learn basic wound care. She asks whether being stabbed is a regular occurrence. The banter is the first easy thing between them and it happens because he is too ill to manage distance.

11. The treatment, and it must be procedurally real. He talks her through it in the clinical register Mira taught him — the old bandage comes off fast rather than gently, because quick and clean beats slow and kind. She asks if he is ready. He tells her to do it. She pulls; he bites back a curse; she apologises; he tells her she did fine.

12. He makes her describe what she sees rather than describing it himself: red, swollen, pus. He is teaching while being treated, which is the only way this man knows how to be helped.

13. The alcohol. He tells her to pour it directly in and not to stop halfway. The first splash whites out his vision and he grips the workbench hard enough to crack his knuckles — and she does not stop, does not hesitate, keeps pouring until the wound is clean. Her Arol Batae training is visible here as the absence of squeamishness rather than as skill.

14. The shirt. She tells him it has to come off; he knows she is right and the thought of being that exposed closes something in his chest. He reaches for the buttons and his fingers will not cooperate. She moves his hands aside and does it herself, quick and impersonal — and when her fingers brush bare skin he feels something that has nothing to do with fever. He fixes his eyes on the far wall and tells himself it is medical necessity.

15. She works with steady hands, asks clarifying questions, does not rush him. He says she is good at this without thinking. She credits her teachers: the Arol Batae hold that an empress should understand battlefield medicine. Every intimate beat in this scene is produced by competence rather than by tenderness, which is what keeps it from being a love scene in a sickroom.

16. She probes, gently and repeatedly, and he deflects. The clinic is impressive for a back-room operation; the friend is particular about organisation. How long has he known him; long enough to trust him with his life, which is true. Why not regular physicians; because of what his father would do if he knew. He tells her the truth about himself while attributing it to somebody else, which is the most Xion thing in the chapter.

17. The moment she almost has him, and it is a professional observation rather than an accusation. She notes that everything is labelled twice — once in script and once in something that is not script — and identifies it as a cipher. He answers neutrally that Master Fen takes his work seriously and believes everyone deserves care regardless of station. She says the friend sounds worth knowing. He agrees, and the narration notes the words feel both true and false.

18. She finishes the bandage and moves around to face him, still close enough that he can hear her breathing change. He notices the faint scar on her chin and how much of her stillness is training and how much is effort. She reaches up and he thinks for a moment she might touch his face; instead her fingers check the bandage's security with professional detachment.

19. He thanks her for not letting him pretend it was fine. She says someone has to keep him from bleeding out through stubbornness, then adds that she needs him alive because he is her only guide. He asks if that is the only reason — the question is out before he can think better of it — and she answers simply that it is not. The moment holds and neither of them moves, and Xion runs through everything he should be remembering: she is the imperial heir, he is the son of her enemy, and the last woman who loved him put a dagger in his shoulder.

20. She breaks it by ordering him to rest, in the register that expects obedience: they will stay the night, and Master Fen will not mind them using his clinic while he is away. He agrees that he will not, and the irony is not lost on him. He eases onto the cot he has slept on more nights than he can count, which she does not need to know. The chapter closes on sound: the creak of old wood, dried herbs, the city beyond — and out front, well past the hour she usually closes, the small dry rhythm of Janice's shears going on and on over cloth nobody ordered.

---

**On the lie, which is the chapter's real subject:** the wound is the occasion, not the story. Xion spends this entire chapter inside his own clinic being cared for by someone he is deceiving about whose clinic it is, and every kindness she does him is therefore also a thing he is stealing. The reader should be uncomfortable in a way neither character is. This is what makes ch11's confession land as relief rather than as revelation — she has already worked most of it out, and he already knows the lie costs more than the truth would.

**On Janice, who says nothing and is the most eloquent person in the chapter:** she gets one paragraph, no dialogue, and three actions — she looks a beat too long at Elara, she sets down the linen, and she takes up shears at the front window over cloth nobody ordered. That is a woman posting herself as a lookout without being asked and without acknowledging that she has done it. Her ch1 discipline holds exactly: she asks the money question and never the identity question, and she extends the same silence to a stranger her tenant has walked in on his own key. **Do not give her a line here.** The shears going on past closing at the chapter's end is her entire character rendered as a noise, and it is a recurrence of ch1's needle in a changed position — there it measured his failure, here it guards his lie.

**On why the intimacy works and how it breaks:** every beat that brings them closer is generated by *procedure* — she has to remove the shirt because the fabric is in the way, she checks the bandage rather than touching his face, she is good at this because the Arol Batae train for battlefield medicine. The tension exists in the gap between what the actions would mean anywhere else and what they mean here. The moment the prose reaches for the feeling directly — anything electric, anything fragile as spun glass — it stops being a scene about two competent people in a small room and becomes an ordinary romance beat. **Prefer the professional register every time; the reader will do the rest.**

**On Elara's near-catch, which is a plant and not a scene:** the cipher observation is the single most dangerous thing said in the chapter and it is delivered while she is tying a bandage. She notices, names it accurately, and lets it go — the same behaviour as ch5's ledger question, and the second demonstration that she files things she has no immediate use for. It pays in ch11 when she confronts him about the key, and it pays again in ch13 when the journals turn out to be the thing worth burning.

**On the fever as a POV instrument:** he is unreliable for most of this chapter and that is a licence the book should use rather than apologise for. Sounds too loud, colours too bright, distance and immediacy at once. It is also why he tells her as much as he does, and why the chapter can be warm without either of them deciding to be.

**On rhythm:** 1,977 words, just above the ch1–8 mean and near-identical to ch8's 1,898. These two are the last chapters before the plan that destroys everything, and ch7 is the one where nothing external happens at all — a fever and a conversation. That is the point. Ch9 then nearly doubles the movement's length and the book never comes back down to this register.
