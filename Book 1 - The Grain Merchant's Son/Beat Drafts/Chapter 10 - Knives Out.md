# Chapter 10: Knives Out

**Timeline:** Day 13. Evening into night — the masquerade at the Larannas estate.
**Characters:** Xion (POV), Elara as "Lady Sarif", Tania Larannas, Rosik Kemvimore, Silvanno, Lord Markus Tervan, Councilor Harvik, assorted nobles
**Emotional work:** Everything Xion built in ch9 works, and then it does not matter. The reader should feel the evening tightening by degrees — survivable, survivable, survivable — and then break in one sentence, on a subject nobody could have drilled for. And they should understand that Elara's control fails not because she is weak but because she is looking at something no amount of preparation covers.
**POV:** Xion — close third-person limited. He is running the operation, so the chapter's tension is his continuous assessment of whether it is holding.

---

## Beats

1. The estate blazing, every window lit. Xion hands Elara down from a hired carriage and is acutely aware that her hand trembles slightly in his. Behind a mask of blue silk and silver wire, her eyes are carefully, perfectly blue. Open on the thing the whole chapter is about — the eyes are already work.

2. The ballroom, established through waste rather than grandeur: marble, tapestries depicting trade wars that starved thousands, and water everywhere indoors. Xion narrates the room for her as a guide, which is his function all evening and the reason the reader can read it too.

3. The younger nobles by a water feature — children of cartel leaders, and Xion's assessment is that they are the dangerous ones, old enough to have power and young enough to use it carelessly. A woman in a bird mask sloshes wine onto marble and a servant appears instantly to clean it; she never looks down.

4. Elara sets her own glass on a passing tray with great care, as though it were the one thing in the room she could still govern. Her control is being shown as a physical act with objects, not as an internal state, and this is the register for her whole performance.

5. Xion pulls her onto the dance floor before she forgets where she is. The musicians are playing something slow and formal — ideal for conversation while appearing to court, which is exactly the cover ch9 built. She says this is worse than the fountains and he agrees.

6. Laughter from the refreshment tables: a story about a servant who tried to steal food and the creative punishment that followed. The audience finds it hilarious. She says she wants to leave; he tells her twenty minutes in would draw attention; she says she does not care; he tells her she does. He meets her eyes, they are blue, and holding them blue is visibly costing her. She takes a breath and holds it the way she practised.

7. An alcove table where they can observe without being surrounded, and Xion delivers the chapter's most useful analysis: most of them are not evil, they are comfortable, and they do not question it because questioning would be uncomfortable. Those who cannot be comfortable either adapt or are destroyed. The system does not tolerate dissent.

8. Lord Terrik and his wife dance past, and Xion annotates them: generous to temples, sponsors of festivals, funders of public works — and supporters of the policy that doubled bread prices last winter while their warehouses stayed full. They probably do not remember it. Just another business decision. The annotation method is the chapter's engine and it works because Xion has known these people his whole life.

9. She asks how he bears being one of them, and he answers that Xion Kemvimore exists here but is not real, only a mask he wears to move through their world. Asked what is under it, he says he tries to be someone who would make Master Fen proud — which is the closest he comes to the truth all evening, delivered as a deflection, to the one person it would actually mean something to.

10. Rosik arrives, and the room reorganises around him. No mask at all — his authority too absolute for pretence. Tania hurries to welcome him with genuine warmth. Elara studies him with the same intensity she brought to the fountains and observes that he does not look like a monster; Xion says monsters rarely do, and that his father believes he is maintaining order and protecting the city from chaos. By controlling its resources, which in his mind is a different thing from starving its people. Rosik's gaze sweeps the room and for a moment Xion thinks it might land on them. It does not.

11. Circulating. Xion introduces Elara to carefully chosen nobles — people unlikely to have Uratha connections, whose conversation will be predictable. She plays the slightly bored merchant's daughter perfectly. The cover is working, and the chapter needs the reader to believe that before it stops working.

12. Tania finds them at the refreshment tables, and Xion understands at once that it is not chance — she was the length of the ballroom away five minutes ago, and hosts do not drift. She opens on the marriage cover: he has hardly let Lady Sarif out of his sight all evening, and she has been watching. Every word of it is true and none of it true for the reason she means, and Xion feels the floor of the lie shift slightly.

13. The realisation that is the chapter's best piece of character work: he spent three days preparing Elara for insults — the fan, the wine glass, answering condescension with condescension — and not one minute on what to do when somebody is kind to her about him. There was no lesson for that. He did not think of one, and standing here he understands precisely why he hadn't.

14. Tania presses, pleasantly, asking Elara whether he is attentive or merely thorough, because one likes to know which sort one is dealing with before anything is settled. Elara answers that he is both and very difficult to distract. Tania laughs, files whatever she came for, and leaves entirely satisfied — and does not appear to notice that in the whole exchange neither of them looked at the other once. They survived it. Xion is considerably less sure what they survived, or why it cost him more than the whole hour before it.

15. Silvanno, near the garden entrance, frozen mid-conversation with his glass halfway to his lips. It slips and shatters. He gets out that they thought Xion was — and cannot finish. Xion cuts him off: not here, not now. Silvanno's mask cannot hide that his eyes are wide and darting; Farleen told him Xion was attacked. Xion grips his arm and promises they will talk.

16. Silvanno's facade cracks entirely — he and Kael have been searching for days — and then he takes a shuddering breath, straightens his mask, and produces a mechanical greeting for Lady Sarif with his attention still locked on Xion. He extracts a promise: his study, after midnight, and don't you dare disappear again. As he walks away he stops to order a servant to clean the glass he broke. Elara observes that he cares about Xion; Xion says he has put a good friend in an impossible position.

17. The evening wears on and her control degrades visibly — the set of her shoulders, the tension in her jaw, the way her eyes want to shift and only iron will keeps them blue. The chapter should feel like a held breath here.

18. The Great Hunt. It starts as ordinary season-planning near the garden doors: Lord Tervan proposing they revive the tradition, someone recalling his father hosted the last one before The Rending, warm nostalgia about three days in the royal forests and the thrill of the chase. A younger woman mentions that the royal family once let commoners hunt alongside nobility, sharing the forest as equals. Tervan waves this away — they have adapted the tradition, made it more appropriate to current circumstances.

19. Elara goes very still beside him. Then a noble in a snake mask supplies the specifics without being asked: the Warrens provide ample prey, and it gives the rabble something to think about, keeps them afraid. Tervan moves to logistics — selection process, hunting grounds, wagering pools.

20. She asks, perfectly controlled and perfectly polite, whether she understood correctly: do they hunt people. Tervan's answer is indulgent and is the worst thing anyone says in the book — not people precisely, but criminals and debtors, the sort who would be enslaved anyway, and this way they get a sporting chance, more than the mines or quarries would give them.

21. She executes the training flawlessly and it destroys her. She repeats a sporting chance flat, and then delivers a line in exactly the light register Xion spent three days teaching her — that the wagering must be the difficult part, so hard to set fair odds when the field is starving — turning her glass a quarter turn the way he showed her, the way a woman does when a conversation has begun to bore her. For one heartbeat it lands as wit. Tervan's mouth moves toward a smile. The snake-masked noble begins to laugh.

22. Then he stops, because he has looked at her eyes. She is still smiling, her voice has not changed, her hand on the glass is perfectly steady — and her eyes have gone amber, then violet, cycling with nothing behind them she can reach. The conversation stops mid-word. A glass shatters. Understanding ripples outward, because everyone knows what colour-changing eyes mean.

23. Tervan breathes that it is impossible. The snake-masked noble pulls his mask aside and is Councilor Harvik, one of Rosik's inner circle. Xion says they need to leave. It is already too late: recognition spreads like circles on water, conversations die, and across the ballroom his father's head turns toward the commotion. Harvik calls for guards.

24. Her hand finds his. She asks run? He says run. They bolt for the garden entrance, her skirts hampering a warrior's instincts, shouts and mobilising guards behind them.

25. The gardens give shadow, hedges and multiple paths, and Xion knows these grounds from childhood parties — including the weak point in the eastern wall where the stones have shifted, which ch9 planted two chapters earlier. Through the hedge line, through ornamental gardens, startling couples among the roses. Twelve feet of rough stone; he boosts her and she scales it with twenty years of training, then reaches down for him. For a moment they balance on top with the estate on one side and the city on the other, and below them guards pour through the hedge line.

26. Silvanno, shocked to find him alive, promised an explanation Xion will not now be there to give. The alarm bells start behind them — the Larannas house first, then the district, then the whole Noble Quarter ringing into the dark. Xion runs, and understands as he runs that whatever they had been doing in this city until tonight, they are not doing it anymore.

---

**On where this sits and why it is long:** ch9 and ch10 are the two longest chapters in the first half, 3,700 and 3,100 against an eight-chapter opening movement averaging under 1,900. Then ch11 collapses to 1,776. That expansion-and-collapse is the book's largest structural gesture so far, and the ball only reads as an event because the preparation was allowed to take up room. **Do not compress either half of it.**

**On the failure, which must not read as a lapse in discipline:** every drill in ch9 works. She takes the coded insult, she holds her eyes through the servant story, she survives a direct probe from the most dangerous woman in the room, and when the Hunt comes up she produces a genuinely excellent line in exactly the register she was taught. **She does not fail at the training. The training is not the relevant instrument.** What breaks her is that she is standing in a room where hunting human beings is a scheduling question, and there is no version of the preparation that covers it. This is why the moment lands as horror rather than as a mistake, and it is why beat 21 must give her the line and let it work for one heartbeat before the eyes are noticed.

**On Tania's probe, which is not plot:** she gets nothing, tells no one, and leaves satisfied. Its entire function is to put the marriage cover under load and reveal that the cover has begun to hold up something real — the training covered insult and did not cover kindness, and Xion knows exactly why he never thought of that lesson. **The detail that carries it is that neither of them looks at the other for the whole exchange**, which is the tell they both produce and neither can see.

**On Rosik in this chapter:** he does not speak, does not approach, and never learns his son is present. He arrives, the room rearranges, his gaze passes over them, and he turns toward a commotion at the end. That is correct and must not be improved into a confrontation. The reader needs him established as a physical fact in Xion's world before Book 2 makes him the antagonist, and the near-miss is worth more than a scene.

**On the escape geography, planted in ch9:** the eastern wall was set up two chapters earlier precisely so it is not produced from nowhere mid-flight. Keep both ends.

**On rhythm:** 3,139 words, the second half of the largest block in the first half of the book: ch9 and ch10 together run 6,834 words, more than the whole of ch1–5. Then ch11 drops to 1,776. **Long-then-short is this novel's signature for a disaster and its aftermath** and it is used three times — ch9–10 into ch11, ch16 into ch17, and ch18–19 into ch20. Keep the pattern legible.
