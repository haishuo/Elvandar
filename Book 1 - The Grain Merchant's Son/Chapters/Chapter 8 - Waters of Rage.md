The fever had broken in the night. Elara had spent it on watch with her back against the clinic door, and when Xion woke she was already up, already dressed, already reading the shutters the way she read everything now — for the ways out.

"Where today?"

"North." He worked his arm into the sling she had made him wear and found the shoulder would take it. "The Noble District."

"I've seen the Noble District."

"You've seen a wall in it."

---

They went north through the waking Middle District, and Xion took the long way because the long way had fewer patrols, and somewhere in the second hour he began noticing things he had walked past every day of his life.

A woman in a doorway washing greens in a bowl the size of two cupped hands. When she finished she carried the bowl out to a clay pot by the step and poured it over whatever was growing there. Not tipping it away. Moving it.

A man drinking from a skin who stopped before he was finished.

Jars in the shaded halves of doorways, under damp cloth, set on the north side where the sun would not find them until afternoon.

Nobody spilled anything. He watched for it after a while, deliberately, the way he would count a pulse. In two hours of a working district he did not see one person lose one mouthful, and it had never once occurred to him that this was a thing a city could be.

---

The marble began at an arch, and under the arch there was a guard post, and a queue.

Six people ahead of them. Xion had come through here perhaps four hundred times and had never once looked at the queue, which he understood — standing in it — was the whole of the arrangement.

The guard had a ledger on a stand. Each person came up and showed something: a chit, a seal, a folded paper. The guard read it, wrote in the ledger, and waved them through. Ten heartbeats each. It was not hostile. It was not anything.

The man in front of them had two crates on a handcart and a paper somebody else had given him.

"Tuesday," the guard said.

"It's for the Verrin house, they told me today—"

"Deliveries to the Verrin house are Tuesday." The guard wrote something. "Come back Tuesday."

The man stood a moment with both hands on the cart. Then he turned it, which took him some doing in the space, and went back down the hill past the queue with his face carefully arranged.

Then Xion.

He did not reach into his pocket, because there was nothing in his pocket to reach for.

"Kemvimore," he said. "And a guest."

The guard's hand never went near the ledger.

"Of course, my lord."

That was all of it. He had not raised his voice and he had shown nothing, and the man with the crates was still in sight down the hill, and Xion walked onto the marble with Elara half a step behind him.

---

She stopped him before they had gone ten paces.

"Kemvimore." She said it exactly as he had said it. "You're Xion Kemvimore."

"You know I am."

"That man is walking back down the hill." She had gone very still, and he had learned by now that the stillness was the thing to watch. "And you said a word."

He said nothing.

"In the square." Her voice did not rise, which was the worst of it. "You could have said that word in the square. Four of them. *I am Xion Kemvimore.* And they would have put the boy down on the stones and apologised to you for the trouble."

He opened his mouth.

Nothing came out of it. The same nothing, arriving in the same order, and he stood in the Noble District with marble under his boots and could not make it move.

"Why didn't you?"

"I couldn't," he said.

"You just did."

There was nothing to set against that, so he did not set anything against it.

Something went out of her face — not anger. Anger he could have worked with. She had put him somewhere, and it was lower than where he had been at breakfast, and she walked on into the district without waiting to see whether he followed.

---

He heard it before he saw it, and understood that she had heard it first, because she had already stopped.

Water.

Not the sound of a jar filling or a skin being poured out. A continuous sound. It did not begin and it did not end and it went on not ending, and it came from a plaza at the end of the street where a fountain three times the height of a man threw water into the air, and caught it, and threw it again.

Two boys and a girl were in the lower basin, in it to the waist. A third boy worked along the rim toward them. One of the boys had a cup — a real cup, glass — and he filled it and poured it over the girl's head, and she shrieked and went under and came up again.

On a bench in the shade a woman in grey fanned herself and did not look up.

Xion did not watch the fountain. He watched Elara, because he had been a healer for eight years and you watch the patient, not the wound.

She was not moving at all.

Then her eyes went amber — not the flicker he had seen in a storeroom, not the slip he had seen over a wall of paper, but a hard clean change, like a lamp being turned up — and stayed.

"Elara."

"I know."

"There are people—"

"I **know.**" She closed them. When she opened them they were blue, and it had cost her, and he could see precisely what.

---

They walked. She asked him nothing for six streets, which he had learned to recognise as thinking, and when she spoke it was not about the fountain.

"How many."

"Seventeen."

"In the district."

"In the district."

She nodded slowly. Then: "Show me the Warrens."

"No."

"Xion—"

"Not yet. And not because you couldn't stand it." He stopped, which made her stop. "Everything you've seen today you saw from outside. The wall, the gate, the plaza. You watched them. You haven't heard them talk."

"And how do I do that."

"There's a masquerade in three days. The Larannas estate — Silvanno's mother." He heard himself proposing it and went on anyway. "Everyone who matters, behind a mask, drunk on wine that costs more than that man's whole cart. They say things at those. Out loud. Because they believe every person in the room is one of them."

"And a mask hides my face."

"A mask hides your face." He looked at her, and she had it before he finished. "It doesn't hide your eyes."

---

They went back down through the arch in the late afternoon. The guard did not look up.

Elara said nothing at all until the marble ran out. Then: "What do I wear?"

It was the first thing she had asked him since the gate.
