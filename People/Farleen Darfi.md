# Farleen Darfi: The Spy Who Stayed

## The Knife That Missed

Here is what happened: Farleen Darfi was ordered to kill Xion Kemvimore. She had been placed in his friend group by the Water cartel to monitor his activities. She reported his charity work as harmless to protect him. She fell in love with him. And when his investigation threatened to expose the hidden princess, the order came — kill him or your family dies.

She tried everything else first. She argued against the search. She urged him to stop. She begged. When nothing worked, she drew her dagger and aimed for his heart.

She hit his shoulder.

A trained operative, close range, clear line. She hit his shoulder. Whether this was deliberate mercy or a flinch she'll never fully understand doesn't matter. What matters is the wound — the scar Xion carries for the rest of the story, the one that aches whenever she's near, the physical proof of the moment when love and duty collided and both lost.

Farleen Darfi's entire story grows from that knife thrust. Everything before it was cover. Everything after it is atonement, complicated by the stubborn fact that the love that made her miss was real, and the betrayal that made her swing was also real, and she has to carry both of those truths through every scene she occupies for the rest of the narrative.

## Paradigm Analysis

**Caretaker → Engineer → Truth Seeker → Spirited Competitor → Founder → Creator**

### Caretaker (Primary)

Every defining choice Farleen makes traces back to love. Not abstract compassion, not ideological commitment to the greater good — specific, personal, devastating love for one person that overrides every other consideration.

She can't kill Xion. The cartel orders it. Her family's safety depends on it. She draws the blade. And she can't do it — or, more precisely, she does it wrong, aiming for the place that will wound without killing. When she returns months later to warn him that cartel forces are closing in, she throws away everything: her position, her family's safety, her entire life. "Because I can't let you die. Because I already tried to kill you once and I'll regret that for the rest of my life."

In the Warrens, her Caretaker drive manifests as desperate protectiveness. She argues against every dangerous plan — not from cowardice but from an inability to watch Xion walk into harm. When Elara enters a trance and walks into Long Knife territory, Farleen begs Xion to let her go: "Why should you die following her?" When he follows anyway, she screams "FINE! Gods damn you, FINE!" and follows too, because she cannot lose him.

Her whispered confession in the Long Knives' darkness — "I can't — I can't lose you" — is the distillation of her entire motivational core.

In Book 2, when Xion is morally devastated by Melina's death and what he became to defeat Rosik, it's Farleen who sits with him through the worst nights. Not demanding he be okay. Not trying to fix him. Just existing beside him in the darkness. This is her Caretaker drive at its most mature: the understanding that sometimes care means presence without agenda.

**Shadow:** Her love is so consuming that it distorts her judgment. She follows Xion into situations she correctly identifies as suicidal. She cannot maintain the professional detachment her training demands because her heart overrides her operational instincts. And her protectiveness of Xion sometimes manifests as hostility toward Elara — the person she perceives as the one who keeps pulling him into danger.

### Engineer (Secondary)

Farleen is, by training and temperament, an extraordinarily capable operative. She reads environments the way other people read faces: identifying watchers in a marketplace, spotting optimal approach angles, planning three exit routes before entering a building. She scouts the scavenger operations alone because "I'm the only one of us trained to move silently." She leads the infiltration of Vex's stronghold with tactical precision. She knows the Warrens' hidden entrances from spy training — an abandoned granary with a partially collapsed floor.

Her Engineer drive is the backbone of the Warrens expedition. Without her tactical capability, the group would not have survived: she guides them through service passages to a hidden entrance, reads the marketplace's social dynamics, identifies the Long Knives watchers' positions, plans and executes the heist on Vex's ledger, and scouts the scavenger operations on a solo mission. She is the group's operational intelligence.

But this competence is instrumental, not primary. She builds plans and reads rooms because doing so protects the person she loves. When her tactical assessment says *leave* and her heart says *stay*, the heart wins every time. She identifies every reason to abandon the mission and follows Xion anyway. Her engineering is in permanent service to her Caretaker core.

In Book 2, the summary notes her "spy training finally used for something good" — coordinating resistance intelligence, passing messages through palace staff, participating in the shadow fund heist planning. The skills that once served a cartel now serve the people she chose.

**Shadow:** Her tactical competence enables her to rationalize. She can construct operational justifications for emotional decisions and present fear-driven urgency as strategic assessment. When she argues against dangerous plans, she's usually right about the risks — but her motivation isn't the risk assessment. It's the terror of losing Xion.

### Truth Seeker (Third)

Farleen is painfully honest about what she is. When Xion asks what was real during the bandaging scene — a moment of physical intimacy echoing their past relationship — she doesn't lie: "Some of it was real. Not all of it. But some." She could have said everything was real. She could have denied everything. Instead she offers the excruciating middle: partial truth, partial cover, the admission that even she can't fully separate the genuine from the operational.

Her truthfulness is selective but fierce. She tells Xion the full story of her assignment: "I was asked to keep an eye on your group. Report back on your activities." She admits to characterizing their charity work as harmless to protect them — an act of moral editing she can't quite take credit for. She confesses that she loved him and that it wasn't part of the assignment. She cuts herself off mid-sentence when the truth gets too close: "I *loved* him. I still —"

Her confrontation with Elara in Chapter 14 is raw truth delivered as fury: "I stabbed him — to maintain my cover. I betrayed everything I believed in to protect cartel interests. And when I finally decided to do the right thing, to actually help him, I threw away my entire life. My family. My position. Everything. So don't you dare call me a coward, princess."

**Shadow:** Her honesty is selective precisely because full honesty would be unbearable. She tells the truth about what she did but cannot fully articulate what she feels. The sentences she cuts off — "I still —" — are the truths her Truth Seeker drive can't complete.

### Spirited Competitor (Fourth)

The rivalry with Elara runs on competitive fire. When Ronas Dermir mistakes Farleen for the princess, her reaction is involuntary and revealing: "her posture shifted. Straightened. Her chin lifted slightly, and Xion saw the ghost of satisfaction cross her face." When Elara corrects the mistake and Ronas's skeptical "Really" devastates her, Farleen's expression is "carefully neutral, but Xion caught the flash of something in her eyes before she looked away. Not quite triumph. But close."

This isn't malice. It's the competitive satisfaction of being perceived as *more* — more regal, more commanding, more convincing — than the actual princess. Farleen competes with Elara not for the throne but for the question of who matters more to Xion, who is more capable, who belongs at his side.

Their professional cooperation during missions — working together on the heist, the scouting, the Warrens navigation — becomes its own form of competition: two extremely capable women forced to demonstrate competence to each other while neither will admit to caring about the other's assessment.

**Shadow:** Her competitive edge with Elara occasionally overrides her better judgment. The near-triumph at Ronas's mistake is a moment where rivalry displaces empathy — she enjoys Elara's humiliation, however briefly, because the competition matters to her more than she'd like to admit.

### Founder (Fifth)

Farleen's relationship with institutional loyalty is the wound at the center of her story. She began as a product of the Water cartel — trained, deployed, loyal to the structure that raised her. Her family (mother, sisters) was leveraged to ensure compliance. Her identity was institutional before it was personal.

When she chose Xion over the cartel, she destroyed her institutional foundation entirely. "I threw away my entire life. My family. My position. Everything." By Chapter 13, she has no Founder allegiance left — no institution to belong to, no structure to serve, no legacy to protect. She is institutionally homeless.

In Book 2, this absence becomes paradoxically liberating. She is "most free" precisely because "nobody watching her anymore — 'failed' spy." The institutional structures that once constrained her have written her off. Her freedom of movement — greater than Silvanno's, differently angled than Kael's — exists because she has nothing institutional left to protect or be protected by.

**Shadow:** Her institutional rootlessness leaves her without the safety net that identity provides. She exists between worlds — "dark practical clothing that marked her as neither noble nor common" — belonging nowhere, attached to one person rather than any structure. If that person were gone, she would have nothing.

### Creator (Sixth)

No evidence of creative or expressive drive in the text. Farleen is tactical, emotional, honest, competitive — but never artistic or visionary. This is her lowest paradigm.

**Shadow:** Minimal. She doesn't operate in this space.

## Backstory

Farleen Darfi is Water cartel — trained in intelligence work by Tania Larannas's organization and deployed to monitor Xion's reform group at The Amber Leaf. Her cover was that of a merchant's daughter. Her father is mentioned in connection with "unexpected guests" — likely spy check-ins with handlers. Her family — specifically her mother and sisters — was used as leverage to ensure her compliance with orders.

Her training was extensive: she knows hidden paths through Kaha'an, can plan and execute infiltration operations, moves with professional efficiency, and can assess tactical situations with the eye of someone who has done this before. She knows a hidden entrance to the Warrens — an abandoned granary with a collapsed floor — and has "used this before" as part of training that involved "knowing the city's hidden paths."

She was placed in Xion's group and spent six months monitoring their activities. She reported their charity work as "harmless" to prevent cartel interference — a small act of moral editing that protected the people she was assigned to watch. She attended their meetings. She became their friend. And somewhere in those six months, the cover became real. Not all of it. But some.

The previous injuries she concealed from Xion — the "falling down stairs" incident revealed during the bandaging scene in Chapter 16 — suggest a history of dangerous assignments before her placement with Xion's group. Her spy work left marks she hid with cover stories. Xion healed her without knowing what caused the wounds.

## Book 1: The Grain Merchant's Son

### The Cover (Chapters 2-3)

Farleen arrives late to The Amber Leaf — "Sorry I'm late. My father had unexpected guests this morning" — an excuse that reads differently once her true role is known. She is warm with Xion: amber eyes meeting his with "that familiar warmth." She participates in the debate about the princess with a question that carries double meaning: "What if you're right? What then?" Every interaction in these chapters operates on two levels — genuine friendship and operational monitoring.

When Xion announces the Arol Batae infiltration, Silvanno and Kael refuse. Farleen stays: "If you're determined to do this, someone should watch your back." The others read this as loyalty. It was also mission protocol — her target is moving, and she needs to move with him.

### The Knife (Chapter 4)

At the Arol Batae compound, after Xion finds Elara, Farleen reveals everything. She was assigned to watch his group. She reported his charity as harmless. She fell in love with him — "That wasn't part of the assignment." And now the cartel has ordered her to kill him because the princess changes everything.

"I don't want to do this. God, Xion, I don't want to do this."

Her blade catches his shoulder instead of his heart. Whether mercy or flinch, the wound becomes the story's most persistent physical metaphor — the scar Xion carries, the ache that returns when Farleen is near.

Elara, who followed Xion from inside the compound, knocks Farleen unconscious with brutal efficiency. Xion is left bleeding, holding the truth about a woman he trusted in one hand and the proof of a princess's existence in the other.

### The Return (Chapter 13)

Months later, Farleen arrives at Xion's clinic with dark hair disheveled, amber eyes red-rimmed, looking like she'd been crying or running or both. Cartel forces are closing in. She has come to warn him — burning her position permanently.

"Because I can't let you die. Because I already tried to kill you once and I'll regret that for the rest of my life."

She reveals the scope of her assignment: she characterized their charity work as harmless, she loved him genuinely, and now she has thrown away everything — family, position, future — to save him. She leads them through service passages and a hidden granary entrance into the Warrens.

The tension with Elara is immediate: "I can't believe you'd even let her within ten feet of you." Farleen's response holds the full weight of her sacrifice: "I'm *sorry*! What more do you want from me? I'm trying to make it right!"

### The Tactical Backbone (Chapters 14-22)

From the moment they enter the Warrens, Farleen becomes the group's operational intelligence. Her arc across these chapters follows a consistent pattern: she identifies the danger, argues against the plan, is overruled, and follows anyway.

In Chapter 14, she confronts both Xion and Elara about their recklessness — furious that she wasn't told about Tam before they descended, furious at Elara's fight that exposed them. Her tirade is both tactical assessment and emotional confession: "I came down here for you. I risked everything to warn you. To get you out."

In Chapter 15, she reads the Warrens marketplace with professional precision, identifying Long Knives watchers and selecting the optimal contact for intelligence gathering.

In Chapter 16, she plans and leads the heist on Vex's stronghold — studying building plans, acquiring dark clothing, mapping three exit routes. A guard slams her into a wall, bruising her ribs badly. The bandaging scene afterward becomes an echo of their past intimacy: Xion wrapping her ribs, the memory of previous injuries she hid with cover stories, the admission that "Some of it was real. Not all of it. But some."

In Chapter 17, she scouts the scavenger operations alone — the most dangerous solo mission — insisting she's the only one trained to move silently. She returns advocating retreat. She's overruled.

In Chapter 19, Elara's trance draws them into Long Knife territory. Farleen's desperation escalates line by line: "Do you even know where we are?" becomes "She doesn't even know what she's doing!" becomes "Is she worth your life? Tell me she's worth it, Xion" becomes the whispered core: "I can't — I can't lose you."

In Chapter 20, after the brass door trial nearly kills Tam, Farleen unleashes fury at Elara: "Xion could've died because of you!" Her anger is specifically protective — she isn't upset about the mission or the danger to herself. She is upset about Xion.

In Chapter 22, after the group returns to the surface, Farleen demonstrates her most mature Caretaker moment: she posts herself near the entrance, watches the street, and "very deliberately gave Xion and Elara space." She knows the emotional confrontation between them is necessary. She lets it happen. She keeps watch while they break and heal.

### The Mistaken Princess (Chapter 18)

In the Iron Hall, Ronas Dermir looks at the group and assumes Farleen is the princess. Her reaction reveals something she usually keeps buried: "her posture shifted. Straightened. Her chin lifted slightly, and Xion saw the ghost of satisfaction cross her face." For one heartbeat, she is seen as regal — and she basks in it.

When Elara corrects the mistake and Ronas's skeptical "Really" crushes her, Farleen's expression holds "not quite triumph. But close." It's the sharpest moment of rivalry between the two women — and it reveals Farleen's self-image. She carries herself with enough natural authority and composure that a Slaver King who has met the actual cartel leaders mistakes her for royalty.

Ronas correctly identifies her as Water cartel: "Gonna guess Water cartel — you one of Tania's?" She is, whether she wants to be or not.

### The Acknowledgment (Chapters 23-25)

When Silvanno and Kael arrive, Xion covers for Farleen — telling a technically true but misleading version of how she warned them. "Farleen's expression shifted — surprise, then gratitude, then something more complicated that Xion couldn't quite read." He protects her secret. She holds his gaze a heartbeat longer than necessary.

At the coronation march, she appears "from near the gates, dressed in dark practical clothing that marked her as neither noble nor common." She reports on security with habitual wariness — even at the moment of triumph, she's scanning for threats.

When Elara takes the throne and looks at Farleen, "something complicated passed between them. Acknowledgment without resolution."

The narrative's final word on Farleen in Book 1: "who'd betrayed everything to save him."

## Book 2: The One You Feed

Farleen becomes paradoxically the most free member of Xion's core group. "Nobody watching her anymore — 'failed' spy." The cartel wrote her off. The institutional structures that once deployed and constrained her have moved on. She exists in the gaps of the power structure, invisible in a way that grants unprecedented freedom of movement.

She uses this freedom for small, persistent acts of care: waiting outside with Xion's favorite pastry — the same gesture each time they meet — when his palace access is restricted. Trying to pass messages through palace staff when he can't reach Elara. Coordinating resistance intelligence with her "spy training finally used for something good."

In Chapter 4, the pastry gesture breaks through. Xion has just been formally expelled from the palace — the last door to Elara closed, politely, definitively. Farleen is waiting with his favorite pastry, same as always. But this time he takes it and hugs her, and she tells him it's going to be okay, and from that accumulated warmth the kiss happens — the first real, on-screen, lip-to-lip kiss in the series. It belongs to Farleen. A messenger shatters the moment almost immediately (Rosik's summons), and Farleen's response — "Go. I'll still be here." — becomes her thesis statement for the entire series. She is not the consolation prize. She is the person who is always there.

She participates in planning the shadow fund heists, bringing operational expertise to the group's escalating campaign against Rosik's coalition.

But her most significant role comes after everything falls apart. After Melina's death, Xion goes cold — frighteningly, immediately cold. Not grief, not shock — operational. He becomes his father: the same architecture of control, the same refusal to break, the same terrifying competence deployed as a substitute for feeling. His friends watch it happen and don't recognize him. Farleen, who has always been able to read Xion's emotions on his face, finds nothing to read. She stays close anyway — through the worst nights, not demanding he be okay, just existing beside him in the darkness, waiting for the version of him she knows to come back. She doesn't know if he will.

The book's final image belongs to Farleen. Xion has been avoiding the low wall — the meeting spot where he and Melina met across the entire book — since her death. Taking different routes. The geography of denial. Then, near the end, his feet take him there before his mind can stop them. He sits down. The cold breaks. He falls apart.

And Farleen finds him. She sits down beside him. She puts her arms around him and gives him a shoulder to cry on. She doesn't say a word. She doesn't need to. "I'll still be here" — from Chapter 4, from the garden, from after the kiss — paid off in silence. Curtains.

This is her entire arc distilled. The spy who fell in love. The knife that missed. The woman who threw everything away and followed him into the dark. And when the dark is all that's left — when everything else has been burned away, when the cold finally shatters — she's the one who is there to hold him.

## The AU Where Farleen Wins (Authorial Note)

There is an alternate universe where Xion chooses Farleen. It is not a bad life. It is not tragic. It is vividly, painfully plausible.

In this AU: Elara marries Kalden. Xion, disinherited from Kemvimore and disconnected from Valanar, is essentially a nobody. He goes back to Master Fen's clinic — permanently, this time. He leads a small life, healing the poor. Farleen is there as his loving and devoted wife. They are never wealthy, but they have a small family: one daughter, maybe a cat. They fall into the footnotes of history as empresses and continental issues happen above their heads, which they never see or realistically care about. Master Fen and his wife, a fixture of the lower wards. He doesn't think about what he "lost" with Elara — he'd feel that was disrespectful to Farleen. He chose her, so it would be wrong to regret it. Besides, she made him happy.

The AU is not miserable. It may even be, in some political respects, *better* — Kalden is not the world's best emperor consort by a mile, but he has shown genuine competence when his ego isn't threatened. The Continental War probably never happens. Thousands of lives are spared. But: Harkim never awakens to what the Provosts are doing. The Globehall continues unabated. Tiberian and Melina literally don't exist. The Warrens are never fixed. Xion, with his scope constrained to his clinic, is unaware of any of this. It's all above his head.

**Why this matters for writing Farleen:** The AU's plausibility is the reason Farleen scenes must land with genuine weight, not consolation-prize energy. She is not offering Xion a lesser life. She is offering him a *different* one — one where he is genuinely happy, one where the world pays for his happiness without him ever knowing. Elara is the answer to "what does the world need from Xion Kemvimore?" Farleen is the answer to "what does Xion Kemvimore need for himself?" The cruel engine of the story is that those two questions cannot have the same answer. Every Farleen scene — the pastry, the kiss, the shoulder at the low wall — must carry the weight of the life she's offering. The reader should feel what's being given up. Not a consolation prize. A real, full, good life that simply isn't the one the story requires.

## Relationships

### Xion Kemvimore

The most complicated relationship in the story. Farleen was his friend, his betrayer, his reluctant ally, his indispensable companion, and the unresolved emotional thread that runs beneath everything else. She loved him genuinely while lying about everything. She stabbed him while trying not to kill him. She returned to save him while knowing she'd destroyed her own life.

Their physical history — implied intimacy before the betrayal, the bandaging scenes that echo it, the scar that aches when she's near — creates a baseline of bodily knowledge that the narrative never resolves. He protects her secret with Silvanno and Kael. She brings him pastries. Neither of them can say what this is.

But in Book 2, Chapter 4, they stop not-saying it — at least for one moment. The first on-screen kiss in the series is theirs: not Elara's, not a peck on the cheek, not a grief reflex. A real kiss, born from accumulated small kindnesses (the pastry, the waiting, the being there), interrupted almost immediately by the world pulling them apart. Book 1's cheek-kiss was cover-story Farleen. This is real Farleen. And Xion reached for her, not the other way around.

The love triangle with Elara is not about competition for Xion's romantic choice. It's about two different kinds of presence: Elara as the person he chose and who chose him, the partner in purpose and vision; Farleen as the person who is *there* — physically, persistently, in the darkness when no one else can reach him. The Chapter 4 kiss gives the reader *evidence* that Farleen isn't the obvious loser — she's the answer to a different question. When Xion eventually chooses Elara, the reader remembers the pastry and the kiss and "I'll still be here," and feels what's being given up.

Farleen also has a practical argument for why she's the better partner — one that has nothing to do with love and everything to do with survival: "If he ever needs to navigate the waters of places like the Coterie or the nobility, he needs someone who knows what people who smile with poisoned lips are like. You can't help him — you're even worse at this than he is. Not everything can be fixed with a sword, princess. I would know — because I used to be such a person." This isn't a boast. It's an admission reframed as an offer. Farleen knows the performative world of cartel politics from the inside because she was a weapon deployed within it. Elara's instinct when faced with political deception is moral fury — sometimes right, sometimes catastrophic (the masquerade proved this). Farleen's instinct is to read the room, map the threats, and navigate without breaking cover. She's not claiming moral superiority over Elara. She's claiming *utility* — and she's right.

### Elara Valanar

Open hostility, gradually tempered by grudging professional respect. Elara sees the woman who put a blade in Xion's shoulder. Farleen sees the woman who keeps pulling him into danger. Their early confrontations are brutal: "At least *I've* never stabbed you" versus "I threw away my entire life... So don't you dare call me a coward, princess."

But the Warrens force cooperation. They work together on the heist. They scout together. They protect the same person from different angles. The hostility doesn't disappear — it calcifies into something harder and more honest. By the coronation, what passes between them is "acknowledgment without resolution." They know what each other is. They can't pretend otherwise.

### Tania Larannas / The Water Cartel

Farleen's original institutional home. Tania deployed her. The cartel trained her. Her family was leveraged to ensure compliance. When she chose Xion over the assignment, she severed this connection permanently — not with dramatic confrontation but with the quiet finality of someone who walks out a door and never comes back.

Ronas correctly identifies her as Water cartel. The training is permanent even if the loyalty isn't. She will always move like someone Tania trained, see threats the way Tania's operatives see them, know the city's hidden paths because the cartel mapped them. The institution shaped her even after she broke from it.

### Silvanno Larannas and Kael Issamil

Part of the original friend group. Farleen's relationships with both are surface-level in the text — they share communication protocols (Silvanno's signal), coordinate in Book 2, and coexist within the group. But Kael's "thoughtful, assessing" look at her in Chapter 23 suggests that not everyone has accepted Xion's cover story about Farleen's past. She may be living on borrowed trust.

## Personal Characteristics

### Physical Presence

Amber eyes — mentioned repeatedly across the story, the physical detail the text returns to most often. Dark hair, disheveled when she arrives to warn Xion. She carries herself with enough composure and poise that Ronas Dermir mistakes her for the imperial princess.

Her body language shifts between two modes: the professional (moving with "practiced caution" and "easy confidence," sleeping with her back against the wall watching both door and window) and the emotional (hands shaking when treating Tam after the brass door trial, amber eyes red-rimmed when she arrives at the clinic). The gap between these modes is the gap between what she was trained to be and what she actually is.

Her clothing at the coronation — "dark practical clothing that marked her as neither noble nor common" — is the most precise physical metaphor for her position. She exists between worlds. She belongs to none of them.

The scar she left on Xion's shoulder is her most enduring physical presence in the story — a wound she inflicted that he carries permanently, the proof of the moment that defined them both.

### Behavioral Patterns

- **Argue, then follow**: Her most consistent pattern. She identifies every reason the plan is wrong, makes her case clearly and forcefully, is overruled, and follows anyway. She cannot abandon the people she loves even when they make decisions she considers suicidal.
- **Tactical assessment as emotional displacement**: When she's frightened, she gets operational. She reads rooms, identifies exits, plans contingencies. The spy training provides a framework for channeling fear into useful action.
- **Selective vulnerability**: She controls her emotional exposure with precision, opening the door to honesty in careful increments: "Some of it was real. Not all of it. But some." The sentences she cuts off — "I still —" — are the moments where control fails.
- **Protective fury**: Her anger is almost always redirected fear for Xion's safety. Her confrontation with Elara after the brass door trial is fury, but the core is terror: "Xion could've died because of you!"
- **Quiet presence**: Her most mature expression — keeping watch while others have necessary conversations, bringing pastries, sitting in the darkness without demanding anything. This is where her Caretaker drive and her training merge: the ability to simply be present, alert, and available.

### The Central Tension

Farleen Darfi is a woman who destroyed her entire life for love and then had to keep living in the wreckage. She can't go back — the cartel wrote her off, her family is beyond her reach, her institutional identity is ashes. She can't go forward — the man she loves is in love with someone else, and the love triangle has no clean resolution. She exists in the space between what she was and what she might become, defined by a single moment with a knife that she can never undo and never stop atoning for.

Her greatest fear isn't death or danger or even losing Xion — it's that the knife was the truest thing she ever did. That the woman who swung the blade is who she really is, and everything since has been a more elaborate cover story than the one the Water cartel gave her.

The evidence says otherwise. The evidence says the truest thing she did was come back. Was lead them into the Warrens. Was follow into the dark. Was sit beside him through the worst nights without demanding he be okay.

But evidence and fear don't negotiate.

So she keeps showing up. Dark practical clothing that marks her as neither noble nor common. Amber eyes scanning everything with habitual wariness. The spy who fell in love with her assignment and burned the world down to prove it was real.

The knife missed.

Everything else hit.
