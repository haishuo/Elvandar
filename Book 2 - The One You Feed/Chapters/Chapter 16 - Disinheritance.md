# Chapter 16: Disinheritance

He found himself at the clinic as if he had simply appeared there.

There was the low wall, and the light going, and the messenger's shape retreating across the square — and then there was the clinic, and he was inside it, at the little table, and between the two there was nothing at all. His feet had carried him home without consulting him. He kept finding the gap the way your tongue keeps finding a missing tooth, absently, again and again, and it gave nothing back.

The pieces came over the next hours, out of order, the way such things come — the messenger's words replaying once the roar had drained out of his ears, a note from Kael that he read three times without feeling any of it, the particular speed at which word of a dead Kemvimore was already moving through the city. He assembled them because assembling things was the only work his mind still knew how to do.

She had gone into the Surface Warrens. Alone, or near enough. To do the thing she had done her whole life — to get out in front of a problem, to find the person on the other side of it and make them see the sense of stopping. And someone up in those ruins, with the leash off and no one left to answer to, had killed her for it. Not in a fight. Not because she was a threat to him. Because she was there, and he could.

Xion sat with that, and followed the line of it back the way he'd follow a fever to its source, and the line did not stop where he wanted it to stop. It ran from a nameless man in the Surface Warrens to Ronas's people loose with the leash off, and from the loose leash to a father coming apart in a mansion, and from the father to six warehouses emptied in a single night by a son who had counted the cost and believed he understood it. He had said the words himself, in the dark, to Elara. *People will die.* He had thought he knew what he was buying. He had never once put his sister's face on it.

The summons came before he had moved from the table. Not a letter — a summons: the Kemvimore seal, a man in the doorway waiting on an answer he'd plainly been told not to return without. Whatever machinery kept the Lord of Grain informed had been turning since the low wall, perhaps since before it. Rosik knew. Of course Rosik knew.

Xion read it once. Then his hand picked up the medical bag without asking him, and he went.

---

He was at the estate's gate before he had the sense of having left the clinic.

The estate met him the way it always met him — flawless, swept, arranged for looking and not for living. He was through the gate and down the hall before any of it reached him as more than a sequence of surfaces. And then he was in the room, and the room was not empty, and that was the first thing that arrived whole.

There were others in it. Estate staff along one wall. Two men he half-knew for Coterie clerks, the kind who carried other men's decisions into ledgers. This was not a father and a son in a private room. This was a proceeding — witnessed, meant to be recorded, meant never to be unsaid — and Rosik had chosen it that way, and the choosing was itself a sentence, passed before a word was spoken.

His father stood at the far end of it. His daughter had been dead less than a day, and he wore the face he had worn every day of Xion's life: composed, unhurried, giving nothing back. If the grief was in him it was somewhere none of this could reach, locked away wherever he kept the things that might have made him a man like other men. There was nothing on him to read. Xion, who had spent his whole life learning to read him, read nothing.

"You are no longer my son," Rosik said. "You have no claim to House Kemvimore. No inheritance. No family."

He let it sit the exact length it needed to sit. Then, in the same even voice, without heat, the way a man reads a figure off a page:

"You stole from me. You turned everything I gave you against me. And you killed your sister as surely as if you had wielded the blade yourself."

*Your sister.* Not *my daughter.* Not *she is gone.* The part of Xion's mind that had never once stopped working — the part that read symptoms, that read systems, that read the shape of a sentence and what the shape was built to do — heard the choice in it and understood it exactly. Rosik was not grieving in this room. Rosik was negotiating. *Your sister* was aimed, with precision, at the one place in Xion where it would do the most harm; and it found the place; and knowing that it had been aimed did nothing to blunt it.

The answer rose in him whole. *She was your daughter too. You are the one who took the leash off Ronas. You pointed a wildfire at the city and told yourself you could call it back, and the chain that ends at Melina does not begin with me. It begins with you.* It was true, as far as he had the truth of it. He had the words. He could set them in order and put them in the room.

He didn't.

Not because he couldn't. Because he saw the move. *Your sister* was bait — a door held open into the one place his father could not be beaten, the place where every accusation had its answer ready, and the answer after that, and the answer after that, where a room of witnesses became a Coterie chamber and Rosik Kemvimore walked out of every chamber the winner. The instant Xion said *she was your daughter too,* the disinheritance stopped being a thing done to him and became a thing he was arguing about — and there was no argument in this world he could win against that man. He was his father's son. He knew precisely how it worked. And he would not play.

So he said nothing.

It was not the silence of the boy he had been at fifteen — the one who had lain senseless while his father unmade the world in two words, and woke to find the only mother he had ever known already gone, and, understanding it, had no words of his own at all: no plea, no argument, only a boy standing dumb in a room. That boy had been frozen; the silence had happened to him. This one he chose, with his eyes open, because it was the single thing in the room his father could not turn to his own use. He stood in the wreckage of his family and refused to hand the man the fight, and it was the nearest thing to a victory on offer anywhere in that house, and it tasted of nothing at all.

And then — for less than a breath, so brief that a part of him was already insisting he'd imagined it — something moved beneath his father's composure that Xion had no name for.

Not the lord. Not the administrator, not the cold negotiator he had faced all his life. Something further down than any of that, and stiller: a flat, patient attention, the attention of a thing that has decided nothing yet and could decide anything — and the sudden, bodily certainty, arriving before his mind could refuse it, that the man across the room could cross the floor and end him with his bare hands and feel no more about it after than he felt now.

Then it was gone, folded back under the surface so completely that the surface might never have broken, and Rosik was only Rosik again, composed and verbal and exact, a father who had just unmade his son in front of witnesses. Xion had no name for what he'd seen. He only knew that he had seen it, and that some animal part of him had gone cold and quiet in the seeing — the way you go still when you understand that the thing across the room is not what you had taken it for.

He picked up his bag. He walked out through the gallery and the swept gardens and the gate, past all of it — the estate, the name, the last thread that had, an hour ago, still tied him to a family — and none of it came with him. He did not look back at the door. There was nothing behind the door to look at.

---

Farleen was at the clinic when he returned.

He had sent no word. No one in his network knew yet; he had told no one, done nothing, moved through the day like a body on a current. But Farleen had always been a thing more easily met than explained, and she was simply there in the dim of the shut clinic, the way she had been simply there at every gate and every low place of all these months. There was no pastry in her hands this time. She looked at his face, and whatever she found there, she did not ask what had happened. She only stepped aside to let him in.

He was sitting down. The bag was somewhere — the table, the floor. He told her what had happened at the estate, or he didn't and she already knew; the order of it left no mark. What stayed was that she stayed.

The cold came in over the hours, and it did not come like grief.

He had braced, somewhere, for grief — for the thing to break in him the way it had broken in the square, the roar and the shaking and the body's honest refusal. It didn't come. What came instead was quieter and far worse: a clarity, a narrowing, the personal boiling off like water and leaving the hard operational residue behind. The machinery that had been running in him since a night of six warehouses did not stop for his sister. It ran cleaner. It ran with everything soft finally out of its way.

He was dimly aware of Farleen watching him. She had always been able to read him off his face — it was one of the oldest things between them — and he could feel her at it now, and coming up against a surface she could not get behind. He knew what was happening on his side of that surface: something drawing quietly shut, a door closed from within, himself on the far side of it and untroubled by the dark. He did not reach to stop it. He watched her fail to reach it either. She brought no pastry. She said nothing. She stayed close, the way she stayed close, and he understood, in some far-off way, that what she was staying close to had changed — and he could not make himself mind.

By the time the light shifted at the window, he was working.

It did not feel like a decision. Decisions had a texture, a moment of weighing, and there was no weighing in this — only that the operational picture was there, whole, assembled out of the heist and the network and months of gathered intelligence, and the part of him that read such pictures was the only part still fully awake. Rosik was cut off at the knees and reeling. Ronas's men were loose. The moment was exact and it would not hold. These were facts, and facts were a country he could still stand in when everything else had washed out from under him.

She let him work through the night. Then, when the grey at the window had warmed toward morning and he had not stopped or looked up in longer than was right, she came and put herself between him and the work.

"Xion. Look at me."

He didn't.

"You're going too fast. You haven't slept, you haven't stopped — not once. Just breathe. Stop, for a minute. That's all I'm asking."

His voice, when it came, was level, and came from a long way off. "Rosik isn't stopping." He turned a page. "So neither can I."

The room went very still.

"...That's exactly what scares me," she said, so quietly it barely crossed the space between them.

He didn't answer. There was no answer in the operational picture, and the operational picture was where he lived now. After a moment he went back to the work. She didn't leave. She drew a chair to the edge of the lamplight and sat down in it, and stayed, because it was the only thing left that she could do.
