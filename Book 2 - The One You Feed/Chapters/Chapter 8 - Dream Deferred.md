# Chapter 8: Dream Deferred

The collar sat exactly right.

Prince Kalden Erulius turned his chin a degree to the left and studied the line of it in the tall mirror, and was satisfied. It was a fine collar — high, stiff, worked in gold thread along a pattern no tailor in Eruliath had invented, because the pattern was not Erulian. It was imperial. He had copied it, thread for thread, from a portrait in a book: the collar worn by Aurelan the Third, who had sat the Ruby Throne four hundred years ago in the great days of the empire, before the provinces forgot what they were and wandered off one by one to play at being kingdoms. Kalden wore Aurelan's collar every morning. It reminded the mirror who it was looking at.

Behind him, the reflection held the rest of it, and the rest of it was good.

The chamber had been built to imperial proportions — everything a hand's-breadth larger than the rooms it echoed, ceilings a touch higher, doorways a touch grander, so that a visitor from Kaha'an who had seen the old imperial apartments would feel, without being able to say why, that these were the same rooms improved. On the desk lay a coin, freshly struck, bearing his own profile in the attitude of an emperor and, on the reverse, the imperial seal of a house that was not quite his. Beside it, a sheaf of documents under the heading of the Imperial Chancellery — an institution he had founded, staffed, and given the weight of two decades, so that when the day came, the machinery of empire would already be turning and would need only a throne to turn around. Through the tall window, the banner of the Aspirant Guard moved in the morning wind above a courtyard where men drilled who had been told, from the day they took the oath, that they were not merely soldiers of a principality but the honor guard of a destiny.

And through the open doorway to the long gallery, dim in the early light, the Hall of Ancestors — where the portraits hung. Erulian lords beside imperial figures, generation reaching to generation, the faces linked down the wall toward the Ruby Throne. Most of the portraits were new. He had commissioned them himself, over the years, as the story required them; a good hall of ancestors, like a good claim, was a thing you built. And on the near wall, framed in glass, the chart: the line of descent his own hand had traced, one winter, down through the old genealogies, back and back through a common blood to House Valanar. It was a real line. He was fond of reminding himself that it was a real line. A third cousin, twice removed, on the mother's side, some centuries gone — but *real*, documented, and, as best any living man could reckon, the nearest that remained. The chart did not dwell on the thinness of the thread. It dwelt on the fact of the connection, which was the only fact that mattered, and had been for twenty years.

He looked at himself a moment longer, alone, with no aide near and no advisor at the door, and he said it, quietly, the way a man says a thing he has earned the right to say and has not yet been given leave to say aloud.

"Emperor Kalden Erulius."

The mirror did not laugh at him. Why would it? It sounded exactly right. It sounded like the last line of a story that had been twenty years in the telling and was very nearly finished.

He smiled.

---

The aide who knocked wore the look of a man carrying something.

"Your Highness. A rider from Balisha. He says — forgive me, he insisted I say it exactly — he says he brings the finest news the continent has heard in a generation, and he begs the honor of delivering it to you himself."

Kalden felt the small clean click of a thing arriving on schedule. He had been expecting Balisha to move; Balisha was always moving, and the Shadow Corps had watched every twitch of it for twenty years and reported, again and again, the same underlying truth beneath the cartel squabbles — the throne stood empty, the wards stood dark, and no pretender's blood had ever woken them. It was only ever a question of when the last obstacle wore away and the continent turned, at last, to the one man who had spent his life preparing to be turned to.

"Bring him," Kalden said, and settled the collar a final quarter-inch, and turned from the mirror to receive his news like an emperor.

The rider, when he came, was *radiant*.

That was the word for it — the man was lit from within, flushed from hard riding and something better than exhaustion, his eyes bright, his whole body leaning toward the moment as though he could not wait another breath to be rid of the joy he carried. He went to one knee, and he was smiling so wide it looked to cost him effort not to laugh, and Kalden thought, with the small warmth of a ruler beholding a loyal man's happiness, *he knows. Somehow he already knows what this means, and he has ridden half to death to be the one who tells me.*

"Your Highness," the rider said, and his voice shook with the size of it. "A Valanar has been found."

The warmth in Kalden's chest did not move. Not yet. The words were arriving in order, and the first of them had not yet arranged themselves into meaning.

"She lives, Highness. A daughter of the true line, hidden all these years — and she has come to Kaha'an, and she has sat the Ruby Throne." The man's words tumbled now, too full to keep in step. "And the wards, Highness — the ancient wards, the ones that have been dark since the Rending, that turned back every pretender who ever laid a hand to them — they *woke*. They blazed for her the moment her blood touched the seal. The whole city saw it. There is no question in it, none, not from anyone — her legitimacy is beyond dispute. The Valanar line is *restored*." He looked up, shining, expectant, generous, offering the news like a gift with both hands. "The empire has an Empress again."

And there it was, laid at Kalden's feet by a man on his knees who believed with his whole joyful heart that he was making a prince happy.

*Valanar. Found. Ruby Throne. Wards. Unquestionable.*

The words went into him one at a time, and one at a time they took something out. He felt the smile from the mirror still on his face — he could feel it, the exact arrangement of it, "Emperor Kalden Erulius" still hanging in the muscles around his mouth — and he felt it come apart, not fall but *disassemble*, each word from the rider's mouth lifting away another piece of it, because each word closed off another door his racing mind had already flung open and started running toward. *A pretender, then — no. The wards. A trick, a staged light — no. The whole city saw. A claim to be contested, a girl to be out-maneuvered, a bloodline to be measured against his own documented line —* no. No. The wards did not blaze for the second-nearest blood. The wards did not blaze for a documented thread, a fond winter's reconstruction, a chart behind glass. They blazed for the true thing, and they had never once blazed for him, and now the whole continent knew there was a true thing, and it was not him, and it never had been.

Twenty years. A palace built a hand's-breadth larger. A currency, a Chancellery, a guard, a hall of purchased ancestors. Twenty years of turning a thin real thread into an inevitable future, and it had taken this beaming boy on his knees the length of a single breath to make all of it into what it had always secretly been, which was the elaborate hobby of a provincial prince who dressed like an emperor in front of a mirror.

The rider was still smiling. That was the unbearable part. He had delivered the worst words Kalden Erulius had ever heard in his life, delivered them like flowers, and he was kneeling there in the wreckage of twenty years lit up with the pleasure of a man who has done a fine thing well, and he did not know. He would never know. He had ridden half to death to drive a blade into a prince and he thought he had brought a blessing.

The silence went on a beat too long. The aide by the door shifted his weight.

"That is," Kalden said, "great news indeed."

His voice came out level. He was distantly, coldly impressed by it — that it could be so level, with the floor gone out from under everything. He heard himself find the shape of the thing a prince would say. "The line restored. After so long. The continent will rejoice." He made himself cross to the desk, made his hand move as if of its own accord, and took up a purse and pressed it into the rider's hands, which was the correct thing, the emperor's thing, the generous acknowledgment of good service. "You have ridden hard to bring me this. Rest. Eat at my table tonight. You have done — " the word came from very far away " — well."

The rider's face, if it were possible, shone brighter. He bowed himself out backward, clutching the purse and the honor both, and Kalden watched him go and understood that the man would tell this story for the rest of his life: the day he brought the great news to the Amber Palace, and the good prince rewarded him, and everyone was glad.

The door closed.

"Leave me," Kalden said to the aide, in the same level voice, and the aide, who had served him long enough to be afraid of exactly that levelness, left quickly.

---

He did not throw the mirror down. He did not sweep the coin and the documents from the desk, or tear the chart from the wall, or shout for wine, or weep. He had watched lesser men do such things and had privately despised them for it. Rage was a confession. Rage told the room that something had reached you.

He simply stood, very still, in the middle of twenty years.

And underneath the stillness, in the place where the patient man had lived — the man who could wait a decade for a coalition to ripen, who had built institution upon institution with the slow certainty of someone laying a road toward a horizon only he could see — something else was rising, something that did not wait and did not build and did not care in the slightest that killing her would not un-blaze the wards or un-ring the bells or return one hour of the twenty years. That part of him knew, and did not care. The girl existed. As long as the girl existed, the mirror was a lie and the collar was a costume and Kalden Erulius was a footnote in someone else's restoration.

The girl could be made not to exist.

It was not a plan. A plan would have consulted the intelligence he did not yet have — who she truly was, who stood around her, how the city held her, whether her death now would open the door back to his claim or slam it forever. The patient man would have gathered all of that first. But the patient man was, for the moment, simply gone, burned off in a single breath by a smiling boy, and what remained reached past every legitimate instrument of the state Kalden had built over twenty years — past the Chancellery, past the Guard, past the chancellor and the general and the whole gleaming apparatus of a destiny — and reached for the one instrument that had never been meant to see the light.

"Send for the master of the Shadow Corps," he said, to the empty room, and somewhere a servant heard, and went.

---

The spymaster came without ceremony, as he always did, and stood, and waited, and did not ask why he had been summoned to the prince's private chambers at the wrong hour with the aides sent away. He had served Kalden a long time. He read the collar, and the desk, and the particular stillness of the man in front of the mirror, and whatever he assembled from it, he kept behind a face that gave nothing back.

Kalden did not turn from the glass. It seemed important, suddenly, not to turn — to say it to the reflection, to the version of himself that still wore Aurelan's collar and had, minutes ago, been an emperor.

"There is a girl in Kaha'an who claims to be a Valanar." His voice was calm. It was, if anything, calmer than before. "The claim is being believed. Correct this mistake." A small pause, the length of a held breath. "Hire whoever you must. I want her dead."

The spymaster did not counsel patience. He did not observe that the wards had already blazed, that the bells had already rung, that a corpse could not unmake a coronation the continent had already witnessed. He did not suggest that a prince might first learn who the girl was before ordering her killed. He had served Kalden through twenty years of magnificent patience, and he had also, once or twice, seen the other thing — the thing that came up fast and cold when the prince was wounded somewhere that mattered — and he knew that this was not an hour for counsel. It was an hour for the instruction to be given and received and carried out of the room before the prince could hear it aloud in the daylight and have to know what he was.

"It will be done," the spymaster said, and bowed, and was gone.

---

Kalden Erulius stood alone with the mirror.

Everything he had built was still there. The coin still bore his profile. The Chancellery's documents still lay under their imperial heading. The Guard still drilled in the courtyard below, and the ancestors still hung along the gallery, and the chart still traced its true thin thread back through the centuries to a throne, and the collar — Aurelan's collar, high and gold and exactly right — still sat at his throat precisely where he had set it that morning, when the world had still been arranged the way twenty years had promised it would be.

None of it had changed. Every piece of it was as beautiful and as solid as it had been at dawn.

And all of it, as of one sentence from a joyful man on his knees, meant nothing at all.

He looked at himself in the glass for a long time.

He did not remove the collar.
