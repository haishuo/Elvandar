Morning found him already at the little table, and the little table already buried.

Farleen came and went through the low room with the ease of someone who had stopped knocking weeks ago, setting reports down at his elbow and carrying off the ones he'd bled dry. Between them they built the day the way they'd built the last several — a piece at a time, out of scraps. What she'd seen with her own eyes, walking the Warrens' edge in the dark while sensible people stayed in. Which alliances in the Coterie were holding, and which had begun, very quietly, to fray. Which of Ronas's names had gone quiet and which had gone loud. He turned each one over, looking for the seam, the place where it all resolved into the cold sum his father was surely driving it toward — and each one refused him, the way they had for days, and he set it down and reached for the next.

"You've been at this since before light," Farleen said once, not quite a question.

"He doesn't sleep either," Xion said, and did not look up, and she let it go.

And then — late in the morning, in something Silvanno's Water contacts had passed quietly up the line — a thread. Not the whole shape of his father; nothing so clean as that. But a hard, specific, dangerous thing: Rosik was preparing to move on Tania Larannas's shadow funds. Not court her, not lean on her — **raid** her, the off-the-books reserves no cartel named and every cartel kept, and do it the way Rosik did everything, with his own hands clean and someone else left holding the theft.

Xion read it twice and felt the drop of it. His father did not do this. Rosik used the people beside him, bled them, outlasted them — but he did not devour his own coalition, because a man who eats his allies has none left, and Rosik had never once spent tomorrow to buy his way through today. Except now he was about to; and that was the same fact Melina kept battering herself against from the inside — *I can't get a true word out of him* — only seen from the other side. Not a lord maneuvering. A man crossing a line he'd spent thirty years never crossing.

And once he saw it as a line, the rest of it opened out ahead of him, quick and clean. Warn Tania before the knife came — set the proof in front of her, let her draw her own conclusion — and Water would pull its weight off Rosik on its own. Sa Ko Ren, who stayed only because Rosik delivered, would feel the coalition start to list and step back sooner than go down with it. Take those two and the smaller houses scattered inside a week, and then there was no structure left for Ronas to be pointed at, and a father with nothing left to operate would have, at last, no choice but to stop. No siege. No blood in the streets. No two of them across a table with the knives out. Not beat him — **contain** him. Pull the supports one at a time until the whole thing came down soft, and no one else had to die for it.

It was the kind of clean unmaking Melina would have admired, and she was the one he had to bring it to: the only person alive who'd take in both halves at once — the strategy and the man they were trying to reach — and tell him, dry as ever, exactly where it would fail. The feeling came up in him thin and electric, the one that had carried him through the whole plan from the first night. *We have him. Not to break him. To bring him in.*

He was still turning it over — how to reach Tania, how far to trust it, whether Silvanno could carry a warning to his own mother without it costing him — when the temple bells began.

He didn't hear them, not at first; they were part of the city the way his own pulse was part of him, and his mind went on laying charcoal down the map. But some part of him had been counting the strokes without being told to, and it reached the end of the count, and the number was wrong. It turned him cold a breath before he understood why.

He looked up. The light had gone long and low across the papers while he wasn't watching. He set the hour against the light, and the whole afternoon dropped out from under the work at once.

Her hour. The wall. Nearly gone.

He was up before the thought had finished. "Keep pulling on Tania," he told Farleen, already sweeping up the pages that mattered — "everything, however small. I want it past arguing by tonight." Then he was through the door and into the streets, the pages against his chest, moving faster than a man in his position had any business being seen.

He had something to bring her at last — the first real way through this that either of them had found — and under the hurry ran a bright, foolish eagerness he hadn't felt in weeks. She was going to love this. Not the warmth of it. The shape of it. She'd sit forward the way she did and find the one seam he'd missed, because she always found it, and between them they'd close it. He was rehearsing the first line before he'd reached the end of the street. *Rosik's moving on Larannas. No — look. It holds.*

He came around the last corner with his heart going and his breath short, the apology and the news already crowding into the same rushed sentence —

and the wall was empty.

He almost laughed, bent over the run of it, dragging air. Made it. He lowered himself onto the warm stone in the usual place, set his hands on his knees, and waited for his breath to level and for her to come around the corner so he could start in on it.

At the far end of the square a vendor was arguing with a customer over the price of something. A dog trotted through on business of its own. Two children ran a shrieking circle around the dry fountain until somebody called them and they went. The afternoon did the small ordinary things afternoons do, and Xion sat on the warm stone and watched them get done, and the eagerness he'd run all this way with, finding nowhere to land, began minute by minute to lose its shape. His eyes kept going to the mouth of the street that came down from the estate. Any moment now.

The sun moved. Xion frowned up at it; she should be here by now. He checked the pages — not because he'd forgotten what was on them, but just for something to do. The dog barked. The vendor's voice carried across the little square. He found he'd been reading the same sentence over and over.

*Where is she?*

The question came, unbidden, to his mind.

*Just come,* he thought. He wouldn't make her explain — whatever had kept her, he'd wave it off before she finished offering it; he had his own to spill first, Tania and the money and the line his father was about to cross, and they'd get to hers after. *Just walk around the corner. We don't even have to talk about why.*

He did not notice that he had begun to ask. He did not notice who he was asking.

Because — and this reached him slowly, the way cold water finds you — she was never late.

He turned it over, sitting there, and it held. Every meeting they had ever had at this wall, across every argument and every tired honesty and every report he didn't want to hear — she had never once been late. Not once. He came first and she came from the estate direction and the meeting began, and it had been as reliable as the turning of the day, so reliable that he understood, now, that he had never in his life wondered whether she would come. It had simply never been a question.

She was just late. That was all. Their father had called some meeting that ran long — a Coterie thing, the kind she couldn't leave without questions she didn't want asked. Or a visitor had come to the estate she couldn't turn away. Or she'd caught her foot on the bad paving up the hill and was sitting somewhere this very minute, cursing the stones, fine, only late. The explanations came in a steady, reasonable stream, and he let them come, and he knew even as he let them that he was letting them, because the only other thing to do was to sit with the thing he was not sitting with.

A woman came from the estate direction and his whole body turned to her before his mind could — and she was not Melina, had never been Melina, walked on past without ever knowing she had bought a man on a wall half a heartbeat of hope and then taken it back. It kept happening. Every figure from that direction. The hope fired without asking him, and there was something shameful in how it wouldn't stop.

He should go. He knew that too. He should stand up and walk to the estate, or send someone, or do any single thing other than sit in a public square and go on waiting. But standing up would change what this was. Right now, this was a man waiting for his sister, who was late. If he rose, if he went and *found out*, it would become something else, and he did not know what the something else was and he was not ready to know it. So he stayed. And the square went on being an ordinary square in an ordinary afternoon around a man who had not moved in a very long time.

---

He saw the messenger before the messenger saw him.

A man came into the square from the lower streets, moving with the particular gait of someone carrying an errand he'd give a great deal to be rid of — not hurrying, but deliberate, the walk of a man who wants a thing over with. He searched the square the way you search a place for one specific face. His eyes crossed Xion, came back, and settled.

And then he did a small thing. He swallowed. His eyes closed, just for a moment, and opened again.

Xion knew that flinch. He had worn it himself, in the corridor outside a room, in the last stretch of hallway before a family looked up and read the answer off his face before he had said a word. It was the thing you did when you had run out of time to find the gentle way and there was no gentle way, and there was only the floor to cross and the words to say. He knew it from the inside. He knew, watching the man square himself and start toward him, exactly what it meant.

He could not make himself stand up.

The man reached him and said something. Xion could barely hear it. There was a sound in his ears by then, a low roar rising, the ocean, or blood, and it caught the man's words and broke them into pieces before they could arrive.

"Lord Kemvimore." A pause that did a great deal of work. "I'm so sorry." Another. "Your sister—"

No.

*No.*

---

The square. The low wall. The sun where the sun was, tilting toward evening now. The vendor still there. The dog gone. The city going on, the way the city goes on, because it does not know how to do anything else.

He found he was leaning back against the wall, and did not know when that had happened. The stone was warm — it had held the day's heat all afternoon and it held it still against his spine, even as the light began to fail — and this fact was somehow the most vivid thing in the world, while everything else had gone too bright at the edges and arrived too loud. He was a healer. He could name it. The narrowing at the sides of his vision, the thin fast thread of his own pulse, the lightness climbing behind his eyes — he had sat beside people in exactly this state and told them, in a level and unhurried voice, *this is your body answering a shock; breathe; it will pass.* He knew the name of every part of what was happening to him, and the knowing did nothing at all. He had always been the calm voice in the room. He was, now, the one on the table.

*Same time next week?*

---

The square. The low wall. The estate direction, empty. The vendor gathered up his goods and left, whistling something Xion couldn't make out. The light went slowly, the way it goes. Xion sat on the wall where he always sat, and did not move, and could not have said how long he had been there.

In some part of himself he could not reach, or reason with, he was still waiting for her to come around the corner.
