He began the way his father would have begun: not with anger, and not yet with a plan, but with a map.

Not a map of streets. There was another kind — the kind Rosik carried behind his eyes and had spent Xion's boyhood trying to teach him to draw, in the years when he still thought his son might inherit the use of him and not only the name. Every house, every cartel, every alliance was a structure, Rosik held, and every structure had a shape you could learn to see if you were patient enough and unsentimental enough to look; and somewhere in every shape there was one member bearing more of the load than all the rest. Find that, and you never had to raise your voice. You pulled the one beam and stood back and let the weight do the work of falling.

Xion had hated the lesson. He had gone and apprenticed himself, in secret, to the opposite of it — to Mira, and to the sight that read a body for where it hurt so that the hurt could be closed. He had held the two apart his whole life, the healing in one hand and his father's cold geometry pushed as far from it as his arm would reach, and he had told himself the distance between them was the whole of who he was.

The trouble was that the geometry had gone in anyway. You cannot sit at a man's table for twenty years and fail to learn the shape of how he thinks, however hard you refuse to want it. It had been in him the entire time, unused, waiting — and now, at the little table where Master Fen used to grind his poultices, he took it out and looked through it on purpose for the first time in his life. Not the healer's sight. That one he left where it belonged, off to the side, clean; he would not carry it into this. The other one. His father's. He turned it on his father's money and began, coldly and well, to draw.

---

The wealth that could be seen — the granaries, the barge fleet, the tidy visible fortune of the Lord of Grain — was not the wealth that mattered, and Xion had understood that since he was old enough to notice which ledgers his father kept in which rooms. The visible fortune was a face. Behind it, written in no book a magistrate could ever demand, ran the other money: the coin from the quiet arrangements, the debts that were not debts, the businesses that paid for a protection they had never asked to buy, the silences that had to be purchased and then purchased again. Extortion money. Blackmail money. Money that existed precisely because it could never be admitted to exist.

Which meant — and the clarity of it went through him like cold water — that it was money his father could never report stolen. You do not go to the Arol Batae and swear out a complaint over the theft of coin you have sworn on your life you never possessed.

That was the fracture. That was the one beam. Rosik's shadow wealth was the member that held the whole edifice up — it was what bought the loyalty and paid the muscle and kept the smaller houses sworn and the enforcers fed — and it was, by its own guilty nature, the single part of the structure that could not defend itself in daylight. Take it, and Rosik could not chase it through any lawful door. Take enough of it, all at once, and the man who had bought this city one silence at a time would wake with nothing left to buy the next silence with.

He knew the shape of where it would be kept, because the shape was his father's and he had spent a lifetime memorizing his father without meaning to. But the shape was not the specifics — which warehouse, which false-fronted brokerage, which "failed" venture was quietly holding a room of strongboxes — and for the specifics he had to go carefully back into a world that no longer quite claimed him. There were still people in the Grain cartel's lower reaches who remembered the heir: who had liked him once, or had cause to owe him, or simply hated the men above them enough to whisper to the one person certain to use it against the top of the house. A warehouse clerk. A barge-master's widow. A factor passed over one time too many. He went to them the way his father would have gone to them — without threat, offering each the one thing that particular person wanted and nothing more — and he hated how easily it came, and he did it anyway, and one whispered specific at a time the map filled in.

Six sites. He sat with the finished thing for a long while, and it was good work, and knowing it was good work was its own kind of sickness.

---

He brought the map to the three of them in the back room of the dead clinic, because it was the last place left that was his and unwatched.

Kael studied it longest. He did not ask why; the why had been settled already, in each of their own minds, the night they had all arrived at the same name. He asked how. He ran a blunt finger from one marked site to the next, counting, and when he looked up his face had the stillness of a man who has understood a thing completely and does not love it.

"All at once," he said.

"All at once," Xion agreed. "Every site, the same night, the same hour. The instant he hears one strongroom's been opened, he moves the rest — he has the wagons and the men and the nerve to empty six warehouses across this city before dawn, and he will. There's no second night. We get one, and we take all of it, or we take a fraction and hand him a son he now knows is coming for his money, with all the time in the world to bury the rest where none of us will ever find it."

They took their pieces off the map the way water finds the low channels. Silvanno would read the weather — his mother's whole apparatus existed to know where coin was moving in this city, and he could turn one careful corner of it to tell them the night Rosik's attention was pointed elsewhere. Kael would find the hands: Iron men who could open a strongroom without noise and carry what was in it, and hold a door if a door needed holding. And Farleen would walk each site first, the way only Farleen could, and come back with the counts — the guards, the locks, the ways in and the ways back out — because a plan drawn on clean paper was a wish until someone who knew how to be invisible had stood inside it and made it real.

It was, Xion thought, watching the three of them bend over the map, precisely the operation his father would have run, staffed the way his father would have staffed it, each person set to the one task their particular gift best fit. He had built a small Rosik in the back of a dead clinic, and it was going to work, and the knowing of that sat in him without a scrap of warmth.

Farleen looked up at him a beat too long. She didn't say anything — she rarely said the thing; she only made sure you knew she'd seen it — and what she had seen, he understood, was that the man giving the orders was standing the way his father stood and pitching his voice the way his father pitched it, low and level and sure. She held his eyes for that one beat. Then she went back to the map, and said nothing, and the nothing followed him the rest of the night.

---

He was at the wall first the next afternoon. He was always first. And Melina crossed the square from the estate at the hour she always came, and for the length of her crossing he let himself have the ordinary version of it — a sister, a low wall, the swept dry fountain, the one fixed point in a spinning month.

Then she sat down and looked at him, and the ordinary version did not survive the looking.

"What have you done." Not sharp. Almost afraid. "No — what are you **about** to do. You've got a look on you I've only ever seen on one other face in my life, and I have spent that whole life learning to be frightened of it."

He didn't answer, and she took the silence for what it was and pressed on.

"I did what I told you I'd do. I looked. Every ledger I could reach and every whisper I could buy, I looked for the thing that would prove he ordered it or prove he didn't — and there's nothing, Xion. No seam. If he did it, he did it in a way that left me not one thread to pull, which is either proof of his guilt or proof that he's Rosik, and from where I stand those two look exactly alike." Her hands were still in her lap, which from Melina meant more than wringing them would have. "So I can't hand you innocence. I know that's what you wanted from me, and I can't give it to you. But I can't hand you guilt either — and you're sitting there having already decided, and I am begging you to notice that you decided it without proof."

"And if I have," he said, carefully.

"Then don't do the thing the deciding leads to. Whatever it is." She gestured at nothing, at all of it, at the look on his face. "Or if you won't hear *don't*, then hear the truest thing I know, the one you keep behaving as though you've forgotten. Father can be **reached**. He always can. I have watched that man take people who wanted him in the ground and turn them into people who dined at his table, and he did it every single time the same way — not by beating them, by finding the one thing they wanted more than they wanted to fight him, and handing it to them. There is always an angle with him. Always. Whatever he's done or hasn't done, there is a version of this that ends with the two of you across a table instead of over each other's wreckage. It exists. I could **find** it, if you'd let me look for it instead of — this."

And he almost told her — that was the worst of it, her frightened earnest face turned up to his in the failing light — because the thing he could not say was that he **agreed** with her. She thought he'd forgotten that Rosik could be reasoned with. He hadn't. He was counting on it. He did not need to find the angle at a table, the way she wanted; he needed only to take from his father, in a single night, everything his father fought with, and leave him facing a loss even he could not talk his way around. And then Rosik would do what Rosik had always done — look at a thing that could not be won and, coldly and sensibly, let it go. Not surrender; his father did not surrender. Withdraw. Cut the loss and step back to build again and bide his time, because that was the reasonable move, and Rosik was the most relentlessly reasonable man Xion had ever known.

He had seen his father angry. He had seen him disappointed past speech, and determined to the edge of something frightening, and cold in every shade a man can be cold. He had never once, in twenty years, seen him come undone — not cornered, not rattled, not for the space of a single breath — and some part of Xion doubted the man was built for it. So he was as certain as he had ever been of anything he had set his hand to: when the money was gone, his father would be reasonable about it, in the bloodless unhurried way he was reasonable about everything, because being reasonable was the one thing Rosik Kemvimore had never in his life failed to be.

He could not say a word of it without handing her the plan, and he would not hand her the plan — because the moment she held it, she would have to choose between warning her father and betraying him, and that was a choice he would not force into her hands.

So he said none of it. He said, "You're not wrong that he's reasonable. You've never once been wrong about that."

She searched his face for the catch. "But you'll do the thing anyway."

"I'll do the thing anyway."

She let out a slow breath, and didn't quite manage to hide how little the agreeing had comforted her — because she had known him too long not to hear how finished he was, under the gentle words.

She left it there, because she was Melina and she knew to the inch how far a point could be pushed before pushing cost more than it earned. She stood and brushed the dust from the wall, and the fear had not gone out of her, but she folded it away where she folded everything, under the composure, out of sight.

"I'm not finished trying," she said. "Not with him and not with you. You should both know that about me by now." A breath, and a thin thread of the old dryness came back. "I've a supper to be charming at and a brother being a fool in a public square, and I can mend exactly one of those before dark, so I'll go be charming and leave you to the other. Let me have another look at him. I'll come back when I've something worth telling — give me two days. You'll have thought better of whatever this is by then, and we'll each pretend the other one came round."

"Two days," he said.

"Give or take." She squeezed his shoulder once, the way she always did, and went back across the square toward the estate the way she always went — the straight back, the unhurried step, a woman walking off into the rest of her day as though the day had better be ready for her.

---

That night he did not sleep, and he did not try to.

He sat at the little table with the finished map in front of him and the single lamp burning low, and he turned the thing over, and it would not stay decided. Everything was in place, or would be: the hands, the weather, the counts, the one dark hour after which his father would be a different kind of dangerous than he had ever been. There was nothing left to arrange. There was only the deciding, which he had done in Elara's bedchamber and kept having to do again, because it refused to hold still.

He knew people would die. Not as an abstraction — he had stood too close to too many dying bodies to be capable of holding it as an abstraction. Break a thing the size of the Grain cartel's shadow economy, in a city already living a bad season from the edge, and the shock would travel outward in ways no map could mark. Wages unpaid. Arrangements collapsed. The fragile, ugly balance that kept the low wards fed lurching into something worse before it found its feet, if it ever found them. Coin that was flowing tonight would stop, and somebody who ate this week would not eat next week, somewhere he would never see it happen — and it would be because of a decision a man had made at a little table in a dead clinic.

And he turned it over, and turned it over, and it always came out the same, which was the unbearable part: not that the answer was easy, but that it was **stable**. Do nothing, and the machine ran on exactly as it was — the tokens, the wells, the hereditary arithmetic of who was worth water — smooth and reasonable and grinding people up forever, his father's steady hand on the wheel and a whole city grateful for the ride. That was a permanent dying, invisible and certain, and it had a body count too, and its body count never once stopped. Act, and he traded that quiet endless dying for a burst of loud and chaotic dying now, the price of pulling the beam, the wreckage of the fall — on the single hope that something less murderous could be raised in the clear space afterward.

There was no innocent road here. That was the whole truth of it, and he made himself hold both halves the way Melina held both halves of everything, without flinching toward the kinder one. He was not choosing between doing harm and doing none. He was choosing which harm, and to whom, and when, for people who had never asked him to and would never learn his name. There was no version of this that cost nothing. There was only the version where he told himself the cost of standing still was not his to answer for — because he had not been the one to set it moving — and he had watched his father live inside exactly that comfort for twenty years, and he would not take it for himself.

So he would carry it. Both the dying he was about to cause and the dying he was trying to end, and the fact that he could never prove the second outweighed the first. He would carry it with his eyes open — which was, he thought, the one part of this that was still his own and not his father's. Rosik broke things and slept. Xion was going to break this thing and not intend to sleep easily about it ever again, and sitting there in the low lamplight he found that he wanted it that way. The weight was the last part of him that was still a healer.

He meant to keep it.
