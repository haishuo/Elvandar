For three days the gate told him nothing, because he no longer went to the gate.

There was no point in it. He sat in the two rooms that had been the clinic and were now just the place where a useless man waited, and he did the thing he had spent a career teaching other people not to do: nothing. Kael brought what he could, which was little. Silvanno brought less. Farleen brought herself and a pastry he did not eat and sat with him in the dark, and did not once pretend that any of it helped. *Elara is alive. Elara has not woken.* That was the whole of the news, handed back to him in slightly different shapes morning and evening, and not one of the shapes gave him a single thing to do about it.

Then, on the evening of the third day, someone knocked who had never knocked before.

When he opened the door there was an Arol Batae guardsman on the step, in the deep blue he had spent three months learning to read as a wall. Xion braced for the courtesy, the inclination of the head, the *you're welcome to wait* — and none of it came.

"Lord Kemvimore. You're to come with me." The man's face held nothing he could read. "Her Imperial Majesty has commanded it. Now, my lord. If you're able."

---

The gate opened.

That was the thing he could not stop feeling, walking through it — through the gate that had not opened for him in three months, across the courtyard gone violet in the dusk, into the long cool corridors he had walked once at her shoulder and been walked out of by men in exactly this blue. The same stone. The same silence. The same faultless machine that had filed him under **nothing** — and all of it parting in front of him now like water pushed aside, because somewhere at the heart of it a single voice had said his name with enough weight behind it to bend the whole apparatus out of true.

He had spent three months learning that personal wish was the one currency the institution would not take. It turned out there was a wish it could not refuse. Not the wish of the man who had found her. The command of the woman on the throne.

They searched him before the last door — thoroughly, without apology, a second man running hands down his arms and his sides while the first held a lamp to his face, and he understood and did not resent it. Three days ago something had gotten past these men and nearly killed the person they existed to keep alive. They would be searching everyone, now, for the rest of their lives. A woman in the robes of the imperial household — a physician, by the way she stood — studied him a moment longer than the guards had, and then stood aside.

The Arol Batae took station at the door and did not follow him through it. Whatever lay on the other side was, it seemed, above even their standing.

---

The room was dim, curtained against the last of the light, and it took his eyes a moment to find her. Then they did, and the healer in him arrived before the rest of him could, because the healer was faster and did not have to survive what it saw.

She was propped against pillows in a bed built for someone larger, and she was alive, and she was wrecked. Not dying — his eye told him that in the first half-second, and the relief of it came so hard it was almost a pain of its own. But the poison had been through her and left its signature in every place he knew to look. The grey cast under her skin, where the blood wasn't reaching. The split lips of a body that had spent days trying to purge everything and getting no water back. The fine constant tremor in the hand that lay on the coverlet, the nerves still paying, in small change, for whatever had been in that cup. Someone had chosen well. He knew it the way he knew a clean break from a bad one — this had been meant to kill, by a hand that understood exactly how, and it had failed by no wider a margin than the care she'd been given after.

"You look," Elara said, and her voice was a ruin of itself, worn down to a rasp, "like you're taking my pulse from across the room."

"I am," he said.

Something moved at the corner of her mouth and cost her to move it. "It's slow," she said. "In case your eyes can't reach. Slow and it skips. The physician thinks it will right itself." A breath, carefully spent. "She thinks a great many things and tells me almost none of them, which is how I know it's bad. You'll tell me. That's half of why I sent for you. You always tell me."

---

He crossed to the chair beside the bed — no one had said he could, and there was no one left in the room to say he couldn't — and sat, close enough now to see that the fury he'd have expected was there and was not, mostly, aimed at him, though some of it was, and the some-of-it was the part that had reached three months through a wall to summon him.

"I woke this morning," she said, "and before the physicians, before I could hold a cup, before I even knew what day it was — the first thing I did was tell them to bring you. And they **hesitated**, Xion. Half-dead, giving the one command I've ever given that no clerk could file into a drawer, and they stood there deciding whether it was allowed. I had to remind them whose city this is." Her jaw tightened. "I'm still not sure they believe me."

"Elara—"

"Why did you stop."

It came out flat, stripped of everything, and that was how he knew it was the thing underneath all the other things. "Why did you stop writing to me. And don't — " the rasp cracked and she pushed through it " — don't tell me you didn't. I know what came. I read every line of it looking for you. *The welfare of the city. In good health.* Court manners, from the man who once told me the truth in a dark room when the truth could have hanged us both. I waited months for one real word from you. And what I got was a secretary's hand telling me you hoped I was keeping well."

And the floor went out from under him — not because it was an accusation but because it was a mirror, held up at exactly the wrong angle, and he was looking straight into his own three months turned back to front. His four pages. Her four lines. He had sat at that little table night after night deciding she had drifted, that the throne had done to her what thrones did, that she had quietly stopped reaching — and she had spent those same nights on the far side of the same wall, deciding the identical thing about him.

"I never stopped," he said. "Not once. I wrote you every week — pages, not pleasantries. The wards, the wells, the reform, my father, all of it, things that could have hanged us both if the wrong hand opened them, and I carried them to the courier myself so I'd know they left my hand whole." He watched it reach her, watched her begin to understand the shape of it. "You never got a word of it. You got the welfare of the city. And I got four lines of secretariat and decided you'd gone cold on me — because I couldn't prove it was the wall, and it was easier to bear if it was you."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them the fury had drained out through some crack in the exhaustion, and what was left was only the exhaustion, and under it something worse. "The same wall," she said. "The whole time. Both of us."

"Both of us."

He reached out and took the trembling hand off the coverlet — the clinical part of him still counting the tremor by old habit, the rest of him only needing, for a moment, to hold something of hers that was warm and moving and alive — and she let him, and turned her hand so their fingers laced, and for the length of a few breaths neither of them said anything at all. It was the first quiet, he thought, that either of them had been handed in three months.

---

"You want to know what it's like in here." She said it into the dimness, not looking at him now, looking at the canopy above the bed. "I can see you building it, out there. Some idea of me up on a throne, deciding things. Let me spare you." A long, careful breath. "I have decided nothing. I proposed ending the tokens and they told me it would take five years and kill people in the meantime, and they were right, and it died. I proposed ten smaller things and lost all ten. And then your father mended the wells his own way — the way that leaves every rotten beam standing — and it **worked**, and I had to get up in front of the city and thank him for it." Her hand tightened in his, weakly. "I thanked them, Xion. I wore the crown you bled to put on me, and I stood on a dais, and I thanked the men I had come to that throne meaning to destroy. And I smiled while I did it, because the other choice was to look like a spiteful girl who'd rather her people go thirsty than admit she'd been beaten."

He had heard the shape of all of it — from Kael, from Silvanno, from Melina at the wall. He had not heard it from inside, in her sanded-down voice, and it was worse from inside. Everything, he was learning, was worse from inside.

"Master Fen," she said, quieter, "saved more people than Empress Elara ever will. I've had three days on my back to do the arithmetic and it isn't close, it isn't even near close." She turned her head on the pillow to look at him. "You took me out of that compound and you showed me a city, and I have never in my life trusted anyone the way I trusted you for it — for handing me the real thing when every other soul I'd ever known handed me a story. And the real thing you showed me put me on a throne where I cannot touch a single one of the wounds you showed me. You gave me sight. And then they sat me down somewhere I can only ever watch."

---

He was quiet a while, holding her hand, listening to her breathe without meaning to — counting it, because it was the sound of the thing he'd been told for three days he could not save, saving itself, one rasp at a time.

Then he said the thing he had carried three months of wall to be unable to say, and could say now only because the wall had opened for the worst reason there was.

"What they gave you," he said. "I've been sitting with it for three days, and I need you to hear it from someone who knows what he's looking at. That was not a warning, and it was not a madman, and it was not ill luck at a table. That was a professional's work. Someone spent a great deal of money on a hand that knew precisely how much would stop your heart, and how to get it into you and nothing else, and they very nearly bought exactly what they paid for." He held her eyes. "That wasn't chaos, Elara. It was a decision. And decisions have a person standing behind them."

"I know." Her voice didn't waver. "I've had three days flat on my back to think about whose."

"Then walk it with me. Because I've walked it a hundred times, and I keep arriving at the same door, and I need you to tell me whether you arrive there too — or whether I've simply talked myself into the answer I was always going to reach."

He counted them off the way he had been counting them off since the flower market, except that now, aloud, with her, it had the terrible orderliness of a diagnosis.

"Ronas." He watched the name land on her — she knew it, she had stood in front of the man in the Iron Hall with a half-dead boy in her arms. "You've met him. You know what he is. If Ronas Dermir decided you were worth killing, there'd be no cup, no cook, no clever poison. He'd come through the front door with a sword, or send twenty men who would, and he'd make certain you knew whose sword it was, because the knowing is half of what he sells. Poison in a wine glass is a coward's tool, or a subtle man's. Ronas is neither. It isn't his shape."

"No," she agreed, low. "He'd want to watch."

"Tania Larannas." Harder, this one — the obvious one, the one the whole city was already saying. "Her people poured your wine. Her household prepared the table. If you're the Arol Batae, that's where you begin and you might never look further. But think about what Tania actually **is**. She's spent this entire crisis refusing to plant a single flag — waiting to see who wins so she can swear she'd been leaning that way all along. She bends. She survives. Killing an empress is the one move she can never take back, the one move that ends the game whether it lands or not, and Tania Larannas does not make a move she can't walk away from." He shook his head. "Her people poured the wine. But pouring the wine is not the same as putting the poison in it. Someone used her table. That is not the same as Tania setting it."

Elara was watching him now with a focus he remembered from the masquerade training — the look she got when she stopped performing and started genuinely learning a thing. "Sa Ko Ren."

And despite all of it he almost smiled, because it was almost funny, and Elara of all people would know exactly how funny. "You fought her. You tell me. Is Sa Ko Ren going to poison a cup?"

Something that was nearly a laugh crossed the ruin of her face and hurt her doing it; he saw it hurt her. "She'd be insulted I asked," Elara said. "She'd call it the lowest thing a person could stoop to, and challenge you to a duel for suggesting she'd stoop to it. If Sa Ko Ren wanted me dead, she'd have done it in the training yard, to my face, and called it an honor and meant the honor." A breath. "She would never in her life put something in my cup."

"No," Xion said. "She wouldn't."

He spread his hands. And at the last he found he did not want to be the one to say it — that after three months of the whole city deciding what Rosik Kemvimore was, he needed the name to come from somewhere that wasn't a son's grievance.

"So," he said. "Who does that leave?"

Elara looked at him for a long moment.

"…Rosik," she said.

He nodded. And hated that he nodded.

"And yet you don't look like a man who's solved anything," she said, because she was as quick as he had always known she was, quick enough to feel the seam in it. "You look like a man who got the answer he expected and trusts it less for having expected it."

"Because it doesn't fit him." There. Out loud — the thing Melina had pressed into his chest at the wall three days ago, the thing he had not once been able to shake since. "That's what I keep catching on. He was **winning**, Elara. He'd already beaten you. He didn't need you dead — he needed you ornamental, and he had that; he had the wells and the reform and a whole city thanking him and calling it good government. A man doesn't set fire to a victory. Killing you now is an enormous risk run by a man who, three days ago, was getting everything he wanted at no risk whatsoever. It's the one piece that won't lie flat. It doesn't fit him."

"Then who," Elara said, "does it fit better?"

And that was the whole of it, laid open in a single question, and neither of them had a thing to set against it. He turned it over one more time in front of her, the way he had turned it a hundred times alone — Ronas who would use a sword, Tania who would never take the risk, Sa Ko who would sooner die than stoop to it — and past those three there was no one. There was no fourth chair at that table. Every road out of the question ran straight into a wall, and there was exactly one gap in the wall, and his father's name stood over it, and the plain fact that his father had no good reason to walk through it was not the same thing as there being anyone else who could.

"No one," he said at last. "It doesn't fit him. And there is no one it fits better. I've tried, Elara. There's no one else with the reach to put a hand inside Tania's own household and turn it into a knife. No one else with the money and the patience to do it clean and leave her holding the cup. He doesn't fit — and he's the only one who fits at all."

---

The dim room held nothing for a while but the sound of her breathing, which he went on listening to, counting, because it was the sound of the thing he had been told he could not save.

He had come into that room a man with nothing he could do. Sitting in the chair no one had given him leave to take, he understood that this had stopped being true — and that its stopping was the worst thing in the room, worse than the poison, worse than the wall. Because the thing he could do now was the thing he had, all his life, made certain he would never do.

"They won't reform," he said. "The Coterie. You've spent three months proving it, and it nearly cost you your life to prove it. They can't be talked onto a better road, because the road they're on **works** — it fills the wells, and it keeps the tokens, and it grinds people up so quietly that everyone thanks them for the water. You can't out-propose it. You can't out-vote it. You certainly can't out-wait it; waiting is the thing it does best of all." He heard his own voice go level and flat and somewhere he didn't quite recognize. "It can't be fixed from the inside. It can only be broken from the outside. And I am the only man in this city who was raised on the inside and is standing on the outside."

Elara was very still against the pillows. "You're talking about your father."

"I'm talking about all of it. He's only the beam that holds the rest up."

"People will die, Xion." She said it not as an objection but as a fact she needed to be sure he had already counted. "You break a thing that size, in a city this near the edge — people who are drinking today stop drinking. People die of it. You know that."

"I know."

"Is that a price you can pay?"

"Is the cost of doing nothing higher?"

Neither of them answered it. The question hung in the dim air between the bed and the chair, and they both looked at it, and they both knew the answer, and neither of them said it — because saying it aloud was a different kind of act than knowing it, and once it was said it could not be taken back.

---

He stayed until the physician came to send him out — until Elara's eyes had lost the long fight to stay open and the rasp had thinned to almost nothing, and she had made him promise, in the last of it, to come back. A promise the guards outside would have to learn to honor now, whether their protocols cared for it or not. He promised. He meant it. It was the one clean thing in the room.

Then he walked back out through the gate that had opened for him, into a city that was, for one more night, still his father's.

He did not feel the cold settle. There was no moment he could have pointed to, no line he felt himself step across. He had made himself a healer, year by year, precisely so that he would never have to become the other thing his blood had on offer — and walking downhill in the dark, already turning over warehouses whose locations he did not yet know and men whose names he had not yet learned, he understood that he was going to reach for the other thing now, and use the whole of it. Not the healer's eye — that one was Mira's, and she had only ever given it to him for mending; he would not drag it down into this. The other sight. His father's. The patience that could wait years for a thing to ripen. The reading of a system not for where it hurt, but for where it would break. All of it, turned around and aimed back up the hill at the man who had put it in him.

He was going to become his father, in order to break his father.

He could not find another road. And somewhere in that dim room, holding a hand that shook, he had stopped looking for one.
