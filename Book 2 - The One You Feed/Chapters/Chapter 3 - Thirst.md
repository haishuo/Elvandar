He walked because the gate was no longer worth standing at, and because the streets were the last part of the city that still felt like his.

He had gone up to the palace wall that morning out of habit more than hope, handed a letter into a courier's gloved hand, been assured the Empress trusted he was in good health, and then turned downhill and simply kept turning downhill — out of the Noble District's watered shade, past the fountains still throwing their bright wasteful arcs into the heat, and down into the parts of Kaha'an that did the actual work of being a city. He was not going anywhere. He had stopped pretending, somewhere around the third street, that he was.

Down here the fountains ended and the wells began, and the wells had lines, and the lines had the particular patience of people who had stood in them their whole lives. He read the city the way he could not stop reading it. A woman carrying a jar with both arms curled around it, the way you carry a thing worth more than its weight. A tally-man at a wellhead with the Water cartel's mark on his sleeve, checking tokens against a ledger before he'd let the bucket down. The ordinary machinery of thirst, running exactly as it always had.

He almost walked past the girl.

That was the cruelty of it done slowly — it did not announce itself the way a wound did. A wound had blood, urgency, a crowd. This was only a child sitting in a doorway out of the sun, very still, her head tipped against the frame, and it was the **stillness** that turned him back on his heel before he had finished deciding to. Children her age did not sit that still. Not the living ones.

He crouched in front of her. Seven, perhaps eight, though hunger and the heat made that a guess. Her eyes were open and sunken, ringed in a bruised grey, and they moved to his face slowly, as though his face had arrived from a long way off. Her lips had cracked and split at the center. When he took her hand and pinched the skin on the back of it gently, the fold stood up for a moment before it sank — a tent of skin that should have flattened at once and didn't.

"Hey," he said, low, the voice he kept for the frightened ones. "Can you tell me your name?"

Her mouth worked. Nothing came.

"She's called Sela." The mother was already there, out of the dark of the doorway and down on her knees beside them, her own face gone tight with the particular shame of the poor caught needing something. Her hands hovered, wanting to snatch the child back from the stranger and not daring to, because the stranger clearly knew what he was doing and she clearly did not know what else to do. "She was fine. She was fine yesterday, she was just tired, I thought she was just —"

"You did nothing wrong." He said it flatly, because it was true and because she needed to hear it in a voice that wasn't asking anything of her. The mother's eyes went to the jar just inside the door — a hand-span of water in the bottom of it, rationed by the mouthful because it had to last. "It's not enough," she said, following his look, the words coming in a rush now that someone was here who might do something with them. "It's never enough. They cut the allotment again after the coronation, everyone swore it would get **better** —" She stopped herself. You did not say things about the cartels aloud, not even now, not even with a new empress on a throne somewhere up the hill.

He was not going to take a dying child's water out of a dying child's house. He looked back the way he'd come instead — the wellhead on the corner, the tally-man with the cartel's mark on his sleeve, the bright ordinary fact of a full cistern not forty paces off. The girl was dying of **permission**, not of drought. The water had been there the whole time.

"Wet her lips with what's in the jar — just wet, not a mouthful," he told the mother, already rising. "Stay with her. I won't be a moment."

The tally-man looked up at his approach with the practiced irritation of a man whose whole day was telling people the number was the number. The irritation held right up until something in Xion's face caught, turned over, and resolved into a name.

"…Lord Kemvimore." He actually straightened.

Xion hated hearing it. He always would. Master Fen could read the girl's pulse and count out spoonfuls and coax a body back from the lip of drought — and Master Fen could not get one cup past this man's ledger. Only the other one could do that. Only the name he had spent years trying to set down.

"The house in that doorway." He nodded back at it. "A full allotment. This week, next week, the month, if I take it into my head to come back and check. Put it on House Kemvimore."

"My lord, it's — the ledger's set for the quarter, it's by token, I can't just—"

"You can." Not loud, not a threat. Only the flat certainty of a man who had spent his whole childhood watching exactly how far that name could shove a thing that wasn't meant to move. "You'll enter it. If anyone asks who authorized it, you give them the name, and the name will be enough. It always is. We both know it always is." A beat. "The water now. Two jars, to carry."

Two jars came to the brim, and no ledger was checked, because *no* was a word for people without the right name — and whatever else Xion had managed to throw away, the name had turned out to be the one thing that wouldn't come off.

He carried the water back and did the slow work with it. A body this dry, given the jar all at once, would only heave it up again and lose what little it had — the worst mercy there was. He wet her lips, then gave it to her a spoonful at a time, a slow count between each, his fingers at the pulse in her wrist reading the thin fast thread of it, talking to her the while — nonsense, the weather, the color of the door — anything to keep those far-off eyes coming back to his face. By the tenth spoonful the pulse had steadied a fraction under his fingers. By the twentieth she was holding the cup herself, weakly, which was the thing he had been waiting for: the body remembering it wanted to live.

It took the better part of an hour. He left them the second jar still full and what coin he could spare, and told the mother how to go on through the night — small and often, and to find a healer if the child stopped keeping it down. He did not say *find Master Fen*. Master Fen had a name people knew now, and a name people knew was a name that drew a crowd, and he had not been that man in weeks. But the hands did not ask whose they were. The hands only did the work.

And the work, he thought — straightening in the doorway with his knees complaining, the girl watching him now with something almost like a child's ordinary wariness — the work was treating the symptom. He had steadied this one. He had a name that could pry a well open and a purse for the rest, this once, for this doorway. But he could not buy a jar for every doorway in the low city, any more than he had hands enough to reach every still child in it, and he knew it — the purse had a bottom, the hands had ten fingers, the doorways had no end. The thing that had put the child there was not a thirst his coin could reach or a fever his hands could break. It was a number in a ledger. It was a room up the hill where two councils that both meant well argued about who should be allowed to change the number, and changed nothing, and the not-changing had a weight, and the weight came down here.

He walked out of the lower city carrying it.

---

He reached the low wall before she did.

It was the same quiet square as before, the same swept and waterless fountain at its heart, the same stretch of sun-warmed stone at the edge where the Noble District softened toward everything below it. He sat where he had sat the first time and waited, and after a while Melina came across the square from the direction of the estate, walking the route she walked, and dropped onto the stone beside him already halfway into a grievance.

"If one more person tells me the tariff tables can't be touched before the quarter turns, I am going to touch them myself, with a hammer." She said it without any real heat, the way you air a complaint you've no intention of acting on — and then she looked at him properly, and stopped. "You've been down in the wards. You've got the look."

"There was a girl." He didn't elaborate. He found he couldn't, quite. "She'll live. This time."

Melina closed her eyes for a moment, and when she opened them the report came out of her the way bad news comes out of someone who has decided the kindest thing is to be quick about it. "It's going to get worse before it does anything else, and I need you to hear how, because you'll hear it wrong from everyone else." The ring turned once under her thumb. "Elara went for the whole thing this week. Not a reform. Abolition. Hereditary water tokens struck out entirely, root and branch, the whole distribution framework gone."

"And the Coterie killed it."

"The Coterie killed it, and Xion —" she looked at him "— they weren't lying about why. You can't strike out the framework overnight. It's the thing the wells actually **run** on. There's nothing built to catch the city if you pull it, and a whole city doesn't get to wait politely in the rubble while someone invents the replacement. Father said it would take five years to do safely. He's not wrong about the five years. That's the part nobody down in your wards will believe, and it's still true."

"So they blocked her and offered nothing."

"They offered plenty." And there it was, the thing that was grinding her down, coming into her voice flat and exact. "Father's people put up an alternative the same afternoon. Keep the framework, fix the allotments inside it. Reweight the quotas toward the low wards, tighten the logistics, stop the tally-men skimming. It wasn't justice. It kept every rotten thing about the system in place." She spread her hands, empty. "It also would have put water in that girl's house by the end of the month. It would have *saved people*, Xion. This season. Real ones."

He already knew the shape of the ending. "Elara rejected it."

"Because it saves the system by improving it. Because the day the token system starts working a little better is the day it becomes impossible to tear out, and she is not on that throne to make the cage more comfortable." Melina's mouth thinned. "And she's not wrong either. That's the whole horror of it. Father is right that you can't pull the framework without killing people, and Elara is right that fixing the framework means the framework wins, and both of them are **right**, and they cancel to nothing, and the nothing is what's standing in that girl's doorway."

For a while neither of them said anything. Out past the wall a cart went by, and a gull that had come up from the harbor picked its way across the dry fountain looking for water that wasn't there.

"You saw him," Xion said finally. "This week. Our father. How is he — actually."

Melina considered how to answer that, which was itself an answer. "He believes it," she said. "That's the thing I can't make either side understand. He isn't obstructing her to keep his pile. I mean — he is, he'd never let the pile go, but that isn't why he sleeps at night. He lived through the Rending. He watched a city with no framework at all, and he watched what filled the empty space, and it was worse than anything the tokens do. Everything he built, he built on the memory of that. When he looks at Elara he genuinely sees a young woman about to knock out the one wall holding the roof up because she doesn't like the wall." She shook her head slowly. "He's wrong. The roof's been crushing people the whole time; he just can't see them from where he stands. But he's not lying, and he's not a fool, and I am so tired of people needing him to be one or the other."

Xion looked at the empty fountain and did the arithmetic he had been not-doing since the doorway.

He had told her the truth. There was no untelling it. In another life Princess Elara might have come to the throne knowing only what a princess is shown — the fountains, the ceremonies, the clean version of the city — and she might have taken Rosik's improved allotments with gratitude and called it progress and put water in that girl's house by the end of the month. Instead Xion had walked her through the wards himself. He had shown her the tally-men and the fabricated debts and the exact machinery of who was allowed to drink. He had done it because it was true, and because she needed to see it to be worth anything up there, and he would do it again.

And it had worked. She saw it now. She saw it so clearly that she could not touch the one thing that would have helped, because touching it meant blessing it. He had given her sight, and the sight was what stayed her hand, and the girl in the doorway was — not only, not simply, but really, along one thread among many that his own hand had helped to spin — the cost of a truth he still could not bring himself to regret.

He was a man who saw causal threads. That was the trouble with him. He could see this one all the way back to his own mouth.

"You've gone somewhere again," Melina said quietly.

"I'm here." He wasn't, quite, but he came back for her. "Next week, then?"

"Next week." She stood and brushed the dust from her skirts, already composed, already half back in the day ahead of her. "Try to eat something. And stop deciding Elara's rejecting you — I can see you've decided it, it's all over your face. Nobody in that palace has time to reject anybody; there's no room in there for anything that isn't the fire. When she comes up for air, she'll come up to you." She was moving now, back toward the estate. "I'm going to have another run at her next session, by the way — get her to take Father's allotment fix as a stopgap. Just this season, just while the wells are killing people, and swear blind it doesn't touch her abolition. A truce, not a surrender. If I can make it sound like the first and not the second, she might swallow it, and that girl of yours has water by the month's end." A grimace. "It's a long shot. She's stubborn as a locked door. Ask me next week — I'll know if I got anywhere."

Then she was gone across the square, back toward the estate and the next session, and Xion sat on a while with the empty fountain, holding the two things she'd handed him — the arithmetic of the council and the arithmetic of the doorway — and finding no sum that came out clean.

---

He tried, after, to reach the others.

It surprised him how hard it had become. There had been a time — it felt like a different century, though it was less than a year — when reaching his friends meant walking into the Amber Leaf and finding them already at the table, or leaving word with the keeper and knowing they'd all drift in by dusk. Now Kael's freedom of movement was bought with Iron business and spent on Iron's schedule, not Xion's; you did not summon a man whose cover was that he had somewhere else to be. And Silvanno was harder still — every hour of Silvanno's day was watched by people counting which way Water might lean, and a second visit in a week would stop being an accident and start being a pattern, and patterns got reported. The casual gravity that had once pulled the four of them to the same table was simply gone. Reaching anyone now took cover and timing and a reason that would survive being overheard. He stood at the mouth of a street he might once have crossed without thinking, and understood that the crossing now cost more than it was worth, and did not make it.

The outside, he was learning, was a lonely place. It was quiet in a way the inside never was, and the quiet was not restful.

He found a step in the shade at the edge of a little market square, lower than the wall, humbler, a place with no history in it, and he sat down on it because his legs had run out of anywhere to take him.

Farleen found him there.

He didn't hear her come. He never did. One moment the step beside him was empty and the next she was folding herself down onto it, close but not touching, and holding out — without a word, without making anything of it — the twist of date-dark pastry from the little stall by the old fountain, still faintly warm, as though she'd timed it.

"You didn't eat," she said. It was, again, not a question.

He took it. This time there was nothing she needed to tell him and nothing he needed to ask, and so they didn't. She didn't want intelligence and she wasn't carrying any. She had simply worked out where his feet would run down to, the way she worked out everything, and come and sat in it with him. Nobody had followed her. Nobody ever did. The great watchful machine of the city that tracked every step Kael and Silvanno took did not spend a single eye on Farleen Darfi, and so the one person in Kaha'an who could simply **appear** beside him, needing no cover and leaving no pattern, was the one everyone had agreed to forget.

He ate the pastry. It was very good. The city moved around them — the market winding down, a man stacking crates, two children chasing each other between the stalls, one of them shrieking with the particular joy of a child who has had enough water and does not know it is a kind of wealth. Farleen sat beside him with her forearms on her knees and watched the square with the habitual half-attention that never fully switched off, and said nothing, and did not need him to be all right.

And somewhere in the middle of the quiet, without deciding to, he smiled.

It was small, and it was tired, and it surprised him more than it would have surprised anyone watching. He could not remember the last one. It cost nothing and fixed nothing — the girl was still in her doorway, the wells still had their lines, Elara was still behind a wall of protocol he could not carry a single true word through. He thought of her up there, in the golden middle of it, unable to do the one small human thing that was happening right now on a stone step at the edge of a market: to sit beside someone and be quiet and let the weight come off for the length of a pastry. The crown would not allow her that. Not because she wouldn't want it. Because the throne he had put her on did not have a step like this one anywhere inside its walls.

Farleen could sit here.

She was sitting here.

He finished the pastry, and the smile stayed a moment longer than the reason for it, and neither of them said anything at all.
