There was nothing for him to do, and the city was the picture of a place with nothing wrong.

That was the strangeness of the days after the reform. Xion walked wards that worked, past lines that moved, and the part of him that had spent fifteen weeks straining against gates and letters and the slow closing of every door found itself, suddenly, with nothing to push on. The crisis he could see had gone underground, back into the tokens and the ledger and the quiet arithmetic that would kill somebody eventually, somewhere he wasn't standing. The crisis everyone else could see was fixed. A man used to being needed does not know, at first, what to do with the feeling of being unnecessary. It sat on him like weather.

He was standing at the edge of the flower market, of all places — doing nothing, buying nothing, watching a woman argue a vendor down over the price of cut stems as though it were the most important thing in the world, because to her, this morning, it was — when he saw Kael coming, and knew from thirty paces that the weather had changed.

Kael did not hurry. Kael never hurried; hurry was a thing that happened to other men. But there was a set to him, a directness of line through the crowd, that Xion had learned to read the way he read a pulse. Kael crossed the last of the distance and did not soften it, did not lead in, did not do any of the things a man does to cushion a blow, because Kael had decided somewhere on the walk over that there was no cushioning it and cushioning it would only waste time.

"Someone tried to kill the Empress."

The flower market went on around the sentence as if it had not been said. The woman got her stems.

---

It came in pieces, because pieces were all Kael had.

A formal dinner at the palace — one of the diplomatic functions the Coterie mounted to show the continent that Kaha'an had a government again, that the men who ran it could set a fine table and seat an ambassador and pour a wine worth crossing borders for. Poison. In the food, or the wine, or something she alone had touched; the accounts didn't agree. But not a clumsy poison, Kael was careful to say — not a warning, not a gesture, not the kind of thing meant to frighten. The kind meant to work. Elara had collapsed at the table in front of the whole seated hall. She was alive. She was badly hurt. Past that, the gates had come down within the hour and taken the rest of the truth behind them, and everything Kael had he'd bought from Iron men on the palace's edges in the narrow window before the lockdown closed.

Xion heard all of it, and then he heard himself asking questions he hadn't decided to ask.

"What kind." The healer had come up through the terror without being summoned, the way it always did, reaching past the fear for the one framework that turned a horror into a problem with edges. "How fast did she go down — at the table, you said, so it was fast, minutes, not hours. Was she seizing? Was she breathing wrong, or was it the heart? Did she keep anything down, did anyone — Kael, did anyone get her to bring it back **up** — "

"I don't know," Kael said. "I don't know any of it. Nobody out here does."

And that was the wall. Xion knew, in the abstract, the difference between a poison that stops the breath and a poison that stops the heart and a poison that takes the body apart slowly over days, knew the different hands you'd play against each, knew that in the first hour the right knowledge was worth more than any physician's whole career after — and every scrap of that knowledge was useless, because it lived in him, out here, and she was in there, being treated by men whose work he could not see, could not question, could not correct. He had spent fifteen weeks locked out of a building. He had never once, until this moment, felt the wall as a thing pressed directly against the body of someone he could not lose.

Silvanno found them before the hour was out. He came the way news traveled to him — through the ambient nerves of the Water world, his mother's world, which had its own reasons to be shaking today — and he wore it worse than Kael did. Kael went still under pressure; Silvanno went bright and quick and thin, his hands not knowing where to be. He didn't have more than Kael had. He had less, and what he had frightened him.

The three of them stood together in a functioning city, and there was not one thing among them that any of them could do.

---

The waiting had no shape. The pieces came out of order, contradicting each other, correcting each other, each one bought or overheard or guessed at some remove from a palace that had sealed itself like a fist.

The dinner had been under Coterie auspices. The catering — the kitchens, the servers, the wine — had been the Water cartel's, because the Water cartel handled the catering for palace functions the way it handled everything touching water in this city, which was to say entirely and as a matter of course. Silvanno said this plainly, the words costing him: it was **routine**. It meant nothing. Water had poured the wine at every state dinner for twenty years and would have poured it whoever sat the throne. But routine was not how it would be heard, and he knew that too. Tania Larannas's people had prepared the meal that nearly killed the Empress, and by nightfall the Arol Batae had that fact and were turning it over, and the whole city that could talk was already saying the shape of a name.

The piece that came last was the one that hollowed him out.

Elara was alive. Elara was unconscious. Not dead — he made himself hold that, gripped it — but not waking either, suspended somewhere between, behind walls, tended by hands he would never get to guide. For a healer there was a particular cruelty in it that he could not have explained to anyone who wasn't one. Bad news was an answer. You could work with an answer, grieve it, act on it. This was not an answer. This was a held breath with no bottom, and he had to keep drawing it, hour after hour, not knowing.

It was into that — into the worst of the not-knowing — that Farleen came.

She didn't bring anything. That was the thing he noticed, distantly, the way you notice a small true fact in the middle of a large terrible one: no pastry, no small kindness, none of the little offerings she'd carried to him at every gate for weeks. She had understood, somewhere on her way to him, that there was nothing small that would reach this, and so she brought the only thing that would, which was herself. She sat down near him and she stayed. She didn't tell him it would be all right. She didn't ask what he needed. She was simply there, in the place where he was, for as long as he was in it, and after a while he found that the fact of her being there had become one of the only things holding the hour together.

---

It was Kael who started the other thing, because Kael's mind could not not-start it.

"Who," he said. Not cruelly. He laid it down the way he laid everything down, like a man reading a gauge. "Who had the reach."

And Xion's mind, which had spent the whole day refusing to think of anything but Elara — whether she breathed, whether she would wake — went where it was built to go, and he hated it for going, and it went anyway.

Not Tania. That was the first thing, and it came fast, because it was the thing everyone else was about to get wrong. Tania's people had served the dinner — but access wasn't the question, access was the trap. Tania Larannas was a woman who read the wind and leaned into it, who had spent these months quietly deciding Rosik was the wind; she did not overturn tables, she **survived** them, and murdering the Empress at her own catered dinner was the single most self-destructive act available to a woman whose entire craft was never being caught on the losing side. The Arol Batae would look at her because her servants had held the wine. Anyone who actually knew her would look **past** her — to the question of who could reach **inside** her operation and turn her own servants into a weapon she never authorized and would never have chosen. Who had that kind of reach into another cartel's household. Who had the money to buy a professional. Who had the intelligence to place him. Who had spent twenty years building exactly the sort of apparatus that could do a thing like this and leave someone else holding the cup.

Who had the **motive** — who, in this whole city, had a reason to want Elara not merely sidelined but **gone**, her legitimacy that constrained everything the Coterie could do simply erased —

He did not do it carefully. Some small watchful part of him knew he wasn't being careful and could not make the rest of him slow down. He did not weigh them one by one, did not give each of the others their fair hearing, did not do the slow honest arithmetic the moment deserved. He was too frightened for arithmetic. His mind skipped the whole careful middle of the thing and landed, all at once, hard, on the one shape that fit — the man who had said *Fine* and moved to the next item, the man building a government in the shell of a throne, the man who for weeks had been making Elara irrelevant — and for whom, once irrelevant stopped being enough, her death was only the next line to cross off.

"Rosik," Kael said, when Xion didn't. Reading the same signs. Not a question.

Silvanno said nothing at all, and his nothing agreed. His mother's world was under the cloud; the only thing that lifted the cloud from Water was the truth that someone had used Water from the outside — and there was exactly one man in Kaha'an with the reach to do it.

The conclusion settled over the three of them not like a discovery but like a confirmation, the last stone dropping into a wall they'd watched go up since the masquerade. It settled into Xion coldest and heaviest of all, because it was his father — and it came with nothing a man could do about it, no answer to give it and no hand he could raise against it. Only the fact, sitting cold and immovable in his chest — his father had reached into the palace to take Elara out of the world — and the knowing of it left him exactly as powerless as not knowing had.

---

Melina came to the wall.

Not at the hour. There was no hour, today; the rhythm they'd built across the weeks had broken with everything else. But she came to the low wall in the quiet square all the same, because it was where he'd be and she knew it was where he'd be, and she came from the estate direction as she always did, walking the same route, and Xion watched her cross the last of the square toward him and saw a thing on her he had never seen there before.

He had seen Melina tired. He had seen her resigned, and torn, and worn thin between two men who each thought they were saving the city. He had never, in all of it, seen her afraid.

"It wasn't him."

She said it before she reached him. Not defensive — she didn't have the flatness of someone defending. She had the wideness of someone horrified, someone who had spent the walk over watching a thing take shape and had come running to get in front of it.

"I can see your face," she said. "I've been able to see your face my whole life. You've decided it was Father. You and Kael and half the city, you've drawn the line straight to him and you've stopped drawing." She stood over him, and her hands were not steady. "Xion. I know what that man is. I know it better than you, better than anyone left alive knows it, because I'm the one who's spent every day of these months standing between what he wants and what he'll say out loud to get it. He builds. He maneuvers. He makes people **irrelevant** — he made Elara irrelevant, he was winning, he had the wells and the story and the whole city thanking him — and a man does not throw all of that on a fire to become a **poisoner**." Her voice caught on the word. "It isn't beneath his morals. He hasn't got morals to be beneath. It's beneath **him**. He'd sooner die than be the kind of man who does murder in the dark, because that man isn't the man who wins, that man is a thug who got frightened — and Father would rather be anything on this earth than frightened."

Xion heard her. He did not brush it aside; this was Melina, whose reading of a room he trusted over his own more often than he'd ever admitted to her, and whose honesty had never once bent to make him comfortable.

"I know you know him," he said, carefully. "I do. But knowing what a man **is** isn't the same as knowing what he **did**. He had the reach. He had the reason. He had every piece of it, Melina, and the only thing standing against all of it is that it doesn't sound like him — and people do things that don't sound like them when the thing they were doing stops being enough."

"You're frightened," she said. "That's not reasoning. That's a frightened man who's found somewhere to put it."

It landed, because it was true, and they both knew it was true, and it changed nothing — because the reach and the reason and the pattern didn't stop being real just because his hands were shaking when he saw them. That was the wedge, though neither of them named it and neither would have called it that: not a fight, not a raised voice, only two people who had read the same city off the same wall for ten weeks discovering, quietly, that they were no longer reading the same page. She could tell him it wasn't Rosik. She could not tell him who it was. And a *no* with no other name behind it is not enough to turn a man who has already found somewhere to stand.

She saw that too. She was too sharp not to.

"Don't do anything," she said at last, and it wasn't a plea, it was a demand, delivered in the exact register their father used for demands, and he wondered if she heard it. "Not on this. Not yet. Give me — " she pressed her mouth flat, thinking, already moving, already working the problem the way she worked every problem "— give me the chance to bring you the truth of it. I'm closer to him than any of you will ever get. If he did this, there'll be a seam and I'll find it, and if he didn't, there'll be something else, and I'll find **that**. Let me look before you decide. That's all. Let me look."

"All right," he said.

He even meant it, in the moment. He watched her go back the way she'd come, toward the estate, toward the house that displayed everything and held nothing, already turning the thing over as she walked — and he thought that she was very likely the most honest person he knew, and that when she looked, honestly, at what her father had become, she would find what he had already found. He did not want that for her. He wanted, fiercely and uselessly, to be wrong, so that she would not have to be the one to prove him right. What he felt instead was the wall between what he believed and what she believed, thin as a hairline crack in fired clay, and the knowledge that walls like that widen.

---

Then he was alone, and the city went on being fine.

The lines moved. The wells flowed. Somewhere up the hill, behind walls that had never once opened for him, Elara lay in the dark with her breath being counted by strangers, poisoned — he was as sure of it as he had ever been sure of anything — by the man who had lifted him onto a horse when he was small.

And there was nothing he could do with the knowing.

He could not reach her; the gate had been shut to him for weeks, and it was sealed now behind a lockdown. He could not put his hands on the one hurt in all the city he would have given anything to mend — the single thing he was actually good for, and the walls made even that impossible. He could not carry a name to the Arol Batae, because a name was the whole of what he had, and no one is taken up on the certainty of a man standing outside the wall. He could not confront his father, because he had nothing to confront him with, and Rosik had never once bled from words. He could not even learn whether she would wake.

He had come into this day the least necessary man in Kaha'an, idle in a flower market because the city, for once, had no wound that needed him. He would leave it needed — desperately, by the one person in the world he could not reach — and no less useless for it. He had never once in his life believed a thing could not be done. Now he sat on a low wall in the failing light with the worst news he had ever been handed and his hands hanging empty — no wound to close, no name to spend, no gate to talk his way through, no plan, no lever, no move anywhere in the world — and understood that this, at last, was the thing there was no doing anything about.
