His father did not send anyone to check on him. That was the part Xion kept snagging on, in the days that followed — that Rosik had set a seven-day clock ticking in the middle of his life and then, having set it, simply turned back to his ledgers, as certain the instruction would be carried out as he was that the sun would trouble itself to rise. The clock ran only in Xion's own head. The urgency was entirely his to feel. Rosik had handed him an impossible thing and gone back to work, and somehow that was worse than a threat would have been, because a threat at least implied you mattered enough to be watched.

Melina met him at the wall the next morning, as she'd promised, and told him what she'd dug out of the Coterie's paper overnight: that their father could not cleanly do the worst of what he'd hinted at, not yet, not without spending capital he'd sooner hoard. "It's a clock, not a blade at your throat," she said. "You've a little more room than he made it sound like. Not much. But some." It helped. It did not help enough. A little more room was still a room he was locked outside of, and the one door he needed to reach was the one no amount of room could open.

So he did the only thing there was to do. He put the whole of what was left to him on a single problem, and for the first time since the bells rang over Elara's coronation, all four of them bent to the same task at once.

Get a message to her. A real one. Not the courteous nothing the palace courier turned his letters into by the time they crossed the wall — something *true*, something that told her what the wards below were living through and what her council was doing in her name and that the man who'd found her was still out here, still trying, still hers. If he could get one honest sentence into her hands, he told himself, the rest would follow. He had to believe the rest would follow. It was that or believe the week was already lost, and he could not yet afford to believe that.

---

Kael went at it the way Kael went at everything: as a machine to be understood and then defeated.

His Iron business bought him the administrative wing — the supply offices, the record rooms, the clerks and stewards who kept the government's paper moving — and he walked those corridors with a ledger under his arm and a reason to be there, and he *mapped*. Who reported to whom. Which office connected to which. Where the seams were. He came back to Xion's rooms after two days of it and laid it out flat and unadorned, the way he laid out everything.

"It's not a wall," he said. "A wall you can go over. It's layers, and the layers don't touch. Iron gets me the administrative ring — the people who move grain orders and guard rosters and laundry accounts. None of them can reach the imperial quarters. The ones who *can* reach the imperial quarters don't take deliveries and don't sign for supplies, so I've got no reason to be in a room with them, and no lever if I were." He turned one hand over, empty. "I kept looking for the person who'd been bribed to keep you out. There isn't one. Nobody's *keeping* you out. The building just doesn't have a hallway that runs from where you are to where she is — the old imperial plan, boxes inside boxes — and what soft edges it did have, the Arol Batae sealed the week she took the throne. There's no one in the doorway to outsmart. There's only a wall nobody ever decided to build against you."

It's not personal, was what he meant. It's architecture. And there was no arguing with a hallway that simply wasn't there.

---

Silvanno had the opposite problem, which was somehow the same problem.

He knew the people. That was the maddening thing — he could name the servants who dressed the Empress's floor, the stewards who carried the private correspondence, the exact human beings whose hands a note would have to pass through. His mother had spent twenty years building the map of who-owed-what across every great house in Kaha'an, and Silvanno had grown up reading it over her shoulder. He knew precisely where the doors were.

He just couldn't be seen opening one.

"I sent a man to sound out one of the imperial-wing maids," he told Xion, low, on a bench in a garden square where it would look like two acquaintances killing an afternoon. "Careful. Nothing in writing, nothing traceable. Two days later my mother asks me — sweetly, over supper, the way she asks the things that aren't questions — whether I've been *corresponding* with anyone at the palace lately." His jaw tightened. "She knows. Not what, but that. And the moment I actually *use* one of these people, it stops being a rumor and becomes a fact, and the fact is *Water is reaching toward the crown*, and my mother has spent this entire crisis making very sure nobody can say that about her. I have every door you need. I can't walk through a single one without setting fire to the one thing keeping my house neutral."

Capability without freedom. He had the whole map and couldn't touch it, and the not-touching was eating him from the inside — Xion could see it, the specific misery of a man who could solve the problem and was forbidden to.

---

Farleen didn't try the doors at all.

She worked the way water worked — around, underneath, through the cracks nobody thought to seal. Not the administrative ring Kael mapped or the social channels Silvanno couldn't use, but the other palace, the one the Noble District never thought about: the delivery yards and the laundry carts and the kitchen staff who came and went a hundred times a day beneath everyone's notice. She spent three days learning its rhythms the way she'd once learned everything, and she got closer than any of them.

She got a note into the hand of a woman who carried bread to the imperial wing.

Xion had written it himself — plain, direct, no cartels and no politics, just the true thing said as simply as he could say it, small enough to tuck into a palm. Farleen passed it on the fourth day and came back and told him it was done, and then the two of them waited, and nothing came back. Not a refusal. Not a warning. Not the woman returning it uncarried. The note went into the palace the way a stone goes into a well, and the well gave back exactly what wells give back, which is the sound of the stone and then nothing at all.

"I can't tell you if she got it," Farleen said. She said it flatly, the trained voice, the one that filed a failure as a data point because feeling it would cost more than she could spend right now. "It went in. Whether it reached her hands, whether the Arol Batae pulled it, whether some steward read it and burned it — I can't see past the wall. There's no seam, Xion. I keep looking for the seam. It isn't there. The place wasn't built to keep out spies. It was built to keep out *everyone*, and everyone includes me."

---

It was somewhere in the middle of that week, watching his three most capable friends break themselves against the same blank wall from three different directions, that the thing finally turned over in Xion's mind — and once it turned, he couldn't turn it back.

The wall that was keeping him out was keeping her in.

It was the same wall. The same machine. The very apparatus that filtered his letters into courteous nothing was filtering *her* world down to whatever the council wanted her to see — the same sanitized briefings, the same curated calm, the same official version of a city that was quietly failing outside her windows. He'd been picturing Elara up there on her throne, insulated, cushioned, deciding things. He should have been picturing her the way he'd have pictured anyone behind a wall this total: alone in the middle of it, handed a version of the world with all the truth strained out, reaching for the people she trusted and finding only the smooth blank surface of a machine that answered every reach with silence.

She was as walled off as he was. Possibly more. And if his true words weren't reaching her — if all that crossed the wall was the hollow courtesy the courier let through —

Then for all she knew, he had stopped writing anything that mattered. For all she knew, he had grown tired, or grown distant, or grown into the kind of man who found the throne he'd built more convenient at arm's length. He had no way to tell her otherwise. He had no way to tell her anything. The silence between them was being manufactured, sentence by sentence, by a machine that meant neither of them any harm — and he could not so much as prove to her that it was a machine.

He wrote her one more letter that night, and he was clever about it — innocuous on its face, nothing to trip a filter, but seeded with things only she would catch: the fountains, the color a certain woman's eyes went when she was afraid, a thing he'd told her once in the dark under the city about who really paid for the water. If it reached her, she would know it was him, wholly him, the true him, in every line.

Nothing came back.

Not a sanitized reply this time. Not even the courteous nothing. No response at all — which meant either it never reached her, or it reached her and the code went over her head, or it reached her and she understood every word and chose not to answer. He turned the three possibilities over until late, and each one was worse than the one before, and he could not for the life of him decide which he most feared was true.

---

They gathered late in the week, and the room had gone quiet in a way it hadn't been at the start.

The momentum was gone — that early, foolish, precious energy of people who had beaten worse odds than this and half expected to beat these too. Kael laid out the final shape of it without a scrap of editorial: the palace security wasn't one lock you could pick but a distributed thing, layered and redundant, and to get a message through you'd need to compromise several rings at once and still have someone on the inside willing to break faith. "It's good work," he said, and there was something almost like respect in it, the flat respect of a man who builds systems acknowledging one built better than he could have. "Whoever designed it built it to survive exactly what we're doing to it."

Silvanno's patience finally went. "Then I walk up to the gate and I *demand* an audience. Loudly. In front of enough people that turning me away becomes its own scandal—"

"And the Arol Batae put you on the ground with perfect courtesy and Water declares for the crown by sundown whether your mother likes it or not," Xion said. "And you've made enemies of the one force in this city that would actually die for Elara, over a note." He shook his head. "They're not the wall because they're against her, Silvanno. They're the wall because they're *for* her. That's what makes it impossible. There's no villain in the doorway to outsmart. Just people doing their jobs well."

Farleen said almost nothing. She'd gotten closest and it hadn't been enough, and she carried that the way she carried everything — folded away where it wouldn't show, her report clipped down to the operational bones. But Xion had learned to read what she didn't say, and what she didn't say was that she had been *built* for this exact work, trained from a girl to slip through the seams of powerful houses, and for the first time in her life she'd met a wall with no seam in it at all.

---

The seventh day came, and Xion had nothing.

No message delivered. No word he could set in his father's hand as proof that the task had ever been possible. Worse than that — he couldn't even tell whether Elara knew he'd been trying at all. Every honest thing he'd sent had gone into the machine and vanished, and on the other side of the wall a woman he loved was, for all he could prove, coming slowly to believe that the man who'd sworn to stand with her had gone quiet when it counted.

And the week, he understood now, had never really been a chance at all. It had been a *record* — a documented courtesy his father could point to later, proof that the family channels were tried and the family channels failed, before he did whatever he'd already decided to do. The chalice had looked like an opportunity when it was handed to him. It had been something else the whole time, and he had drunk it down to the bottom, and the bottom was this: seven days spent, nothing to show, and no way of knowing what his father would make of that when Xion finally stood in front of him with empty hands.

---

On the seventh evening he had failed his father's task, and could not reach Elara, and had nowhere left to put himself — and so he did the only thing left in the world that had ever been purely his own. He went back to the clinic.

There was no ceremony to it. He unlocked the door of the two rooms he'd never quite given up, took the small painted board out of the drawer where it had waited, and hung *Master Fen* back on its hook beside the door. The name went on like an old coat — worn soft, still his, a thing he had built rather than been handed. He needed one thing in his life to be the way it had always been. He needed, after a week of doors that wouldn't open, one door that opened when he pushed it. He needed to put his hands on a wound he could actually close.

It took less than a morning to learn he'd lost even that.

The third patient of the day was a man he'd stitched twice before, a porter with bad knees who'd never once needed the healer's real name because the arrangement had never required one. He came in, lowered himself onto the bench, glanced up — and Xion watched the double take happen, watched the porter's face run the exact arithmetic the tally-man's had run at the wellhead, the plain clothes and the bearing and then, landing last and hardest, the recognition.

"Wait." The porter half-rose. "You're — you're *him*. The lord. The one that found her." Not hostile. Almost awed, which was worse. "Master Fen's been *you* this whole—"

The name had gone through every ward of Kaha'an by now. *The Grain lord's son. The one who found the Empress.* Master Fen had never been as invisible as the alias needed him to be, and now the alias was a false name pinned to a face half the city could place on sight. The porter would tell it as a wonder — *you'll never guess who stitched my knee* — and it would be a good story, and a good story was precisely the poison. Master Fen had worked because he was no one. Because no one who came to him owed anyone an account of it. Because a man could bleed and be poor and be helped in a room where none of it went into anyone's ledger.

By afternoon there was a different kind of visitor. A merchant in good cloth, who had not come to be healed, stood in the doorway turning his hat in his hands and offered — warmly, as though it were a gift and not a coffin — to *sponsor* the clinic. A proper storefront. His family's name over the door. In exchange for nothing at all, he said, only the small pleasure of being known to support the healer who served the man who served the Empress. The unplaceable Master Fen, the stranger no one could connect to anything, had never in his life been offered such a thing, because there had never been anything in him worth a merchant's name being seen beside.

Xion stood in the middle of his own waiting room and understood it the way you finally understand a diagnosis you've spent weeks not looking at: the thing that had made this place work was gone, and it was not coming back, because it had never been the rooms or the skill. It had been *invisibility* — and invisibility was the one possession his own name had spent these last two months burning off him, until there was nothing left of Master Fen but a man everybody recognized.

He took the sign down two days after he'd hung it.

It wasn't a renunciation; he hadn't the strength in him for a gesture that size, and it would have been a lie besides — he was still a healer, his hands still knew what they knew, and the porter's knees still needed looking at. He simply lifted the little board off its hook and put it back in the drawer, and turned the merchant away with more courtesy than the offer deserved, and kept the rooms, because the rooms were the one place in the city that were his and no one else's.

There was, in truth, another roof. That was the sour heart of it. The Kemvimore estate would take him in tonight if he walked up its long swept path and asked — Rosik had barred no door to him, struck his name from no ledger; in the eyes of the whole city he was still the Grain lord's son, still the heir, and that was the entire reason a well filled and a merchant groveled at the sound of the name. The one door in Kaha'an certain to open at his touch was his father's. He simply could not make himself walk through it. He'd had a title once, and lost it at a gate. He'd had an Empress to serve, and couldn't reach her through a wall. He had a father still, and a place at that father's table still, and would sooner lie down in the dark of a dead clinic than take it. And sitting alone in the two rooms that had been the truest thing he ever built, he understood that he'd had one last thing that was only his — the ability to be no one, and do good quietly, and answer to nothing — and the week had taken that too, without any villain in the doorway at all.

He put his head in his hands, in the place where Master Fen used to live, and for a while he did not think about how to reach Elara, or what he'd say to his father, or any of it. For a while there was just a healer with no one left to heal, in the dark, at the bottom of the chalice.
