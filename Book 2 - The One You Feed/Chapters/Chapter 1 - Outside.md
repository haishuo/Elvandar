# Chapter 1: Outside

The gate did not open, and the man in front of it did not tell Xion no.

That was the part he had not gotten used to. There was no *no*. A *no* would have been something to push against, a wall he could look for the weak stone in. Instead there was the Arol Batae guardsman in his deep blue, standing with his spear grounded and his face composed into the particular blankness of a man doing his job well, and there was the answer that was not an answer.

"Your message will reach the Empress, Lord Kemvimore." The guardsman inclined his head, a courtesy so precise it managed to be its own kind of dismissal. "You're welcome to wait."

Xion had waited yesterday. He had waited the day before that. He knew, now, what the waiting bought.

He handed over the folded paper anyway. He had tried different words each time — a request for an audience, a note on the water rationing in the Middle District that Elara would want to see, once just her name and a single line beneath it that he had thrown away before he reached the gate and rewritten from memory twice more. Today it was a report. Facts she could act on. He had learned, over these weeks, that facts traveled better than feeling through the machinery behind these walls. He was no longer sure either one traveled at all.

The guardsman took it the way he took all of them: carefully, respectfully, as though it mattered. Perhaps it did reach her. Perhaps somewhere past the courtyard and the honor guard and the long cool corridors his words were being read by someone whose job was to decide what the Empress needed to see, and his did not make the list. He had no way to know. That was the whole architecture of the thing. It did not keep him out with force. It kept him out with procedure, and procedure could not be argued with, because there was no one inside it who had decided anything.

Two weeks ago he had been on the other side of this gate.

He let himself remember it, standing there in the heat with nothing else to do. The first days after the coronation, when the palace had still been half-asleep and no one had worked out yet who belonged where. He had walked those corridors at Elara's shoulder. When the cartel leaders came to pay their careful respects, it had been Xion who leaned in and told her, quietly, which smiles to trust and which to count — *Tania will offer you everything and mean the offer, not the everything; Sa Ko Ren will say less than she thinks, so weigh the silences.* He had known the shape of the room. He had been useful. For a handful of days he had let himself believe that this was what came next: that he had found her, and put her here, and would help her hold it.

Then the security protocols had begun to formalize. Not against him. Around him. Rosters were drawn up of who had standing to be in which wing, and standing meant office, position, a title the palace could write in a ledger. *Lord Kemvimore* was a courtesy. It described a house he had been raised in, not a role he could name. His only claim to the corridors on the other side of this gate was that the Empress wished him there — and personal wish, he was learning, was the one currency the institution did not accept. It was too new to itself to accept anything else.

He stepped back from the gate. Not away. Just back, enough to acknowledge it — the pale stone, the blue guard, the door that was not locked and would not open. A closed door was at least honest. This was worse. This was a door held shut by everyone's good manners, and there was no arguing with manners.

He turned and walked.

---

He did not go far. There was a place he had fallen into the habit of, these last weeks, without quite deciding to — a low wall along the edge of a quiet square where the Noble District softened toward the rest of the city, close enough to the palace that he could pretend he was still nearby, far enough that he did not have to keep watching the gate not open. A dry fountain stood at the square's center, its basin swept clean by someone who still cared for it. Little traffic came through. He could sit on the wall in the shade and be no one for an hour, which was a thing he had once been very good at and was forgetting how to do.

He got there first. He always got there first.

He heard her before he saw her — the particular unhurried step of someone who had learned to move through crowded rooms without disturbing them — and when he turned she was already crossing the square toward him from the direction of the estate. She sat down on the wall beside him without asking. Only family sat down like that.

"The better part of a month," she said, by way of greeting. "You were gone the better part of a month, and do you know how I found out my little brother had turned up the lost heir to the empire and put her on the Ruby Throne? A crier. In the street. Same breath as the grain prices and a notice about somebody's missing dog." She shook her head, slow and wondering. "I had to stand there and be *surprised*, Xion. In public. As though I weren't related to the man who did it."

"Come on, Melina, you know that wasn't the sort of thing I could put in a letter."

"You put far less in letters, for years." But there was no real heat under it. There never was, with her. The grievance was only the shape she poured the worry into, and what she actually meant sat under it, unsaid and unmistakable: *you vanished, and I didn't know whether you were alive.* She had been telling him that one way or another since they were children — since he'd first started slipping out of the estate to places she couldn't follow, into a version of his life she had no door into. He had gotten unforgivably good at not hearing it. "You could have come to me. After. Even after would have counted."

"I know."

"You didn't."

"I know that too."

She let it sit, then let it go, which was one more thing she was better at than he had ever deserved. It was the whole map of them in four exchanges: he left their father's world and she stayed in it, and the staying had made her the one who reached across the distance, every time, because he never learned how.

Now that the teasing had spent itself he could see how tired she was — not the performed kind that wants you to notice, but the real kind, worked deep into the shoulders from a day spent standing between people. He knew the posture. He'd grown up watching her hold it.

"How is she?" he asked.

Melina didn't pretend not to know who he meant. "Cornered," she said. "Politely. You'd recognize it."

He would. He had been sitting in the same corner all morning.

"The Coterie's simply... continued," she went on, and her voice flattened into the clean, diagnostic register she always reached for when the feeling underneath got to be too much — the tone she used to make a problem sound like a thing outside herself. "That's the thing nobody quite says out loud. They ran this city for twenty years in the Valanar name, with no Valanar to answer to. Now there's an Empress on the throne and they've gone right on running it, because the machinery is theirs. The grain moves through Grain. The water moves through Water. The courts, the tariffs, the labor rolls — all of it runs on cartel infrastructure, because there isn't any other kind. Father has made himself Prime Minister without anyone ever using the word. He doesn't need the word. He has the flow."

"And Elara?"

"Elara proposes." Melina's mouth thinned. "She proposes beautiful things. Things that would take years to build safely — new distribution, whole institutions, the tokens gone entirely. The Coterie proposes things that would help people by next month, inside the system that already exists. She rejects those, because they keep the system. They reject hers, because hers would break the system before there's anything to replace it. She won't work within it. They won't work outside it." She spread her hands, empty. "So nothing moves. She proposes things that would take years. We propose things that would actually help people now. She rejects everything. Nothing gets done. And in between the two of them, people are still lining up at the wells with tokens they inherited, or didn't."

Xion looked at the swept, dry fountain in the middle of the square and said nothing for a moment.

He had done this. That was the thought that came to him, unwanted and exact. Not the paralysis — but the terms of it. When Elara had been nobody, a woman with impossible eyes learning her own city street by street, it had been Xion who showed her the truth of it. The water rights that priced the poor out of drinking. The debts conjured from nothing. The hunger the Grain cartel manufactured and called order. He had shown her the wounds because they were real and because she needed to see them to be worth anything on that throne, and he had been right to.

And now she sat above a council of the people who ran those wounds, and she could not touch them, because he had taught her to see them clearly. She looked at the Coterie and saw the disease, correctly, and could not bring herself to use the only hands that knew how to move the city — because the hands were the disease. He had given her the truth. The truth had become the walls of her cell. He had put her on the throne and handed her, in the same motion, the reason she couldn't rule from it.

"You've gone somewhere," Melina said, watching him.

"I'm here."

"You've got Father's face on. The one where you're doing arithmetic you don't like." She said it lightly, but she was reading him, the way she read everyone, and it landed close enough that he didn't answer.

She let it go. That was a kindness of hers he had never properly thanked her for — she always knew exactly where the sore place was, and she almost never pressed it. She only made sure you knew she'd found it, so you wouldn't feel alone with it.

Then, more quietly, in a different register: "There's something else. It's probably nothing."

"You don't say *probably nothing* about nothing."

A small, tired huff that was almost a laugh. "Father's been talking to Ronas again. Not the usual — not the seat at the table, the labor arrangements, the things everyone knows about. Something closer than that." She turned the ring on her finger, once, a habit he remembered from when they were children and she was worried and didn't want to show it. "I don't like it. Ronas himself, I understand. He's a monster but he's a *legible* monster; you know where his edges are. It's his people. Some of them —" She stopped, looking for the word, and didn't find a better one. "Some of Ronas's people aren't like him. They're worse. And Father's the sort of man who thinks a tool is a tool."

Xion filed it the way he filed a symptom he couldn't place yet — noted, set aside, watch it. His mind was still at the gate, still on the closed door and the woman behind it who thought, maybe, that he had stopped writing. "He keeps them on a leash, doesn't he? Ronas. That's the whole point of Ronas."

"Yes." Melina didn't sound reassured by her own answer. "That's the whole point of Ronas."

She didn't push it. She'd said the thing; whether it landed was for him to decide, and she had learned a long time ago not to press a point past the moment it could do any good.

---

"You could go and see him," she said, after a while. She said it the way she'd have recommended a medicine that tasted of tar. "He'd see you. He wants to."

"He wants to *use* me. He wants me to walk back in there and un-say everything I said to her." Xion shook his head. "He thinks I have a key I'm just refusing to turn."

"I know what he thinks." Melina's voice was even. "I'm not telling you he's right. I'm telling you the door to him is the one door in this city that's still open to you, and you're a man standing outside a lot of closed ones." She saw his face and lifted a hand before he could answer. "I'm not going to make you. I've spent long enough trying to make the two of you into something you won't be. I only —" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "I'd rather not be the only one holding both ends of it. That's all."

He didn't have an answer for that — nothing that wasn't a promise he might not keep — so he didn't reach for one. He only bumped his shoulder against hers, the way he had when they were small, and she let herself lean into it for a moment before she straightened and put the composure back on.

"I should go," she said, and stood, and smoothed her skirts, and was Melina Kemvimore of the House of Grain again, all grace and no visible seams. "There's a supper I have to be a face at."

"Whose?"

"Does it matter?" A dry flick of a smile. "You know you never come to these functions anyway. Find me here in a few days. I'll know more, and you'll have thought yourself into some plan I'll have to talk you out of."

"You assume I have plans."

"You always have plans. You just call them worries until they're too late to be anything else." She said it fondly, already turning, already going, back across the square toward the estate, toward the palace, toward all the rooms with her in them and him outside. He watched her until she reached the corner — the straight back, the unhurried step, a woman walking into the rest of her afternoon like it was a thing she meant to win.

Then she was gone, and he was alone in the square with the swept, dry fountain.

---

He went back to the gate. He knew it was foolish while he was doing it. The gate had not changed. The guardsman had not changed — perhaps a different man now, the shift turned over, but the same blue, the same grounded spear, the same faultless courtesy that closed around him like water closing over a stone.

"Your message will reach the Empress, Lord Kemvimore. You're welcome to wait."

He did not leave a message this time. He had nothing new to say that the paper would carry, and he had already learned what the waiting bought. He stood a moment longer than he meant to, looking through the bars at the courtyard beyond, the pale stone gone gold now as the afternoon tilted west, and somewhere past it the corridors he had walked at her shoulder two weeks and a lifetime ago.

Then he turned his back on it and walked into the city.

He went the long way, down out of the Noble District, and let the city change around him as he descended, because he could not stop himself from reading it. That was the curse of the healer's eye; it did not switch off because the man was heartsick. He passed the fountains of the high streets still running in their wasteful, beautiful abundance, water cascading over stone for no reason but that it always had. A few streets down the fountains stopped, and the wells began, and the wells had lines, and the lines had the particular stillness of people who had done this every day of their lives and expected to do it every day still to come. He passed a woman rationing a jar between three children by the careful mouthful. He passed a man with the grey lips and sunken eyes that meant his body was drinking itself. Master Fen could have named the man's ailment in a breath and could have done nothing about its cause, which was not in the body at all.

The healer in him saw the symptoms in every doorway. The reformer in him knew the disease, could have drawn it out on a page — the tokens, the flow, the twenty years of it hardened into the shape of the natural world. And the third man, the one who had walked into a gods-forsaken compound after a woman whose eyes changed color and come out the other side having put an Empress on the Ruby Throne, that man stood in the failing afternoon light in the middle of everything he had risked all of it to fix, outside her gate, outside her walls, outside the room where the deciding happened —

— and wondered, for the first time since the bells had rung, whether he had cured anything at all.

Tomorrow, he would try the gate again.

He already knew what the waiting bought. He went anyway. That, he was beginning to understand, was simply who he was.
