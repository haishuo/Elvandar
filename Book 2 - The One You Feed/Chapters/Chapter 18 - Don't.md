The cold had a list.

Not written down; it didn't need writing. The names sat in him in order, each paired with the reading that opened it, the way a physician carries his round in his head — which door, and what waits behind it. He had been assembling this longer than he'd known he was assembling it: through Melina's reports at the wall, through Farleen's walking and Silvanno's listening and Kael's errands into Iron, through every Coterie session and cartel table he had watched from the wrong side of. He knew these people. He knew, for each of them, the one thing that moved them. The cold did not intend to waste the knowing.

Silvanno and Kael moved in its orbit, the way they had all this long while. They could see the shape of it assembling now, name by name. Kael was useful because Iron opened doors to him. Silvanno had gone quiet — the particular quiet he fell into when a thing in front of him wouldn't fit any shape he had for it. Neither of them said the thing both of them were watching.

---

He went to the training hall first.

The same one. He knew it — a long, low building at the industrial edge of the city, hard-packed dirt underfoot, weapons racked down the walls, high windows letting in light that had never been put there to be admired. He had stood in this room months ago and watched Elara fight for her throne on this floor: outmatched from the first exchange and pressing in regardless, against a woman with forty years and every advantage on her, understanding somewhere in the middle of it that she could not win and going harder for the understanding. Sa Ko Ren had called the halt herself, when she had seen enough. Iron had pledged that day. And Xion had been the one who knew, before Elara set foot in the circle, exactly what was being tested — that it had never been about winning. He had never told Sa Ko so. He hadn't needed to. She had looked at him, once, that day — at him, not through him toward his father — and filed something away where she kept such things.

He was counting on the thing she'd filed.

She was where she was always going to be: on her own ground, in her own hall, none of the diplomatic lacquer she wore in the Coterie chambers anywhere on her. She looked up when he came in, and something in the look had changed since the last time it fell on him — she was not seeing Rosik's shadow now. He didn't cast that shape any longer, and the lack of it sat on him plain as a mark. She waited to see what the disinherited son wanted on her floor.

He didn't work up to it. He led with the true thing, because reading what was true was the whole of Sa Ko Ren's art and she would know it when she heard it. Elara's valor had been real. Sa Ko's seeing of it had been real. He had stood right there. He set all of it down between them as fact — and then set beside it the thing that could not share a room with the fact and leave the room standing.

"I thought Terinok warriors were honorable." Flat. Not a courtroom voice; a man laying a problem on the table and asking for help with it. "Elara proved her valor to you in single combat, on this floor. Yet you ally with the man who tried to have her assassinated."

Sa Ko Ren had no clean answer, and he watched her decline to manufacture one — watched her not take the easy exits, one by one, as they came to her. She could not swear Rosik hadn't done it; she knew no more than Xion truly did about what his father had ordered, and the poison had come off a table Tania's people laid. Nor could she defend standing at Rosik's side without defending the thing underneath it — the thing he had wagered, walking in here, that she had already been turning over alone in the dark. He knew why she'd gone to Rosik; anyone who had watched her could read it. Not greed — she had no use for greed. Frustration: people dying while a beautiful government met and met and decided nothing, and Rosik's faction, whatever else it was, at least governing. She had known what that choice cost her when she made it. He was counting on her never once having stopped knowing.

To have the cost named back to her — by Rosik's own son, in the plain grammar of Terinok honor, with Elara's valor for the edge of it — did not call for argument. It called for her to rationalize or to reckon. Sa Ko Ren did not rationalize.

She stepped back. Said it in as few words as she said anything: she was done lending Rosik her weight. No terms, no negotiation, nothing softened at the edges — the same directness she'd have carried into a strike, turned now on a thing she had decided was beneath her. It took only as long as the deciding. And a woman of her standing did not withdraw quietly; Terinok's validation went out of Rosik's coalition with her, and everyone who had been watching to see which way Iron leaned now had their answer.

---

Tania Larannas was another instrument, and he played her in another key.

She had spent her whole life reading the wind — everyone knew it of her — and she did not need telling which way it had turned now. Her people had laid the table the poison came off, and the weeks since had not been easy ones to be seen near Rosik in. Xion read her plain: no believer in anyone's cause, a survivor who had backed what looked like the winning side and would back the next the moment the figures were past arguing.

He didn't ask her to care about Elara. He didn't reach for honor, or loyalty, or the good of the city. He didn't sell her anything at all. With Tania you set the thing on the table and let her count it herself; the restraint was the respect, and it was the only kind she had any use for.

"I don't think you need me to tell you which way things are going with Rosik." He said it mildly, almost as an aside. "And he was going to steal from you, before the end — the quiet money, the accounts no one's meant to know you keep. Thought you should know."

He didn't say the rest of it. The rest of it didn't want saying, and saying it would only have been an insult: that Rosik was a drowning man now, and a drowning man grasps at whatever's nearest and drags it under with him, and a woman still holding his hand when he went would go down inside his grip. Tania could run that sum faster than he could speak it. In the beat of silence before she answered, she already had.

She asked one question — not *will you keep me safe* but *what do I get* — and his answer was enough. She had been waiting, he was fairly sure, for nothing more than someone with clearer sight than her own to confirm the thing she'd already half-settled: that there was a winning side, that it had a plan, and that the door onto it had not yet shut.

---

There were three more, across the next day, and there is no need to set each one down. Once you had watched him do it twice, you had the method, and the method did not change — only the key did, each one filed to a different lock. One of them cared, when it came to it, for nothing but what the histories would say of him, and Xion showed him the sentence he was about to be written into. One had been swallowing a grievance against Rosik for years; Xion did no more than hold it up to the light and ask, without heat, why he was still swallowing it. The last was the simplest and coldest of them: here are the figures, here is where Rosik ends, here is what it will cost to be found standing beside that ending. He threatened no one. He only showed each of them, one after another, that the threat was already in the room, had been for some time, and had their name on it.

By the end of the second day there was no coalition. Not broken — dispersed. Messages went unanswered. Chairs went unfilled. The small passive weight that holds a structure upright simply drew back, one shoulder at a time, each because it had decided on its own that Rosik was finished and it would not be caught going down beside him. The thing did not fall. It was abandoned from the inside, piece by piece, until the man at the center of it sat in a spotless house with his wine and found that no one came anymore.

---

Afterward — somewhere quiet, the two of them, because the cold did not celebrate and there was nothing in Xion to celebrate with — Silvanno went back over what was left, the way a man does when he means to be sure a finished thing is truly finished. One of the five had been his own mother, and the intelligence that turned her had come up through him in the first place; neither of them said so. The room held the flat, particular silence of something just ended.

"That was..." Silvanno said. He stopped. He chose. "Impressive."

The stop was the whole of it. Xion heard him reach for the word and heard him set down the one beneath it — heard him land on *impressive* because it was the nearest thing he could say that would not break something, and because the true word, said aloud, would become a thing that stood in the room with both their names on it. Xion knew the true word. He had been living inside it a week and a half; he knew its weather from within. He could not have it said to him yet — not by Silvanno, not by anyone he had ever let close enough to mean it.

"Don't," he said.

That was all of it. Not modesty; he had done the thing, and he knew to the grain what the thing was. Only: not yet. Not aloud. Not while there was still this much of it left to do.
