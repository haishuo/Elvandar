He had not set foot on the estate since the morning he left it to follow a woman with impossible eyes, and the house met him the way it met everyone — by being beautiful, and by making very sure he understood he was not to touch anything.

The gardens came first, and they were flawless. Every hedge clipped to a geometry a mason could have ruled with a straightedge. Every path swept so clean the desert might have agreed, as a personal favor, to keep its dust elsewhere. The fountains ran — of course they ran; water came cheap to the man who priced the city's bread, whatever it cost the wards below — arcing over pale stone in patterns chosen for the eye and no other reason. It was stunning. It had always been stunning. It looked like nowhere anyone had ever actually lived.

Inside was worse, because inside was where the absence had teeth. He walked the corridors he'd grown up in and found not a thing out of place: no scuffed floor, no worn armrest gone soft from a hundred sittings, no stray cup, no evidence anywhere that human beings did more in these rooms than maintain them. It was beautiful the way a gallery is beautiful. You admired it. You did not put your feet up. And a stranger walking through it for the first time would learn everything worth knowing about the man who kept it before that man ever said a word.

He had never known it any other way. That was the thing people who hadn't grown up here always got wrong — they assumed a boy raised in a house like this must have watched the warmth drain out of it, must carry some before-and-after in him. But the warmth had gone before Xion was old enough to keep a scrap of it. His mother was a name, a portrait behind a door on the east wing that was kept exactly as it had been and never opened, a set of stories he'd been told by a sister old enough to remember what he wasn't. The house had been a monument his whole life. He'd simply grown up inside the monument and mistaken it, for a while, for the world.

The one warmth he *did* remember in these walls hadn't been his mother's at all.

It had been a room off the kitchens, and an apothecary's hands, and a woman who called him by his name instead of his title and taught a sick, lonely, over-watched boy how to grind a poultice and listen to a cough — in secret, because the son of House Kemvimore had no business learning a servant's craft. Mira had made this house survivable. Mira had made it, for a few stolen hours a week, a home. And his father had ended that with two words in one of these very corridors, and the corridors had gone on being swept and flawless and beautiful, exactly as if nothing had bled on them at all.

He was still standing in that particular hallway, not quite looking at the door he wasn't looking at, when Melina found him.

---

"You came." She didn't sound surprised, only braced — she'd been waiting, and not idly. There was a tautness to her he recognized, the specific readiness of someone who has walked ahead into a room to check it for edges before letting you follow. "Good. I wanted to catch you before he does."

"How bad."

"Bad in the quiet way, not the loud one — that's what I need you to understand." She drew him a little aside, out of the sightline of a passing steward, her voice dropping into brisk and low. "He's not angry. Angry I could work with. He's *focused*, Xion, in the way that — you know the way. He's decided the situation is a problem, and he's decided you're the tool that solves it, and he's got that look he gets where the rest of the world is just friction between him and the answer." She pressed her lips together. "Here's the part you won't want to hear. Sa Ko Ren sat with the Empress twice this week. Privately. A *junior* seat at the Coterie, and she has the Empress's ear, and Father has letters that go unanswered. Do you understand how that lands with him? What it *is*, to a man like our father, to be gone around by someone he's counted beneath him since the day she walked in out of the Wild Sands with a treasure-hunter's fortune and no name to go with it?"

He understood. He'd understood it before she finished. It wasn't a policy grievance. Rosik Kemvimore had built an entire self on being, demonstrably, the most capable person in any room he entered — and something in that architecture cracked when a woman he'd catalogued as beneath him started deciding things he wasn't consulted on. That wasn't inconvenience. That was a wound at the foundation.

"And you?" he asked. "In the middle of all of it. How are you holding up, actually?"

"Oh, don't." But there was warmth in the refusal. "I'm holding up by not answering questions like that. I've got a tariff subcommittee at dusk and a father who thinks I can talk sense into two people who've each decided the other one's the disease, so if you don't mind I'll fall apart on my own schedule, thank you." She straightened his collar, a small automatic thing she'd probably done since they were children, and did not seem to notice she was doing. "Just — don't hand him the argument. Whatever he says. He wins arguments. It's the one thing he's never once lost. Don't give him one."

It was the truest thing anyone had said to him in weeks, and he didn't answer it. He just went in.

---

His father did not stand.

That was the first thing, and it told the rest. Rosik was at the long table in the room where he did the work that mattered — not the public parlor with its arranged flowers, the *operational* room, maps and ledgers and the clean cold order of a man who ran a quarter of a city from a chair. Tall, lean, silver-haired, dressed with the understated expense of someone who had nothing left to prove to anyone and knew it. He glanced up as Xion entered, and his eyes did the thing they always did — swept, weighed, filed — and then he simply went on with what he'd been doing for the length of a breath, because rising for his son was not a calculation that came out in favor of rising.

Xion had described this man to Elara once, across a masquerade ballroom, while nobles orbited him like moons. *He doesn't look like a monster*, she'd said. *Monsters rarely do*, Xion had answered. But that had been Rosik performing for a room. This was Rosik in the one place he never had to perform, and the mask that was thin at a masquerade was thinner still here, and what showed through it was not warmth and was not rage. It was a kind of stillness. The economical, unwasted stillness of a man who had never in his life needed to raise his voice to be the most dangerous thing in a room, and knew it, and had arranged his whole existence so that everyone else knew it too.

"You poisoned her against us."

No greeting. No preamble. He said it the way another man might open a ledger to the relevant page.

"You found a frightened girl who knew nothing of this city, and you walked her through it, and of everything you could have shown her you chose the wounds. The water rights. The debts. The hunger. You made certain the first thing she learned about her own capital was that the people who kept it standing for twenty years are parasites who ought to be torn out root and branch." A small movement of one hand, dismissing an objection Xion hadn't raised. "I'm not interested in whether you meant to. The outcome is the same either way. You gave the throne a curriculum, and the curriculum was *us as the enemy*, and now she sits up there rejecting every functional thing the Coterie proposes because functional things come from the people you taught her to despise. She would rather the wells fail justly than run unjustly. And the wells are failing, and it is just, and people are dying of the justice of it."

There it was — the thing that made him impossible. Because it was *true*. Not all of it, and not the conclusion he built on it, but the spine of it was true and Xion could not pretend otherwise: he had shown Elara the wounds because the wounds were real and she'd needed to see them, and the sight he'd given her had become a wall she couldn't govern past. Somewhere in the low wards a girl named Sela had come within a spoonful of dying while two councils that both believed themselves righteous argued over who was permitted to change a number in a ledger. The accusation bruised because it was built on something that wouldn't dent.

And the reply was right there too, whole and sharp, sitting behind his teeth. *Not showing her the fountains doesn't make them run any less. And it was you who built them — you and the rest of them, these very ones splashing outside your window as much as any. I only pointed.* He could say it. It was even true. And it would move nothing, because Rosik did not lose arguments — he fed on them, turned every true thing you set in front of him into more ground to stand on, and the one guaranteed result of saying it aloud was the two of them still standing here an hour from now with his father further ahead than when they'd begun. *Don't hand him the argument.* Melina had known. She always knew.

So he said none of it. He said nothing at all, and watched his father not care that he'd said nothing.

Because Rosik hadn't come to relitigate. He'd come to assign.

"So you'll fix it." Flat. Certain. The imperative mood with no softening anywhere in it. "You found her, you freed her, you set her on that throne, and she trusts you — misplaced as that trust is, it's real, and it's the one lever in this city I don't hold and can't buy. So you will use it. You will make her understand that ruling is not the same as tearing down. That governance requires structures, and the structures require the men who built them, and a wise sovereign works with what exists rather than setting fire to it in the name of what doesn't." He turned back a page in the ledger before him, as though the conversation were already most of the way to over. "One week. Make her hear reason, or watch what happens when the Coterie decides it doesn't require her permission to keep a city alive."

*One week.*

Xion felt the whole impossible architecture of it settle onto him at once. The gate that wouldn't open. The letters that came back saying nothing. The Arol Batae who had walked him out with such terrible courtesy and filed him under *non-essential*. Every road to Elara was closed or watched or gutted somewhere between his hand and hers, and his father had just handed him seven days to walk down all of them and arrive.

And he didn't say it. That was the thing he understood standing there, clearer than he'd ever understood it — he could not tell his father *I can't reach her*, because Rosik did not hear the word *can't* the way other people heard it. Rosik heard it as a confession of insufficiency. Say *I can't get past the gate* and the conversation would stop being about the gate in the space of a breath; it would become about whether Xion was capable, and Rosik would sweep the gate aside the way he swept everything aside that stood between him and an outcome — as an obstacle that existed only for lesser men. The obstacle was real. It would not matter. Raising it would cost more than swallowing it. So he swallowed it, the way he'd swallowed the sanitized letters and the closed gate and every diminishment of the last two months, and let the impossible deadline sit on his chest and said nothing.

He did not agree. He did not refuse. He let it hang in the still air between them, unacknowledged, and Rosik — who knew his son too well to expect *compliance*, and had never in his life required agreement to consider a thing decided — let it hang too, satisfied. The instruction existed now. What Xion did with it was Xion's to carry.

Melina had come in at some point and set herself, without seeming to, at an angle between them — a small silent geometry Xion knew from a lifetime of watching her do it, positioning herself in the line of fire the way you'd stand between a fire and a curtain. A glance at Xion that said *don't*. A half-turn toward their father that meant nothing and was meant to mean nothing, a bit of motion to bleed the tension out of the room. She'd been doing it since they were small. It had never once worked and she had never once stopped, and it didn't work now: there was no tension to bleed, because a confrontation requires two people pushing and Xion had refused to push and Rosik had never needed to.

"That's all," Rosik said, and it was — he'd said the thing he'd summoned his son across the city to say, and a matter said was, to him, a matter handled. He was already looking back down at his ledgers before Xion had turned for the door, the clock he'd set already running in a part of the world he considered settled.

---

Melina walked him as far as the garden gate, which was as far as she went, because she lived on the inside of it and he, by his own choice, did not.

"He means it about the week," she said.

"I know."

"Then don't stand here letting me fuss at you — go and start spending it; you haven't the hours to waste." She said it lightly, but her hand had found his sleeve and stayed there. "I'll do what I can from in here. Get into the Coterie's paper, find out what Father can actually do to you inside seven days and how fast, and how much of it is only him being frightening. Give me until morning to know for certain." The lightness thinned to something plainer. "You did an impossible thing once already, little brother. I've not forgotten that. Find me at the wall — tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow."

"Good." She squeezed his arm, once, and was gone back up the swept path toward the flawless house and the tariff subcommittee and the next impossible afternoon of standing between two men who'd made themselves a canyon, and he watched her go until the hedges took her, and then he let himself out through the gate that closed behind him with the soft expensive sound of everything in that place.

He walked back toward the city with a week in his pocket he had no way to spend.

He had put an Empress on the Ruby Throne. He had crossed the whole of Kaha'an on the strength of believing one impossible thing, and he had been *right*, and the being-right had bought him this: seven days, granted by a man who delivered ultimatums the way other men remarked on the weather, to make a woman behind walls he couldn't breach listen to reason on behalf of a system she had every reason in the world to hate. The gate wouldn't open. The letters said nothing.

Somewhere behind him his father had already stopped thinking about it, the matter filed, the outcome merely pending. Somewhere ahead of him was a wall he couldn't climb and a week that had already started counting down.

He kept walking, because walking was a thing he could still do, and thought about how to spend seven days reaching a woman who did not know he was still trying.
