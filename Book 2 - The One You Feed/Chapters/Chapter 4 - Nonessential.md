He knew something had changed before anyone said a word, because the guard was waiting for him.

Not standing his post the way the gate guards stood, eyes moving, weight easy — waiting, specifically, for Xion, the way you wait to deliver news you'd rather not. And it was Deshan on the gate, which made it worse. Deshan had been one of the friendly ones in the first week, a broad quiet Arol Batae with a scar through one eyebrow who had actually walked Xion down to the Empress's study himself once, back when the palace was still half-asleep and no one had decided yet who Xion was. He did not look easy now. He looked like a man who had been given an order he didn't love and intended to carry out cleanly anyway, because that was the job.

"Lord Kemvimore." A small inclination of the head. "There's been a determination on standing. I'm to give it to you rather than leave you wondering, so I'll give it to you straight." He said it like a man reciting something he'd been made to memorize, because he was. "You hold no office and no official position within the household. Under the security protocols as they now stand, the palace precinct is closed to all non-essential personnel. That's the whole of it. I'm sorry."

*Non-essential personnel.* There it was — the exact institutional weight of the thing, and its exact personal one, folded into a single clerk's phrase that had been written by someone who had never met him and applied to him by someone who had. Xion turned the words over and found nothing under them to argue with. That was the trouble with procedure. There was no lie in it to catch. It was simply true: he held no office. He was essential to nothing the palace could name.

"I understand," he said, because Deshan deserved that much, and because there was no version of pushing that ended anywhere but here.

They walked him out. That was the part he would not have predicted would land the way it did — not thrown out, not barred, *walked out*, an escort of two falling in on either side of him at a respectful distance, unhurried, courteous, the way you'd conduct an honored guest to his carriage. No one touched him. No one needed to. They simply accompanied him from the inside of the wall to the outside of it, and the gate did its silent well-oiled work behind him, and the courtesy of it was worse than any hand on his collar would have been, because a hand on his collar would at least have been a fight.

Then he was standing in the street with the palace at his back, and for the first time since the coronation it was truly *at his back* — not a place he was trying to get into, but a place that had finished with him and filed the finishing away.

He had put her on that throne. He had believed — it seemed a child's belief now, standing here — that if he could only get her inside those walls, get the right person into the room where the deciding happened, then the deciding would change. He had staked everything he had, these past months, on the conviction that proximity was power. And here was the answer the world had for that conviction, delivered without malice by a man who had once done him a kindness: you can put the right person in the room, and then the room can close its door, and there is no clause in any protocol that makes an exception for the man who opened it.

He started walking. He didn't decide where. The city took him the way it always took him now, downhill and away, and the strange thing — the thing he turned over as he went — was that nothing had actually been taken from him today that he'd still had this morning. The letters had already stopped meaning anything. The gate had already stopped opening. Kael and Silvanno and Farleen still moved through the city; Melina still met him at the wall. All of that was intact. Nothing had been lost. It had only, at last, been **said** — stamped, recorded, made official — and it turned out that a thing you already knew could still take the legs out from under you the moment someone wrote it down.

---

Farleen was waiting where she always seemed to wait — back from the gate, in the mouth of a side street where she could watch the palace entrance without a single guard's eye ever finding a reason to land on her. She was very good at the geometry of not being seen. He had stopped being surprised by it.

She had the pastry.

Of course she had the pastry — the same twist of date-dark dough from the same stall, still faintly warm, the small fixed point his narrowing world had come to orbit without his ever agreeing to let it. She held it out as he came up, and she did not ask him what had happened, because she was reading it off him the way she read everything, and whatever was on his face had already told her the shape of it. She had read the other thing off him once, months ago — the impossible, lit-up certainty of the man who'd called them all together to swear, against every scrap of sense, that the lost heir to the empire was real, and that he was the one who'd find her. She hadn't needed telling then. She didn't need telling now.

He took the pastry. And something about the taking undid him.

It was not that the gesture was more than it had ever been. It was exactly what it had been in the market square three days ago and on the walk home before that — small, warm, unremarkable, a person handing another person something to eat. It was that everything around the gesture had gotten smaller. The palace had closed. The letters were sand. The map of people who would still stand in a street with him had been shrinking for weeks, and here at the bottom of all that shrinking was Farleen Darfi with a pastry, present, unhurried, needing nothing, and the sheer solid **fact** of her was suddenly more than he could hold at arm's length.

He pulled her in.

Not the way you embrace a friend at a graveside. The way you take hold of the one thing in the room that isn't an abstraction, when every other thing you built your life on has quietly turned to paper. She came against him without stiffening, and she was warm, and she was there — actually there, in a way the gate and the sanitized letters and the golden unreachable woman up the hill were not — and he felt some knot he'd been carrying since the coronation give way against her shoulder.

"It's all right," she said into the side of his head, low and steady, the trained voice, the woman who had walked through worse rooms than this one and come out reading the exits. "Xion. It's going to be all right." But her hand had come up flat between his shoulder blades and it stayed there past the moment the words needed it to, and she did not step back, and she did not let him go.

And then — he could not afterward have said which of them, only that it was him who moved first, him who closed the last of the distance — the space between comfort and the other thing simply stopped being there. His mouth found hers. Her hand slid from his back to the side of his face, and her fingers were cool along his jaw, and she kissed him back without a flicker of hesitation, as though she had answered this question a long time ago and had only been waiting for him to get around to asking it.

It was not a beginning and it was not a mistake and it was not any of the careful categories these long months had built between them. It was just true — the knife and the scar and the pastry and the return and the long dark of the Warrens, all of it collapsing into one warm point on a nothing street while the machinery that had been grinding him down for weeks forgot, for a breath, to keep grinding. He was not thinking about Elara. For once, the part of him that was always thinking about Elara had gone quiet, and he let it stay quiet. Right now there was only this, and it was enough, and it was hers.

"Lord Kemvimore."

The voice was flat, official, and entirely without shame at what it was interrupting — a liveried messenger a few paces off, waiting with the practiced patience of a man used to being the least welcome thing in any doorway. They came apart. The street rushed back in: the noise, the light, the palace standing pale and finished at the end of it, the messenger's incurious eyes.

"Rosik Kemvimore expects you," the man said. "Immediately."

Of course he did. Xion did not even have to work out how his father knew — Rosik always knew, that was the entire principle of Rosik — and the timing was its own message, precise as everything Rosik did: the palace door had shut on him not an hour ago, and here already was the other door swinging open, the one Melina had told him would be the only one left. *You're a man standing outside a lot of closed ones.* He had not believed how fast the last of them would open.

He looked back at Farleen. Whatever was on his own face, he didn't know; hers had settled into something clear — not a brave front, not the flinch of a woman deciding a thing had been a blunder. A small smile, and a certain one.

"Go," she said. "I'll still be here."

So he went.
