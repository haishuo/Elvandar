# Chapter 10: Poison

**Timeline:** Week 15 post-coronation
**Characters:** Xion, Kael, Silvanno, Farleen, Melina
**Emotional work:** Terror, helplessness, and the wrong conclusion — reached badly, by a frightened man, and named as such by the one person who loves them both.

---

## Beats

1. Xion opens the chapter as **the least necessary man in Kaha'an**, and the prose puts him somewhere deliberately trivial: the edge of the flower market, buying nothing, doing nothing, watching a woman argue a vendor down over the price of cut stems as though it were the most important thing in the world — because to her, this morning, it is. The crisis he can see has gone underground into the tokens and the ledger. The crisis everyone else could see is fixed. A man used to being needed doesn't know at first what to do with the feeling of being unnecessary. It sits on him like weather.

2. He sees Kael coming and knows from thirty paces that the weather has changed. Kael doesn't hurry — hurry is a thing that happens to other men — but there is a directness of line through the crowd that Xion has learned to read the way he reads a pulse. Kael crosses the last of the distance and doesn't cushion it, because he decided somewhere on the walk over that there was no cushioning it and cushioning would only waste time. *"Someone tried to kill the Empress."* The flower market goes on around the sentence as if it hadn't been said. The woman gets her stems.

---

3. It comes in pieces, because pieces are all Kael has. A formal dinner at the palace — one of the diplomatic functions the Coterie mounts to show the continent that Kaha'an has a government again, that the men who run it can set a fine table and seat an ambassador and pour a wine worth crossing borders for. Poison: in the food, or the wine, or something she alone touched; the accounts don't agree. But Kael is careful to specify **not a clumsy poison** — not a warning, not a gesture, not the kind meant to frighten. The kind meant to work. Elara collapsed at the table in front of the whole seated hall. She is alive. She is badly hurt. Past that, the gates came down within the hour and took the rest of the truth behind them; everything Kael has he bought from Iron men on the palace's edges in the narrow window before the lockdown closed.

4. **The healer comes up through the terror unsummoned**, and this is how the chapter renders Xion's fear — not as panic but as the wrong expertise arriving at full strength. He hears himself asking questions he didn't decide to ask: what kind, how fast did she go down, at the table so it was minutes not hours, was she seizing, was she breathing wrong or was it the heart, did she keep anything down, *did anyone get her to bring it back up.* Kael doesn't know. Nobody outside the palace does.

5. And that is the wall. Xion knows in the abstract the difference between a poison that stops the breath and one that stops the heart and one that takes the body apart over days; knows the different hands you'd play against each; knows that in the first hour the right knowledge is worth more than any physician's whole career afterward — and every scrap of it is useless, because it lives in him, out here, and she is in there, tended by men whose work he cannot see, question, or correct. He has spent fifteen weeks locked out of a building. He has never until this moment felt the wall as a thing pressed directly against the body of someone he cannot lose.

6. Silvanno arrives within the hour, through the ambient nerves of the Water world, which has its own reasons to be shaking today. He wears it worse than Kael does: Kael goes still under pressure, Silvanno goes bright and quick and thin, his hands not knowing where to be. He has less than Kael has, and what he has frightens him. The three of them stand together in a functioning city and there is not one thing among them any of them can do. *(Background, deliberately unremarked: the Ver'anar — Balisha's old war-magic corps, as publicly known as the Arol Batae and sworn to the same blood — are technically Elara's to call. Nobody thinks of them. A poisoning at a state dinner isn't what they've held themselves apart for, and it hasn't occurred to Elara to ask them for anything either. The absence passes without comment because no one has reason to treat them as a live option.)*

7. The waiting has no shape. Pieces come out of order, contradicting and correcting each other, each bought or overheard or guessed at some remove from a palace sealed like a fist. The dinner was under Coterie auspices. The catering — kitchens, servers, wine — was the Water cartel's, and **Silvanno says so himself**, plainly, the words costing him: it was *routine*, it means nothing, Water has poured the wine at every state dinner for twenty years and would have poured it whoever sat the throne. But routine is not how it will be heard, and he knows that too. By nightfall the Arol Batae have the fact and are turning it over, and the whole city that can talk is already saying the shape of a name.

8. The piece that comes last hollows him out: Elara is alive, and unconscious. Not dead — he makes himself hold that, grips it — but not waking either. Suspended, behind walls, tended by hands he will never get to guide. There is a cruelty in it particular to healers that he couldn't explain to anyone who isn't one: **bad news is an answer.** You can work with an answer, grieve it, act on it. This is a held breath with no bottom, and he has to keep drawing it, hour after hour, not knowing.

9. **Farleen comes into the worst of the not-knowing, and she brings nothing** — and he notices the nothing, distantly, the way you notice a small true fact in the middle of a large terrible one. No pastry, no small kindness, none of the offerings she has carried to him at every gate for weeks. She understood somewhere on her way that there is nothing small that will reach this, so she brings the only thing that will, which is herself. She sits near him and stays. She doesn't tell him it will be all right. She doesn't ask what he needs. She is simply there for as long as he is there, and after a while he finds that the fact of her being there has become one of the only things holding the hour together.

---

10. Kael starts the other thing, because his mind cannot not-start it. Not cruelly — laid down the way he lays everything down, like a man reading a gauge: *"Who. Who had the reach."* And Xion's mind, which has spent the whole day refusing to think of anything but whether she breathes and whether she'll wake, goes where it is built to go. He hates it for going. It goes anyway.

11. **The one piece of the analysis he does properly is Tania**, and it's genuinely good: her people served the dinner, but access isn't the question, access is the trap. Tania Larannas reads the wind and leans into it, and has spent these months quietly deciding Rosik is the wind. She doesn't overturn tables, she **survives** them, and murdering the Empress at her own catered dinner is the single most self-destructive act available to a woman whose entire craft is never being caught on the losing side. The Arol Batae will look at her because her servants held the wine. Anyone who actually knows her looks *past* her — to who could reach **inside** her operation and turn her own servants into a weapon she never authorized and would never have chosen.

12. **And then — critically — he does not do the rest of it.** The prose is explicit and unsparing about this: he is not careful. Some small watchful part of him knows he isn't being careful and cannot make the rest of him slow down. He does not weigh the others one by one, does not give each of them a fair hearing, does not do the slow honest arithmetic the moment deserves. **He is too frightened for arithmetic.** His mind skips the whole careful middle and lands all at once, hard, on the one shape that fits: the man who said *Fine* and moved to the next item, the man building a government in the shell of a throne, the man who has spent weeks making Elara irrelevant — and for whom, once irrelevant stopped being enough, her death is only the next line to cross off.

13. Kael says the name when Xion doesn't. *"Rosik."* Reading the same signs. Not a question. Silvanno says nothing at all and his nothing agrees — his mother's world is under the cloud, and the only thing that lifts the cloud from Water is the truth that someone used Water from the outside, and there is exactly one man in Kaha'an with the reach to do it. (Note that both of them have their own reasons to want this answer, and neither is examining them either.)

14. The conclusion settles over the three of them not like a discovery but like a **confirmation** — the last stone dropping into a wall they've watched go up since the masquerade. It settles into Xion coldest and heaviest, because it is his father, and because it comes with nothing a man can do about it: no answer to give it, no hand to raise against it. Only the fact sitting cold and immovable in his chest — *his father reached into the palace to take Elara out of the world* — and the knowing of it leaves him exactly as powerless as not knowing did.

---

15. **Melina comes to the wall, not at the hour** — there is no hour today, the rhythm broke with everything else — but to the same low wall in the same quiet square, from the estate direction, walking the same route, because it is where he'll be and she knows it. And Xion sees on her something he has never seen there before. He has seen Melina tired, resigned, torn, worn thin between two men who each think they're saving the city. He has never seen her **afraid**.

16. She says it before she reaches him: *"It wasn't him."* Not defensive — she hasn't the flatness of someone defending. She has the wideness of someone horrified, who spent the walk over watching a thing take shape and came running to get in front of it. She can see his face; she's been able to see his face her whole life; he and Kael and half the city have drawn the line straight to their father and stopped drawing.

17. **Her argument, and it is not a moral one — which is what makes it credible.** She knows what that man is, better than anyone left alive, because she is the one who has spent every day of these months standing between what he wants and what he'll say out loud to get it. He builds. He maneuvers. He makes people **irrelevant** — he made Elara irrelevant, he was *winning*, he had the wells and the story and the whole city thanking him — and a man does not throw all of that on a fire to become a poisoner. And then the distinction that is pure Rosik: *it isn't beneath his morals. He hasn't got morals to be beneath. It's beneath **him**.* He'd sooner die than be the kind of man who does murder in the dark, because that man isn't the man who wins — that man is a thug who got frightened, and Rosik would rather be anything on this earth than frightened.

18. Xion hears her and does not brush it aside; this is Melina, whose reading of a room he trusts over his own more often than he has ever admitted, and whose honesty has never once bent to make him comfortable. His counter is the honest one available to him: knowing what a man **is** isn't the same as knowing what he **did**. Rosik had the reach, the reason, every piece of it, and the only thing standing against all of it is that it doesn't sound like him — and people do things that don't sound like them when the thing they were doing stops being enough.

19. **And she names the actual mechanism, which the reader has just watched operate:** *"You're frightened. That's not reasoning. That's a frightened man who's found somewhere to put it."* It lands, because it is true, and they both know it is true, and **it changes nothing** — because the reach and the reason and the pattern don't stop being real just because his hands were shaking when he saw them.

20. That is the wedge, and neither of them names it or would call it that: not a fight, not a raised voice, only two people who have read the same city off the same wall for ten weeks discovering quietly that they are no longer reading the same page. The asymmetry is what defeats her: **she can tell him it wasn't Rosik; she cannot tell him who it was.** And a *no* with no other name behind it is not enough to turn a man who has already found somewhere to stand. She sees that too. She is too sharp not to.

21. **So she asks for the thing that gets her killed, and she asks for it as a demand.** Don't do anything — not on this, not yet. Give her the chance to bring him the truth of it. She is closer to their father than any of them will ever get; if he did this there'll be a seam and she'll find it, and if he didn't there'll be something else and she'll find **that**. *Let me look before you decide. That's all. Let me look.* Delivered, the prose notes, in the exact register their father uses for demands — and Xion wonders whether she hears it. He says *all right*, and means it in the moment.

22. And then the turn of the knife, which is Xion's alone: he watches her go back toward the estate, already working the problem as she walks, and thinks that she is very likely the most honest person he knows — and that when she looks honestly at what their father has become, **she will find what he has already found.** He does not want that for her. He wants, fiercely and uselessly, to be wrong, so that she won't have to be the one to prove him right. What he feels instead is the wall between what he believes and what she believes, thin as a hairline crack in fired clay, and the knowledge that walls like that widen.

---

23. Final movement: he is alone and the city goes on being fine. The lines move. The wells flow. And somewhere up the hill behind walls that have never once opened for him, Elara lies in the dark with her breath being counted by strangers — poisoned, he is as sure of it as he has ever been sure of anything, by **the man who lifted him onto a horse when he was small.**

24. The litany of what he cannot do, and it is the chapter's real ending. He can't reach her; the gate has been shut for weeks and is sealed now behind a lockdown. He can't put his hands on the one hurt in all the city he would give anything to mend — the single thing he is actually good for, and the walls make even that impossible. He can't carry a name to the Arol Batae, because a name is the whole of what he has and nobody is taken up on the certainty of a man standing outside the wall. He can't confront his father, because he has nothing to confront him with and Rosik has never once bled from words. He can't even learn whether she will wake.

25. He came into this day the least necessary man in Kaha'an, idle in a flower market because the city for once had no wound that needed him. He leaves it **needed — desperately, by the one person in the world he cannot reach — and no less useless for it.** He has never once in his life believed a thing could not be done. Now he sits on a low wall in the failing light with the worst news he has ever been handed and his hands hanging empty — no wound to close, no name to spend, no gate to talk his way through, no plan, no lever, no move anywhere in the world — and understands that this, at last, is the thing there is no doing anything about.

---

**The dramatic irony is the chapter's engine, but the chapter is harder on Xion than the irony alone would require.** He does not reason his way carefully to the wrong answer. He does one piece of the analysis properly — clearing Tania — and then skips the entire middle of it because he is too frightened to do arithmetic. The narration says so at the moment it happens, and Melina says it to his face a scene later. So the reader is not merely better-informed than Xion; they watch him fail at a thing he is normally good at, and watch him know it and be unable to stop.

**This is deliberately *not* "the wrong conclusion must feel inevitable."** An earlier plan had him methodically eliminate Ronas, Sa Ko Ren, the smaller houses, and external enemies here before arriving at Rosik. That version made him look right. The revision makes him look *understandable*, which is worse and truer, and it means his later actions rest on a foundation he had every capacity to test and didn't. Do not restore the methodical-elimination version to this chapter; it would let him off.

**The careful elimination now lives in Ch11**, where Xion asks Elara to audit his reasoning and the two of them work through Ronas, Tania, and Sa Ko Ren aloud. Note what the relocation does: the rigorous version happens *after* the conclusion was already reached, which makes it confirmation rather than verification — and Xion arranges it so that Elara says the name instead of him. Ch10 is the leap; Ch11 is the justification built afterward to stand on.

**Melina's fear is new**, and her case is deliberately amoral. She doesn't argue that their father is too good for murder — she argues that murder is beneath his self-conception, that a poisoner is a frightened thug and Rosik would rather be anything than frightened. That argument is *correct*, and it still fails, because she has only a negative to offer.

**The request is the trigger.** *Let me look before you decide* is the load-bearing sentence of the second half of the book. She goes looking because she asked to, and because he said yes, and because she is the only person positioned to look. Everything in Ch15 proceeds from this beat. Note also that she frames it as a demand in Rosik's own cadence — the family resemblance surfacing in the one Kemvimore who uses it to protect someone.

**Farleen's presence without gesture.** No pastry, no embrace, no comfort offered. The absence of the established pattern is the point: this is past the reach of small things, and what's left is the person behind them. Her value was never in what she brings. It's that she comes.

**The hairline crack.** Not a fight. Two people who love each other, disagreeing with care, about whether their father is a murderer — a question with no compromise position. The reader has been trained across ten chapters of wall scenes to watch this relationship closely, and this is the first scene where the wall doesn't hold them together.
