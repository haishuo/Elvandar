# Chapter 3: Thirst

**Timeline:** Week 4-5 post-coronation
**Characters:** Xion, Sela (a dehydrated child), her mother, a Water cartel tally-man, Melina, Farleen
**Emotional work:** The political dysfunction gets a body count. The paralysis between two incompatible visions of governance is measured in a child in a doorway — and in what Xion has to become to get her a cup of water.

---

## Beats

1. Xion walks, because the gate is no longer worth standing at and the streets are the last part of the city that still feels like his. He went up to the wall that morning out of habit rather than hope, handed a letter into a courier's gloved hand, was assured the Empress trusts he is in good health, and then turned downhill and kept turning downhill — out of the Noble District's watered shade, past the fountains throwing their bright wasteful arcs, into the parts of Kaha'an that do the actual work of being a city. He is not going anywhere. He stopped pretending otherwise around the third street.

2. He reads the city because he cannot stop reading it. Where the fountains end the wells begin, the wells have lines, and the lines have the particular patience of people who have stood in them their whole lives. A woman carrying a jar with both arms curled around it, the way you carry a thing worth more than its weight. A tally-man at a wellhead with the Water cartel's mark on his sleeve, checking tokens against a ledger before he'll let the bucket down. The ordinary machinery of thirst, running exactly as it always has.

3. He almost walks past the girl — and that is the cruelty of it done slowly. It doesn't announce itself the way a wound does; a wound has blood and urgency and a crowd. This is only a child sitting very still in a doorway out of the sun, and it is the **stillness** that turns him on his heel before he's finished deciding to. Children her age don't sit that still. Not the living ones.

4. The assessment, done through his actual instrument. Seven or eight, though hunger and heat make it a guess. Eyes open, sunken, ringed in bruised grey, moving to his face slowly as though his face arrived from a long way off. Lips cracked and split at the center. He pinches the skin on the back of her hand and the fold stands up before it sinks — a tent of skin that should have flattened at once and doesn't. He asks her name in the voice he keeps for frightened ones. Her mouth works; nothing comes.

5. The mother arrives out of the dark of the doorway with the particular shame of the poor caught needing something — hands hovering, wanting to snatch the child back from the stranger and not daring to, because he clearly knows what he's doing and she clearly doesn't know what else to do. *She was fine yesterday, she was just tired, I thought she was just* — Xion cuts it off flatly, because it's true and because she needs to hear it in a voice that isn't asking anything of her: **you did nothing wrong.** The jar just inside the door has a hand-span of water in it, rationed by the mouthful because it has to last. The allotment was cut again after the coronation, and everyone swore it would get *better* — and then the mother stops herself, because you don't say things about the cartels aloud, not even now, not even with a new empress on a throne somewhere up the hill.

6. **The chapter's hinge, and it is not a medical act.** He is not going to take a dying child's water out of a dying child's house. He looks back the way he came instead: the wellhead on the corner, the tally-man, the bright ordinary fact of a full cistern not forty paces off. The girl is dying of **permission**, not of drought. The water was there the whole time.

7. He goes to the tally-man, and the tally-man's practiced irritation holds until something in Xion's face catches, turns over, and resolves into a name. *...Lord Kemvimore.* He actually straightens. Xion hates hearing it and always will — because Master Fen can read the girl's pulse and count out spoonfuls and coax a body back from the lip of drought, and Master Fen cannot get one cup past this man's ledger. Only the other one can do that. Only the name he has spent years trying to set down.

8. So he uses it. A full allotment for that house — this week, next week, the month, if he takes it into his head to come back and check. Put it on House Kemvimore. The tally-man protests that the ledger is set for the quarter, it's by token, he can't just — and Xion says *you can*, not loudly, not as a threat, with the flat certainty of a man who spent his whole childhood watching exactly how far that name could shove a thing that wasn't meant to move. Give them the name if anyone asks; the name will be enough, it always is, and they both know it always is. Two jars come to the brim and no ledger is checked, because *no* is a word for people without the right name — and whatever else Xion has managed to throw away, the name has turned out to be the one thing that won't come off. *(First of the book's demonstrations that the instrument he despises is the instrument that works — and it is only available to him because he has not been disinherited yet. See the footer.)*

9. Then the slow work, done right. A body this dry given the jar all at once will only heave it up again and lose what little it has — the worst mercy there is. He wets her lips, then spoons it into her a mouthful at a time with a slow count between each, fingers at the pulse in her wrist reading the thin fast thread of it, talking the whole while about nothing — the weather, the color of the door — anything to keep those far-off eyes coming back to his face. By the tenth spoonful the pulse steadies a fraction. By the twentieth she is holding the cup herself, weakly, which is the thing he has been waiting for: the body remembering it wants to live.

10. It takes the better part of an hour. He leaves the second jar full and what coin he can spare, and tells the mother how to go on through the night — small and often, find a healer if the child stops keeping it down. He does *not* say *find Master Fen*. Master Fen has a name people know now, and a name people know draws a crowd, and he hasn't been that man in weeks. But the hands don't ask whose they are. The hands only do the work.

11. Standing in the doorway afterward with his knees complaining, he does the arithmetic of scale, and it's the argument the whole book runs on: he steadied this one. He has a name that can pry a well open and a purse for the rest, this once, for this doorway. He cannot buy a jar for every doorway in the low city any more than he has hands enough to reach every still child in it — the purse has a bottom, the hands have ten fingers, the doorways have no end. What put the child there is not a thirst his coin can reach or a fever his hands can break. It is a number in a ledger, and a room up the hill where two councils that both mean well argue about who is allowed to change the number, and change nothing — and the not-changing has a weight, and the weight comes down here. He walks out of the lower city carrying it.

---

12. The low wall, second appearance. Same quiet square, same swept waterless fountain, same stretch of sun-warmed stone. He gets there first again. Melina comes across from the direction of the estate already halfway into a grievance about the tariff tables and what she intends to do to them with a hammer — no real heat in it, a complaint aired rather than acted on — and then she looks at him properly and stops. *You've been down in the wards. You've got the look.* All he gives her is: there was a girl, she'll live, this time. He finds he can't elaborate.

13. Melina's report, delivered fast because she's decided quick is the kindest way. Elara went for the whole thing this week: not reform, **abolition** — hereditary water tokens struck out root and branch, the entire distribution framework gone. The Coterie killed it.

14. And then the part Melina insists he hear from her because he'll hear it wrong from everyone else: **they weren't lying about why.** You can't strike out the framework overnight; it's the thing the wells actually run on; nothing is built to catch the city if you pull it, and a city doesn't wait politely in the rubble while someone invents the replacement. Rosik said five years to do it safely, and he isn't wrong about the five years. Nobody down in Xion's wards will believe that, and it's still true.

15. The alternative, offered the same afternoon: keep the framework, fix the allotments inside it — reweight the quotas toward the low wards, tighten logistics, stop the tally-men skimming. It isn't justice. It keeps every rotten thing about the system in place. It would also have put water in that girl's house by the end of the month. It would have saved people. Real ones. This season.

16. Elara rejected it, and Melina can articulate exactly why without agreeing to like it: because the day the token system starts working a little better is the day it becomes impossible to tear out, and she is not on that throne to make the cage more comfortable. Melina's summation is the chapter's thesis and she refuses to take a side in it — *Father is right that you can't pull the framework without killing people, and Elara is right that fixing the framework means the framework wins, and both of them are right, and they cancel to nothing, and the nothing is what's standing in that girl's doorway.* Neither of them says anything for a while. A gull up from the harbor picks its way across the dry fountain looking for water that isn't there.

17. Xion asks how their father is, *actually*. Melina considers how to answer, which is itself an answer. He believes it. He isn't obstructing Elara to keep his pile — he is, he'd never let the pile go, but that isn't why he sleeps at night. He lived through the Rending. He watched a city with no framework at all and watched what filled the empty space, and it was worse than anything the tokens do. Everything he built he built on the memory of that, and when he looks at Elara he genuinely sees a young woman about to knock out the one wall holding the roof up because she doesn't like the wall. Melina's verdict is exact and unsentimental: he's wrong — the roof has been crushing people the whole time, he just can't see them from where he stands — but he isn't lying and he isn't a fool, and she is so tired of people needing him to be one or the other.

18. Xion looks at the empty fountain and finally does the arithmetic he has been not-doing since the doorway, and the counterfactual is what makes it unbearable. He told Elara the truth, and there's no untelling it. In another life Princess Elara comes to the throne knowing only what a princess is shown — the fountains, the ceremonies, the clean version of the city — and takes Rosik's improved allotments with gratitude and calls it progress and puts water in that girl's house by the end of the month. Instead Xion walked her through the wards himself: the tally-men, the fabricated debts, the exact machinery of who is allowed to drink. He did it because it was true and because she needed to see it to be worth anything up there, and he would do it again.

19. And it worked. She sees it now — sees it so clearly that she cannot touch the one thing that would have helped, because touching it means blessing it. **He gave her sight, and sight became the cage.** The girl in the doorway is, not solely and not simply but really, along one thread among many that his own hand helped spin, the cost of a truth he still cannot bring himself to regret. He is a man who sees causal threads; that's the trouble with him; he can follow this one all the way back to his own mouth.

20. Two things Melina hands him on her way out. First, an accurate read he doesn't want: *stop deciding Elara is rejecting you — I can see you've decided it, it's all over your face. Nobody in that palace has time to reject anybody; there's no room in there for anything that isn't the fire. When she comes up for air, she'll come up to you.* Second, a plan of her own: she means to have another run at Elara next session and get her to take Rosik's allotment fix as a stopgap — just this season, just while the wells are killing people, sworn blind not to touch her abolition. A truce, not a surrender. If she can make it sound like the first rather than the second, Elara might swallow it, and the girl has water by month's end. She rates it a long shot; Elara is stubborn as a locked door. *Ask me next week — I'll know if I got anywhere.* They set next week, and the cadence has now varied twice.

21. She goes back across the square toward the estate and the next session. Xion sits on a while with the empty fountain, holding the two arithmetics she handed him — the council's and the doorway's — and finding no sum that comes out clean.

---

22. He tries, after, to reach the others, and it surprises him how hard it has become. Less than a year ago — it feels like a different century — reaching his friends meant walking into the Amber Leaf and finding them already at the table, or leaving word with the keeper and knowing they'd drift in by dusk. Now Kael's freedom of movement is bought with Iron business and spent on Iron's schedule, not Xion's; you don't summon a man whose cover is that he has somewhere else to be. Silvanno is harder still — a second visit in a week stops being an accident and starts being a pattern, and patterns get reported. The casual gravity that once pulled the four of them to one table is simply gone; reaching anyone now costs cover, timing, and a reason that will survive being overheard. He stands at the mouth of a street he'd once have crossed without thinking, understands the crossing now costs more than it's worth, and doesn't make it. The outside is a lonely place — quiet in a way the inside never is, and the quiet is not restful.

23. He finds a step in the shade at the edge of a little market square — lower than the wall, humbler, a place with no history in it — and sits down because his legs have run out of anywhere to take him.

24. Farleen finds him there. He doesn't hear her come; he never does. One moment the step is empty and the next she's folding herself onto it, close but not touching, holding out the twist of date-dark pastry from the stall by the old fountain, still faintly warm, as though she timed it. *You didn't eat.* Again not a question.

25. What makes this appearance different from Ch2's: there is no intelligence in it, in either direction. She doesn't want any and isn't carrying any. She simply worked out where his feet would run down to — the way she works out everything — and came and sat in it with him. Nobody followed her. Nobody ever does. The great watchful machine that tracks every step Kael and Silvanno take doesn't spend a single eye on Farleen Darfi, so the one person in Kaha'an who can simply **appear** beside him, needing no cover and leaving no pattern, is the one everyone agreed to forget.

26. The quiet. The market winding down, a man stacking crates, two children chasing each other between the stalls, one shrieking with the particular joy of a child who has had enough water and doesn't know it is a kind of wealth. Farleen watches the square with the habitual half-attention that never fully switches off, says nothing, and does not need him to be all right.

27. Somewhere in the middle of it, without deciding to, he smiles. Small, tired, and it surprises him more than it would surprise anyone watching; he can't remember the last one. It costs nothing and fixes nothing — the girl is still in her doorway, the wells still have their lines, Elara is still behind a wall of protocol he cannot carry one true word through.

28. Final beat, and the comparison is the point: he thinks of Elara up there in the golden middle of it, unable to do the one small human thing happening right now on a stone step at the edge of a market — sit beside someone, be quiet, let the weight come off for the length of a pastry. The crown won't allow her that. Not because she wouldn't want it; because the throne he put her on doesn't have a step like this one anywhere inside its walls. Farleen *can* sit here. She is sitting here. He finishes the pastry, and the smile stays a moment longer than the reason for it, and neither of them says anything at all.

---

**The name as instrument:** The tally-man beat is the load-bearing one and it must not be softened. Xion gets the water not as a healer but as a Kemvimore, by leaning on exactly the machinery he despises, and he knows it while he's doing it. It also quietly establishes the resource he still has and will lose: he is not disinherited yet, and the name is why *no* isn't a word that applies to him. Ch16 takes this away, and this scene is why that theft costs something concrete rather than symbolic.

**Melina attachment work:** She's genuinely distressed about the water crisis — feeling it, not performing it. She defends neither side, refuses to let Rosik be flattened into a villain or a fool, and is actively trying to broker the stopgap herself. The reader sees a woman smarter than her circumstances allow her to be, trapped between two truths she can't reconcile, doing the work anyway. Her stopgap plan is also plot: it's a thing in motion that has not resolved.

**Moral complexity seed:** This chapter is why the heist won't feel heroic. Xion's eventual decision to break the Coterie is born here — the system can't be reformed and the paralysis is killing people. But the reader also sees that Rosik's incremental approach *would have helped*. The question isn't who's right. It's what kind of rightness you can live with.

**Farleen's pastry (second appearance):** The pattern deepens, and the contrast does the work. Kael can't come. Silvanno can't come. Melina had to leave. Farleen is here — and unlike Ch2 she brings nothing operational, which is precisely what makes it land. The reader registers what her presence means because of everyone else's constraint, and the chapter closes by measuring it against the one thing the Empress cannot have.
