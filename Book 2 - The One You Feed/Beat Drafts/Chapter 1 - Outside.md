# Chapter 1: Outside

**Timeline:** Week 2-3 post-coronation
**Characters:** Xion, Melina
**Emotional work:** Hope curdling into frustration. The new status quo is bureaucratic exclusion, not dramatic confrontation.

---

## Beats

1. Xion stands outside the palace gates. Not for the first time — this is becoming routine. The Arol Batae guard on duty is polite, professional, and completely immovable. There is no hostility. There is procedure.

2. Xion tries the approach that worked yesterday, or the day before — a message for the Empress, a request for an audience, whatever variation he's been cycling through. The guard receives it with the same respectful non-answer. The message will be delivered. Lord Kemvimore is welcome to wait.

3. While waiting, Xion reflects on how different the first week was. He was inside those walls. He helped Elara navigate her first meetings with the Coterie — explaining who could be trusted, who was positioning, what the gestures and protocols meant. He felt useful. Necessary. That access is over now. Security protocols are formalizing. "Lord Kemvimore" has no official title, no position, no reason to be in the palace beyond the Empress's personal preference — and personal preference is losing ground to institutional procedure.

4. The gate does not open. Xion eventually steps back from it — not leaving, but acknowledging the wall. This is the door of the chapter's title: not locked, not barred, just... closed. Politely. Definitively.

(Background texture, not a scene beat: somewhere behind those walls, technically Elara's to command, sit the Ver'anar — Balisha's old war-magic corps, as publicly known as the Arol Batae and sworn to the same blood. Nobody is thinking about them. Not Xion, standing outside a gate the Arol Batae are guarding. Not Elara, who was raised by the Arol Batae and reaches for them the way you reach for family, not a relation you've only been told about.)

5. Melina finds him at a spot away from the palace gates — a low wall near a courtyard, or a bench in a quiet square, somewhere between the Noble District and the rest of the city. She's coming from the palace, not going to it — she has access Xion doesn't, through Rosik's position on the Coterie. She sits beside him. This is the first time. It will not be the last. The reader doesn't know yet that this spot, this routine — Xion arriving first, Melina coming from the direction of the estate or the palace, sitting where she sits — will become the most important structural fixture in the book. For now, it's just a sister finding her brother. She looks exhausted. Not the theatrical exhaustion of someone performing stress, but the genuine fatigue of someone who has been mediating between incompatible forces all day.

6. Sibling greeting. There's warmth here — these two care about each other — but also the slight awkwardness of people whose lives have diverged. Melina stayed in their father's world. Xion left it. They meet in the gap.

7. Xion asks about Elara. Melina gives him the political picture, delivered not as a briefing but as a sister who's tired and worried. The Coterie has assumed governance as the natural continuation of twenty years of ruling "in the Valanar name." Rosik has positioned himself as de facto Prime Minister. The machinery of government runs through cartel infrastructure because there is no other infrastructure.

8. Melina describes the paralysis: Elara proposes reforms; the Coterie objects on grounds of practicality, timeline, economic disruption; nothing moves. Her specific assessment, the line that captures her exhaustion and her Engineer clarity: "She proposes things that would take years. We propose things that would actually help people now. She rejects everything. Nothing gets done."

9. Xion pushes — what does Elara actually want? Melina explains: Elara wants to dismantle the cartel system entirely. The Coterie wants to reform within it. Neither will accept the other's framework. The result is a government that governs nothing while two sides argue about the architecture of governance.

10. Xion feels the weight of this. He told Elara the truth about the cartels in Book 1. He was right to tell her. But the truth has become a cage — she can't work with the Coterie because she sees them (correctly) as the problem. But she can't govern without them because they *are* the governance. The seed of the book's central dilemma: doing the right thing can still create terrible consequences.

11. Melina shifts to something that bothers her on a more personal level. Rosik has been in contact with Ronas Dermir. Not the political contact — everyone knows the Slavers cartel exists — but something closer. "Some of Ronas's people... they're not like him. They're worse." She says it casually, the way someone names a threat they haven't fully processed yet. First Ronas seed planted.

12. Xion registers the warning but doesn't pursue it — he's still focused on Elara, on access, on the door that won't open. Melina sees this and doesn't push. She knows her brother. She's delivered the information. Whether it lands is up to him.

13. They discuss what Xion plans to do. He doesn't have a clear answer. Go back to the clinic? Wait for Elara to summon him? Try the gate again tomorrow? The options all feel inadequate. He put Elara on that throne. He should be in there, helping her use it.

14. Melina suggests he talk to their father. Not with enthusiasm — more like someone recommending medicine that tastes terrible. Rosik wants to see him. Xion deflects. The conversation doesn't resolve; it just trails off, the way conversations between people who love each other but can't help each other often do.

15. Melina leaves — she has somewhere to be, someone to meet, another gap to bridge. Xion watches her go and recognizes something he hasn't articulated: Melina is holding things together that want to fly apart, and she's been doing it for years, and she's tired.

16. Xion turns back to the palace. The gate is still closed. The guard is still polite. He leaves.

17. Final beat: Xion walks through the city. The streets of Balisha around him — the contrast between the palace district and what lies beyond it. He passes through the transition zone where wealth gives way to need. The healer in him sees the symptoms everywhere. The reformer in him knows the disease. The man who put an empress on the throne stands outside her gate and wonders if he cured anything at all.

---

**This chapter establishes the meeting pattern** — Xion and Melina, a specific rhythm of encounter that will recur across the book. The reader needs to internalize this pattern so that its absence in Chapter 15 is devastating.

**Melina attachment work:** She's sharp, she's tired, she's trying to hold things together. The reader likes her immediately — not because she's heroic but because she's real. She's the person in every family who does the thankless work of keeping people connected.
