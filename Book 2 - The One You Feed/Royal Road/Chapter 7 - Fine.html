He went back to the estate to report a failure, and the strange thing was how little he dreaded it.

There was nothing left to dread. Dread was for the space before a blow landed, and this blow had landed days ago, quietly, in a palace kitchen where a note went into a wall and never came out. He walked the swept paths and the flawless corridors the same way he'd walked them a week before, except that this time nothing in him was bracing, because there was nothing to brace for. He had failed. He knew he'd failed. He was here to hand his father a result, and the result was nothing, and he'd made a kind of peace with carrying nothing in his hands because there had never been any chance of carrying anything else.

Melina met him in the same hall as before, and she didn't look anxious this time. She looked resigned, which was worse.

"He knows," she said, before he could speak. "He's known since about the fourth day. You don't need to build up to it." She said it gently, the way you'd break news that had already stopped being news. "Go in, say the true thing, come out. Don't give him the long version. He hasn't got a use for it, and you'll only hand him more to file."

"Has he been waiting for me?"

"He hasn't been thinking about you at all." She caught the flicker that crossed his face and didn't soften it, because softening it would have been a lie. "That's not cruelty, Xion. That's just where he is. The week's over in his ledger. He closed it days ago and moved on to the next page." A pause, and something tightened at the corner of her mouth. "It's the next page I'd worry about."

He didn't ask what she meant. He was about to find out.

---

Rosik was not alone when Xion came in, and that told him more than anything his father could have said.

A steward stood at the near end of the long table with a sheaf of papers, and Rosik was going through them at the unhurried pace of a man reading something he'd asked for and expected to be useful — names, from the glimpse Xion caught, columns of them, a list of somethings against a list of someones. Rosik did not send the steward away. He simply glanced up, registered his son, and let the interruption cost him exactly as much attention as he judged it worth, which was not much.

"Well?"

"I couldn't reach her." Xion kept it to that. Nothing about the gate or the sanitized letters or the wall with no seam in it. Just the fact, set down flat. He'd understood, standing in this same room a week ago, that his father heard reasons as excuses and obstacles as the confessions of men who weren't good enough — and that the shortest true sentence was the only one worth handing a man like that. "The message didn't get through. I don't know if she knows I tried."

He waited for something. He wasn't sure for what — an accusation, a lecture, the cold precise cataloguing of his insufficiency. Some acknowledgment, even a punishing one, that a father had asked a thing of a son and the son had not done it.

Rosik turned a page.

"Fine."

That was all. One word, and not even a hard one — no weight leaned on it, no edge, nothing for Xion to push back against. It was the sound a man makes crossing a finished line off a list, the small verbal tick of moving on. Fine. The son was given his chance. The son couldn't take it. Next option. And the terrible thing, the thing that sat in Xion's chest all the way back down to the city, was that there was no anger in it anywhere, because anger would have meant Xion still mattered enough to be angry at. You did not rage at a tool that failed to do the job. You set it down, and you reached for a different tool, and you did not think about the first one again.

Rosik was already back in his papers before Xion reached the door. Behind him he heard his father say, to the steward, in the exact same voice he'd just used on his only son — "Larannas first. She'll want to be asked before Ren is, and she'll be right to." — and the door closed on it, and the meeting Xion had dreaded for nothing was over.

---

He learned the rest the way he learned everything now: in pieces, from the outside, brought to him by the one person still standing on the inside.

Over the days that followed, the shape of it assembled — through what Melina saw, through what Kael's clerks overheard, through the particular quality of the quiet that settled over the Coterie's public dealings, the quiet of people who have stopped arguing because something has been decided elsewhere. His father was building something. Not obstructing, this time. Building. Approaching Tania Larannas first, exactly as Xion had heard him say — Water courted before Iron, because Tania would take offense at being second and Sa Ko Ren wouldn't care either way. Then Sa Ko, through the one door that opened her: her disgust at a government that met and met and met and never once decided anything while people died waiting on the decision. Then the smaller houses, the ones who'd been holding their breath to see which way the weight would fall, offered a place to stand now that the weight was clearly falling one way.

And when Xion sat at the low wall and Melina came over from the estate and sat where she always sat, she laid the whole of it out, and it was worse than obstruction, and it was worse because it was reasonable.

"He's not fighting her anymore," she said. "That's the thing you have to understand, and it's the thing that frightens me. Fighting her meant he still needed her — needed her to lose a vote, needed her to be blocked, needed the throne to be a thing you contended with. He's stopped needing her." Her thumb rolled the ring, once. "There's old law, Xion. Imperial law, from before the Rending — provisions for a throne that can't function. Regency, interregnum, an incapacitated sovereign; the machinery for keeping the realm running when there's no working hand on the crown. The Coterie didn't exist when any of it was written — but when the Rending emptied the throne, the Coterie is what filled the gap. Twenty years governing in the Valanar name, under exactly that old authority. Nobody ever repealed it; for twenty years it was simply how things worked. And it turns out a law written for a throne no one sits doesn't much care whether the throne is empty — or merely... ignored."

"He's going to govern around her."

"He's going to make governing around her the normal way of things, one reasonable step at a time, until the day someone notices the Empress hasn't actually decided anything in months and can't remember when she last did." Her voice was very even, the evenness she used when the thing underneath it was not even at all. "He doesn't have to depose her. Depose her and you've got a martyr, a Valanar with the wards answering to her blood and half the city ready to die for the girl who came back. He's not stupid; he'd never. He just has to make the throne ceremonial. She keeps the title. She keeps the crown and the wards and the pretty ancient light. The Coterie keeps the deciding. And in a year nobody can point to the day it changed, because there was no day. There was only a great many reasonable afternoons."

Xion looked at the dry fountain and felt the scale of it settle onto him, and the worst part — the part that made his father genuinely dangerous instead of merely cruel — was that he could not find the place where Rosik was wrong. People were dying of the paralysis. The wells were failing while an Empress and a council canceled each other to nothing. Somebody did have to govern, and of everyone in this city with a hand near the levers, Rosik Kemvimore was, demonstrably, the most capable of actually keeping a desert city of this size fed and watered and on its feet. He wasn't seizing power in the night. He was looking at a broken machine and building the one repair that would make it turn, and the repair happened to route all the current around the woman on the throne. By his own lights it wasn't a coup. It was maintenance.

That was the thing. He was right enough to be terrible.

"What happens to her," Xion said. "Actually. In this."

"Nothing, for a while. That's the mercy of it and the horror of it both." Melina spread her hands. "She won't be hurt. She won't be threatened. She'll be thanked, probably, publicly, often — the Empress whose restoration united the city, long may she reign. She'll sit the throne and wear the crown and open the festivals and put her seal on things that were decided without her, and she will slowly stop being asked, because asking her stopped being how anything got done." A breath. "She'll be queen of nothing, Xion. And the cruelest part is she may not even see the moment it happens, because it won't happen in a moment. It'll happen in a hundred small conveniences, each one sensible on its own."

And there it was, the thing that had been circling him the whole conversation, landing now without either of them having to name it: he knew this. He had lived this. A month ago an apologetic man in blue had walked him out through a gate for the crime of holding no official standing, no title, no reason to be inside beyond the wish of a woman who wanted him there — and the wish had lost, quietly, to procedure. Non-essential personnel. He had been made unnecessary by a system that meant him no harm, one polite protocol at a time. And now his father was preparing to do the same thing to the Empress herself — the same erasure, only lawful, only vast, only dressed in the whole weight of pre-imperial statute instead of a single guardsman's regret. What had been done to him at a gate was about to be done to Elara at the scale of a throne. He had been the rehearsal. She was the performance.

He didn't say any of it. Melina knew it already; he could see that she'd already made the same connection, and had chosen, out of the particular kindness that ran under everything sharp in her, not to be the one to say it to him.

---

"I have to go back," she said, when the light started to go. She always had to go back. "There's a working supper — Father wants Tania relaxed before he asks her the real question, which means he wants me across the table being charming at her, which means I get to spend my evening helping build the exact thing I've just spent an hour telling you frightens me." A short, dry breath that wasn't quite a laugh. "You'd think I'd have found a way out of that particular knot by now. Twenty-six years old and still the family's smile."

"You could not go."

"And leave him with no one at that table who loves him? No." She said it simply, without drama, a fact rather than a wound. "Somebody in that house has to still be a person and not a piece on his board. If I stop, there's no one. I'm not ready to let that be true yet." She stood, smoothing her skirts, already half turned toward the estate and the supper and the long careful evening ahead. "I'll keep my ears open. If the timing on any of it firms up — when he actually moves, not just who he's flattering — you'll know it within the hour. Come find me here in three days — the house should have loosened its grip on me by then, and I'll have more."

"Three days."

"Good." A quick squeeze of his shoulder, and she went, back across the square the way she'd come, toward the flawless house and the working supper and the next round of the impossible arithmetic she'd been solving her whole life. A hard evening's work, the kind she'd rather have skipped and would do well regardless, because doing it well was the only lever she had left on a thing nobody could hold.

Xion watched her cross the square and turn up the street beyond.

His father had stopped arguing. It had taken him until now to see what that meant: an argument is a thing two people are having. You can lose an argument and still be in the room.

He was not in the room.
