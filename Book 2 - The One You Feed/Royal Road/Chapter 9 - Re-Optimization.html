Time, which had been moving in days, began to move in weeks.

That was the first thing Xion noticed, though it took him a while to have a word for it. For two months he had lived the way you live through a crisis — each morning its own event, each gate and letter and meeting a thing that happened at a specific hour and could be counted. Now the hours ran together. There was no single afternoon on which his father won. There was only a process, turning over out of sight, week by week, and the sum of the weeks was that the thing which had been a deadlock for two grinding months became, quietly, a government.

He watched it the only way left to him — from the outside, in fragments. Melina from within the estate. Kael through the seams of Iron business. Silvanno from the ambient weather of the Noble District, which he read the way his mother did. And where in the early weeks the fragments had assembled into a picture of gridlock — everyone straining against everyone, nothing moving — now they assembled into a picture of momentum, and momentum all running one way.

Rosik had Tania. Not bought, not threatened — convinced, which was worse, because it meant she'd looked at the ledger and agreed. He had the smaller houses, who went where the weight went. And, most tellingly of all, he was starting to have Sa Ko Ren — the one member of that whole council Xion would have sworn couldn't be had, the Terinok woman who cared nothing for maneuver and everything for whether a thing was true. She wasn't enthusiastic. By every account she still looked at Rosik like something she'd scrape off her boot. But she had spent months watching an Empress propose beautiful things that went nowhere while people died in the waiting, and Sa Ko Ren, whatever else she was, respected results — and results, at long last, were on offer.

The results had a name. Melina brought it to him at the wall: the Coterie was going to force through a water reform. Not Elara's abolition, not a compromise with it — Rosik's. The improved-allotment plan, the one Elara had refused across a council table months ago because fixing the cage meant blessing the cage. Refined now. Strengthened. And backed by enough votes to pass with or without an Empress's seal.

"The same old statutes," she said. "There's a clause in them for plague and famine — when the dying outruns anything the throne can do about it, those governing in the crown's name may act without the crown's seal. Father's dressed the water reform up as a public-health emergency to reach it." The ring went round her finger, once. "Legally it's a stretch you could drive a wagon through — the clause was written for corpses in the streets, not for reweighting a few allotments the Empress declined to sign. But it doesn't matter that it's thin. They have the votes and the wells and the wagons. They don't need her to say yes; they need her not to say no in time to matter. And she won't — she'll hear of it the way she hears of everything, late, and through a wall."

She was right. By the time Elara understood what was moving, it had already moved.

The reform passed.

And then it did the thing that Xion had not, in his worst imaginings, been ready for.

It worked.

---

He didn't believe it from the reports. He believed it from his feet.

He went down into the low wards — the same descent he'd made the day he found a still child in a doorway, out of the watered shade and into the parts of the city that did the work of being a city — because he could not make himself trust a thing this large without putting his own eyes on it. And the wards had changed.

Not transformed. Not saved. The tokens were still tokens; the tally-men still wore the Water cartel's mark and still checked the ledger before they'd let a bucket down. But the ledger said something different now. The allotments had been reweighted toward the low streets, and the difference was not abstract. The line at the wellhead — the same wellhead where he'd spent his father's name on two jars of water for a dying girl — moved faster than it had, and reached further, and turned fewer people away. A woman filled a jar to the brim without a tally-man's hand on her wrist. Down a side street, children were playing the way children play when they have had enough water to waste some, shrieking, chasing, and not one of them was sitting too still in a doorway.

He stood at the mouth of that street for a long time.

He had bought one girl her life, that day, with a name he loathed and a purse that had a bottom. He had told himself, walking away, that he could not do it for every doorway in the low city, and he had been right — his coin reached one family, his hands reached one child, and the ends of both had been visible from the start. And now, without his coin and without his hands, the thing he despised more than anything in the world had reached down into these streets and done for all of them what he could only ever have done for one. The reform he wanted to hate had put water in the jars he couldn't fill. The system was killing fewer children this week than it had last week, and the reason was his father.

There was no honest way to stand in that street and call it a bad thing. Children were drinking who would otherwise be dying. It was good, and it had come from the hand he'd spent his life trying to strike.

That was the trap of the entire thing, he understood, standing there with the sound of the children in his ears. It wasn't that the reform was a trick. It wasn't hollow, or cynical, or secretly cruel. It was real. It was good. His father had looked at people dying and built the thing that stopped them dying, and it had worked, and every jar filled was a true and unarguable fact — and every true unarguable fact was another stone in the wall of the thing that would go on deciding, long after this crisis was forgotten, which children in this city were worth water and which were not.

Because the tokens remained. The hereditary logic remained — the quiet arithmetic that said your grandfather's standing set the size of your ration. The Warrens still had no allotment at all; no reweighting reached that far down, because the people in the dark below had never been on the ledger to begin with. Nothing had been fixed. Something had merely been optimized — a machine that had begun, visibly, to grind people up, tuned by a master's hand until it ran smooth again and the grinding went back to being invisible. And an injustice you cannot see is an injustice no one has to answer for.

The fever had broken. The disease was exactly where it had always been. And the man who'd broken the fever was, this week, being thanked for it in every ward of the city.

---

Melina was already relieved when she reached the wall, and Xion found he couldn't begrudge her a scrap of it.

"They're not dying," she said, sitting where she sat, and for a moment there was something almost light in her, the first he'd seen in a long while. "You've been down there, I can tell by your face. So you've seen it. The Middle District wells are flowing, the counting-houses have stopped screaming, the — Xion, people who were going to be dead by harvest are going to see harvest. I've spent two months watching everyone I know argue about the shape of the rescue while nobody threw the rope. Somebody finally threw the rope. I'm allowed to be glad the rope got thrown."

"You are," he said, and meant it. "I'm glad too. That's the problem."

"That's the problem." She let out a breath, and the lightness didn't leave her exactly, but it made room beside itself for the other thing, the sharper thing, the thing she saw that the glad half of the city didn't. "Because it's not just that people aren't dying, which is good. It's that Father can now prove his way works. He has evidence. Sworn, witnessed, drinking-it-from-a-cup evidence. And proof, Xion — proof is the single most dangerous thing that man could possibly have gotten his hands on." She looked at him steadily. "Everything up to now, he could only assert. Now he can point. He pointed at the wells and said my way fills them and hers empties them, and the wells agreed with him. How do you argue with a well?"

"You don't."

"You don't. That's what I'm telling you. Nobody does." And there it was, the whole grey weight of it, laid out by the one person who could hold both halves at once without flinching. "He saved lives. That's true. It'll always be true. And he's going to spend that truth for the rest of his life, and every place he spends it, the thing that made those people need saving in the first place gets a little more permanent, a little more reasonable, a little more like just how the world is. He didn't just win a vote. He won the story."

She was quiet a moment, turning the ring.

"And the cruelest part," she said, "is that I can't even want him to be wrong. Because wanting him to be wrong means wanting the wells to stop flowing. And I've stood in those streets too."

She was quiet a moment, and something else surfaced in her then — not the political tiredness but a more personal impatience, the kind she usually kept a better hand on.

"Come back to the house."

He started to say something; she went over the top of it.

"Let me say it once, because I've been holding it for weeks. When you didn't come home after the deadline, I understood it. You'd just failed the thing he set you, and you weren't going to walk back in and let him watch you do it. That's pride, and I've got the same blood, so I understood it." She looked at him straight on. "But that was weeks ago. And the part you won't let yourself see is that he isn't watching, Xion. He said 'Fine,' and he went back to his ledgers, and he has not once wondered where you sleep since. There's no one up there counting how long you can last out here. There's no satisfaction you're denying him, because he isn't waiting on it." A short breath. "So what is it for, now? You're sleeping in a dead clinic to spite a man who isn't looking at you. That stopped being principle a while ago. Now it's just stubbornness in an empty room, and I'm the only one who can even see you doing it."

He had no answer, which was its own kind of answer, and she watched it land and didn't grind it in any further than it had already ground itself.

"I'm not asking you to agree with him. I'd never." She got to her feet and shook the dust from her skirts. "I'm telling you there's a bed in that house that's still yours, and a sister who'd sooner have you across a supper table than up here at a wall, and the only thing standing between you and either of them, anymore, is you." The edge went out of her then, leaving just the tiredness and the fondness under it. "Think about it. That's all. Just think about it."

---

The story turned exactly the way she said it would, and Xion watched it turn.

Within days the shape of the telling had changed across the whole city, and it had changed against Elara. It was not that anyone attacked her. It was worse than that: the facts simply rearranged themselves around a new center, and the new center was that the Coterie had saved the low wards and the Empress had spent months in the way. She had refused this very reform once — everyone remembered that now, everyone said it, she had it in her hands months ago and she threw it back because it wasn't perfect enough. The people who had been dying did not care about the architecture of justice. They cared about water. The Coterie had given them water. The Empress had given them a principle, and you cannot drink a principle.

And then came the part that Xion, hearing it from Silvanno, had to sit with for a while before he could take the next breath.

Elara had to thank them.

Not surrender — nothing so crude. She simply had to stand up, in public, and acknowledge that the reform had saved lives. Because it had, and refusing to say so would have made her look exactly like the thing they were quietly painting her as: petty, ideological, a girl who'd rather be right than let her people drink. So she said the words. She praised the Coterie's swift action in a time of crisis. She was gracious. She was, Silvanno said, note-perfect. And every gracious note out of her mouth was a nail she was driving, with her own hand, into the legitimacy of the system she had come to that throne to tear down. They hadn't beaten her. They'd made her bless them, on a dais, before the whole city, and smile while she did it.

"That's the part I keep coming back to," Kael said, when the two of them walked a length of the Iron district's edge where a conversation could get lost in the noise of the forges. He didn't waste words on outrage; he never did. He laid it out like a man reading a gauge. "It isn't that they won a fight. It's that the ground moved. Two months ago the question in this city was how do we get around the cartels. This week the question is why did the Empress fight the thing that fed us. That's not a setback. That's the map being redrawn under your feet." He was silent a few paces. "Whatever it is you've been circling these past weeks — and don't tell me you haven't, I've watched you circle it — the ground you were going to stand on to do it is getting smaller every week the wells keep flowing. Every week this holds, the answer to should someone break the Coterie gets a little more obviously no."

Xion didn't argue, because Kael wasn't wrong, and because the thing Kael was too polite to say aloud had already taken up residence in Xion's own chest, cold and clear.

Whatever he did now — whatever he had been circling, in the part of him that had been quietly turning since a child nearly died of permission in a doorway — he would not be breaking a thing that was failing. That would have been easy. That would have been clean. He would be breaking a thing that was, this very week, keeping children alive. That was the price now. Not courage against a broken machine, but the willingness to take a working one apart, and to accept the cost of every jar that went unfilled in the taking, in the name of a justice that no one currently dying of thirst had asked him for.

---

He walked home through wards that worked.

The lines moved. The children played. The counting-houses had stopped screaming and the wagons rolled on their schedules and, somewhere up the hill, an Empress who had faced down every power in this city to sit a throne was, tonight, queen of a city that had just learned to be grateful to the men she'd meant to destroy.

Xion could see the machine beneath the calm — could see it the way he'd always been able to see the disease beneath the fever, the token beneath the flowing water, the next crisis and the next and the one after that, all of them coming, all of them written into the hereditary arithmetic that no reweighted allotment had touched. He knew it was there. He knew it as surely as he knew anything.

But knowing it changed nothing tonight, because tonight the children had water, and the water was real, and there is nothing in the world harder to argue with than a thing that is, right now, actually working.
