What he had won looked, laid out, like a great deal of subtraction.

Rosik was gone — not beaten in any way you could point at, only absent, somewhere on a road, moving toward whatever position he meant to hold next. The coalition was scattered. The shadow accounts were seized and already spent. The Grain cartel had come apart at the top, and its pieces were sorting themselves into new arrangements he had no hand in and could not have shaped now if he'd tried. He had spent these weeks taking a thing apart, and taking a thing apart and building one in its place were different skills, and he knew exactly which he had practiced. The old order was gone. Its replacement was months from existing. In between there was only the old thing's own momentum, running on the memory of the man who had wound it.

The shadow-fund money had gone the way he'd meant it to go: into the wards Ronas's men had wrecked, into the hands the wrecking had cost. It was the right thing to have done with it. It was also, in the plainest terms, a large hole opened in an imperial account that could least afford one, at the exact moment a new government was trying to teach itself to stand. Someone, before long, would have to sit across a table from Elara's new men and account for where a very great sum had gone, and why it was rebuilding the lower wards instead of steadying the institutions those men were charged with keeping upright. The cold set the task on the list. It was, he noticed, the first task in a long while that was merely a task — a thing to be managed, and not a person to be taken apart.

Elara was mending from the poison more slowly than either of them had the patience for; the body kept its own reckoning, indifferent to whether the empress considered herself finished with it. The crown did not slow to let her heal. When they were together now it was in the narrow windows the institution allowed — brief, freighted with everything neither of them had found the room to say, shaped always by the plain fact that she was the empress and this was what being the empress was. The heist had opened the palace to him. It had not changed what the palace was. He had more of its doors now than he had ever had, and more doors was not the same thing as being near her, and they both knew it and neither said it. He was glad past any words he owned that she was alive. He told her so in the ways that did not need words. She told him back the same way.

"We've a few months in the old arrangement, maybe," one of them said, in one of those windows — it hardly mattered which; they had been finishing each other's hard sentences for the better part of a year. "We'll think of something." It was not comfort. It was the flat estimate of two people who had done difficult things before and expected to do more of them. The Coterie was broken and something would grow where it had stood. The city ran on inertia and would a while yet. They would think of something. That was what hope looked like now.

And Farleen was still here.

She had been here through the nights the palace could not be, which was most of the nights. A woman with no post, no title, no institutional reason to stay anywhere — and she stayed. She did not ask him to explain himself, or to be better, or to feel the things that were not yet in reach. She sat with him in rooms that held the flat silence of things that had ended, and she was present the way a person is present when they have decided their presence is a fact and not a question you get to argue. She had watched him, from close enough to see all of it, through the two days it took to take a coalition apart. She had not told him what she'd seen. She was waiting, he understood dimly, for the version of him that would be able to hear it.

---

He had been walking. The cold wanted occupation, and with the operations finished, walking would serve. He went through the Noble Quarter and down into the Middle District along routes he knew by heart — he had traced every one of them across these months, carrying messages, keeping off the notice of the wrong eyes. The street that let out by the fountain. The alley through to the cloth-merchants' row. The wider way past the second market. He did not go near the quiet square with the low wall. He did not think about not going near it. His feet simply took the other way, every time, and he let them, and did not ask why.

The tasks were done. Not all of them — the administrative work would grind on for months, an empire not being a thing that ordered itself — but the ones the cold had been set against since the afternoon on the wall were finished. Rosik, gone. The coalition, gone. The money, moved. The rebuilding, arranged. The cold reached for the next item and closed on nothing. The machinery kept turning. It had gone warm with the turning, and there was nowhere left to send the heat.

---

One afternoon in the middle of the week he walked with no route at all. He had come from a man who had told him something; he had heard the words and let them pass without holding any of them, and then he was walking, because his body wanted a thing to do while his mind went, at last, to the place it had been trying to reach for weeks and never once been let. There had always been a next task, and the next task had always arrived to turn him aside before he got there. There was no next task now. Nothing turned him aside.

And what he found, when nothing turned him aside, was not his sister.

It was laughter. His father's — the uncontrolled kind, the kind that gets out of a man before he can decide whether to allow it. His father's study, the wrong hour of the day, some joke that had landed badly and then, a beat later, landed perfectly, and his father's face doing a thing his father's face was not built to do. And beneath it, older: his father's hands lifting him onto a horse much too tall for him, and one of those hands staying, flat and certain, against his back, holding him steady at an age when he had still needed holding and had not yet learned to be ashamed of needing it. Warmth. Plain warmth, with no ledger anywhere beneath it.

The cold had nowhere to put this. The cold had been built to fight a threat, and the threat had been real — the opponent real, the harm the opponent did real, every reckoning it had run against him sound. But the opponent had also been the man whose hand had held him on that horse. The memory came from an angle the cold had never been made to guard, because it had never been a threat, and so it came straight through — and behind it the door it had been holding shut for weeks swung open. And he understood, with a clarity that cut worse than grief, that the thing he had spent all these months at war with had never been only a man. It had been a man he had once loved without condition, and had never entirely managed to stop.

He was not watching where his feet went. He was inside the horse and the hand and the laugh, and inside there was nothing the cold could sort or file or turn to use, and while he was in there his body walked the road it had walked so many times. Left at the fountain. Right beneath the baker's overhang. The second-market road, and left again, and the tree that leaned over the low wall.

He came back to the surface already sitting down.

His body had done all of it without him — the turn into the square, the crossing, the settling onto the warm stone in the usual place — the old routine his muscles still carried: come to the square, sit at the wall, wait. So he sat, and he waited, placidly, almost at peace, the way you wait through a thing your body has done a hundred times, and he looked to the estate direction — the path she always came by, close enough to walk, never so close the meeting felt as though it happened on Rosik's ground — and he waited for her to come around the corner the way she always came.

The estate direction was quiet.

She did not come around the corner. She did not cross the square from the estate side with her particular unhurried step. She was not going to. Not today, not any day — and it reached him now not as a fact he had been given in careful language and understood in every sentence, which it was, but as the plain bodily truth of an empty path where a person should have been and never would be again. He had known it for weeks. He had never once sat here and let it be true.

And the cold broke.

Not all at once — the way a wall goes, which is from the inside, out of a fault that was there long before any of it showed on the surface. Weeks of grief shut away and fed to a thing that turned it into precision. The precision was spent. The engine had nothing left to run on and nowhere to send what it had been burning, and so it stopped — and what it had been standing on all this while was still there, and had been the whole time, waiting. Not the operator. Not the healer. Not the cold clean son who had unmade his father's world in three weeks and felt nothing while he did it. Only a brother, on a low wall, in a square that did not care, who was never going to see his sister come around that corner again.

He put his face in his hands, and he wept.

---

He did not hear her come. He did not know how long he had been there, or how she had known to look, or whether she had been somewhere near for days or had only guessed the one place his feet would finally betray him to. It did not matter, and he did not ask. She came around the corner of the square — not the estate direction; her own — and she saw him at the wall, and she crossed to him, and she sat down beside him with no space left between them, and she did not say anything.

There was nothing that needed saying. She put her arms around him, and he did not stiffen and he did not pull away; he turned into her, and she took the weight of it, and gave him her shoulder, and held on.
