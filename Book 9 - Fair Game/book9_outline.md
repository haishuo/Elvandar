# Book 9: Fair Game — High-Level Outline

**Status: in planning.** *(Folder created 2026-11-21 on the author's direction.)* This outline holds what has
been settled or proposed for Book 9 so far; anything marked *proposed* or *open* is not yet ruled. The series
outline and the Book 8 outline were updated for nine books on 2026-11-21. The title is a working title.

**Narrative Engine:** Uratha's civil war. After Book 8 the Council fails to remove Leowin Draskin, Kandor
Belus tries to arrest him with a small strike on the Ivory Tower, the strike fails, and Draskin declares him
in rebellion. **The man who wanted to avoid war is the one who starts it.**

**POV:** Mainly Kandor Belus (`People/Kandor Belus.md`), on the model of Book 4, which is told entirely from
Harkim's. Whether it is strictly single-POV or carries a secondary thread is open.

**Timeline:** Begins after Book 8, following the failed vote. Length not set. The saga measures in months.

**Core Theme:** *Not yet set.* The title points at it: whether a contest can be fair when one side keeps every
rule and rigs the result.

---

## WHY THIS BOOK EXISTS

**The material was already there.** [The Failed Vote Against Draskin](../Offstage/The%20Failed%20Vote%20Against%20Draskin.md)
ended on a powder keg: four votes to remove Draskin and none to keep him, and he stays; the Ilhamori sent to
Belus; an amendment Draskin cannot pass. Either that is a book or it is a large loose end.

**It is Uratha's book, not the Valanars'.** Book 8 stays the end of the Valanar story: *the torch passes* is
still true, and nothing in this book undoes the parents' final adventure. **Book 4 and Book 9 bracket the
twins' books**: Uratha's war seen from the ground through a junior scholar, then Uratha's war seen from the top
through its War Provost.

## THE OPENING: THE STRIKE ON THE IVORY TOWER

*(Author's ruling, 2026-11-21. Moved here from `Offstage/` once the book existed to stage it.)*

**What comes before it is Offstage.** The vote has failed and the two-year prohibition bars another motion.
**Belus proposed that motion**, and the other four Towers joined him, so the lawful route was his and so was its
failure. Draskin has begun moving toward an amendment requiring all seven Provosts to vote for removal.

**Then the letters.** *(Author's ruling, 2026-11-21.)* Ilhamori officers bring Belus a question from the
Scholar-King, in writing:

> *Do you reaffirm your loyalty to the Scholar-King Leowin Draskin?*

Belus answers, in writing:

> *The Ruby Tower will answer to the Council of Provosts, as it always has.*

**These are the exact words, and they must land as written.** Both letters are civil and correct, could be read
aloud in Council, and could be shown to no magistrate as either a threat or a defiance. **Both men know
exactly what the other has said.**

- **Draskin's** *reaffirm* presumes a loyalty already given, so anything short of yes is its withdrawal. *The
  Scholar-King Leowin Draskin* fuses the office with the man, and loyalty to the man is the real request. It is
  a yes-or-no question, which makes any third answer an answer. **The letter is civil and the couriers are
  not.**
- **Belus** refuses the frame and gives no yes or no that could be quoted. *The Ruby Tower*, not *I*: the
  institution answers, not the man. *Will answer to* replaces loyalty with accountability. *The Council of
  Provosts* is the body that has just voted four to none to remove the man asking. *As it always has* is his
  reply to *re-*: nothing needs reaffirming, because nothing on his side has changed.

**The narration never decodes them.** Belus may take a long time over his reply, and that is all the page
shows. If the reader needs the letters explained, they have not worked.

**Belus concludes that the lawful routes are closed.** Removal has failed on the arithmetic and cannot be tried
again for two years. Prosecution is blocked by the immunity. Recall belongs to a Tower's own faculty, and
Ivory's will not recall its Scholar-King. Draskin cannot pass the amendment against five Towers, but Belus
reads the letter and its couriers as preparation for a purge: accusations, suspensions, vacated seats, replacement
Provosts, and an amendment passed by the replacements. Draskin permanent, and permanently immune. **If he does
nothing soon, he believes, Uratha as he knows it will vanish.**

**What he cannot recognise has a precise shape.** The Ilhamori silenced the Globehall's discoverers in secret.
Draskin is now using the same apparatus **openly, as a way of governing**: sending it to a sitting Provost to
ask about his loyalty. Belus need not see the connection. **Nobody points at it.**

**He chooses the smallest intervention he can design.** A small, fast strike force from the Ruby Tower moves on
the Ivory Tower with a single objective: take Draskin into custody, in irons if necessary, so that he can be
removed and tried by Sapphire. He knows Onyx and Amethyst are implicated too and could retaliate even if he
succeeds, and hopes the strike will be fast enough that Draskin is already under arrest before they can react.
He has no settled view of how the courts would treat it. **He knows he is exceeding his authority as War
Provost and expects to lose his seat when it is over.** He goes anyway, for the Uratha he remembers.

**The strike fails, and on its merits.** The Ilhamori fight the Ruby force off. They are the service that
arrests powerful mages who do not want to be arrested, and in Book 8 they overpowered Xion in the Globehall
itself. The Ivory Tower is their ground, and taking it is close, room-by-room work of exactly the kind they
train for. **Ruby is a war college, not a police force** (`Places/Uratha/Uratha_ The Land of Scholars.md`,
*The Ruby Tower*): it can do an arrest, as marines can do a police action, but it is not better at it than
the people trained for it, and its strength is holding fortified positions, not taking them. **The force was kept small to limit bloodshed, and it is too small to
overwhelm the defenders: the same choice that makes it humane makes it lose.** No leak or betrayal is
established, and none is to be added unless the author places one.

**Draskin immediately declares Belus in rebellion.** The attack gives him what the vote did not:

- **A military emergency.** An armed assault on the Ivory Tower lets the Scholar-King issue temporary orders on
  military service on the authority of the office, without a Council vote.
- **A real treason.** He no longer needs to manufacture the accusations a purge would have required.
- **A divided opposition.** The four other Towers that voted to remove him must now choose between him and a
  Provost who attacked the seat of government. **This is exactly what Melina feared foreign intervention would
  cause** (Failed Vote), and Belus does it himself.

**Whether the declaration is itself lawful is contested.** The constitution gives the Scholar-King emergency
military authority, but suspension of a Provost belongs to the Council, recall to the Tower's faculty, and the
vacating of a seat to a treason conviction. Nothing in it lets the Scholar-King alone declare a sitting Provost
a rebel. **Both sides can claim the constitution, and neither has clean hands.** That is what makes it a civil
war rather than a suppression. And the immunity covers *conduct undertaken under claimed official authority*,
so every emergency order Draskin gives is shielded too, and raises the price of ever leaving office.

## THE TITLE

***Fair Game* carries three meanings, and nobody on the page states any of them.**

- **A fair contest.** What Belus believed he was owed: the vote, the rules, the Council. **Draskin cheated within
  the rules**: lawful notice, timed for Sapphire's absence, with no proxy permitted; then the Ilhamori sent
  through the proper channel, carrying a threat. Belus cannot name a rule Draskin broke and can see that the
  game was never fair.
- **A legitimate target.** What the declaration makes Belus. It is also what leaving office would make Draskin,
  whose whole strategy is to avoid that moment.
- **A game at all.** Draskin *enjoys politics*; for him it has always been a game. Belus's grievance is not that
  it is a game but that it is not fair.

**The tragedy is in the first meaning.** The man who cares whether the game is fair is the one who openly breaks
the rules, and the man who cheated gets to stand on the constitution and call him the rebel. Belus loses the
contest and loses the right to call it unfair.

**Nobody states it.** Not Belus, not the narration. If the words ever appear, once, flat, late, and not from
him as a thesis.

## BALISHA

**Seen from Belus's side, what Balisha does is a foreign power's choice.** The temptation will be to cut to the
palace in Kaha'an. Book 4's discipline is not to.

**Elara and Xion will not aid Draskin.** *(Author's ruling, 2026-11-21.)* His government's "hospitality" is
what they have just come home from. Elara told the Provosts *you've just guaranteed Uratha burns*; Xion was
overpowered by the Ilhamori, who are now Draskin's instrument. **Backing Draskin is not on the table.**

**Whether Draskin is the lawful government is not settled for Balisha by Uratha's constitution.** He holds
office lawfully, on the arithmetic alone; lawful and legitimate have come apart. Recognition is Balisha's
choice. *Proposed reading, not canon:* a government that imprisoned the Empress and Emperor Consort of an
Alliance partner on a state visit has a strong claim to have broken the Alliance first, so whatever obligation
Balisha owes Uratha's lawful government may already be void. **The Tripartite Alliance's terms have not been
checked**, and should be before anything rests on this.

**So the real choices are three**, and none is clean:

- **Back Belus**: a man with a real treason on his record, whose strike split the coalition that might have
  removed Draskin lawfully.
- **Stand aside**: Draskin purges his way to permanent office and permanent immunity.
- **Back the Council, not the man**: the four Towers and the constitution against both of them. The hardest
  to make work, because the Council cannot field an army and Belus can.

**Elara is still Empress.** *The torch passes* in Book 8 concerns who carries the story, not the crown, so
Balisha's decision on Uratha is formally hers. *Proposed, not ruled:* if the twins ever have to hold back a
parent, that is somewhere this family has never been. It must survive rule 2 against Elara's sheet first.

**The twins' old argument must not re-run.** *Tiberian charges, Melina restrains* has already played at Silaris
(Book 5), at *march on Uratha* (Book 8) and after the failed vote. Its premise was that intervention would split
the Towers and hand Draskin an emergency; **Belus has now done both**, so that argument is overtaken. If the
twins appear, they are somewhere new.

## AVAILABLE, NOT PLACED

*Nothing here is decided. These are resources the book can draw on.*

- **Book 4's people.** Harkim and his network, Perrin Halmore, and above all **Neth**, the Ilhamori enforcer
  who turned. A war against the Ilhamori has obvious uses for someone who knows them from inside.
- **The Orb Weaver, from outside.** In this book it is no longer a secret from Tiberian. From Belus's side it
  could be a broker he buys information from and cannot identify, with the reader knowing exactly who it is.
- **Irene's channels.** Uratha has no banking houses of international reach, and a civil war there is in part
  a war about how money moves. Money could reach Belus by routes he never sees.
- **His province.** As a Provost, Belus governs a province in all but name. If he escapes, that is what turns a
  failed strike into a civil war rather than a rounded-up conspiracy.

## Structural Notes

**The order of his failures is the book's spine.** He started with the lawful route; Draskin defeated it
lawfully and in bad faith; he chose the smallest intervention he could; it failed because of the restraint
that made it decent. **Every step is in character, and every step makes the next one worse.**

**His horror at the Globehall is not guilt.** He did not know (`People/Kandor Belus.md`, *What He Knew*). The
Uratha he fights for is its civic culture, and it was real. Do not write his patriotism as self-deception.
