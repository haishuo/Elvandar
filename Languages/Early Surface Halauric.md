# Early Surface Halauric

**Status:** LEGACY RECONSTRUCTION — superseded by the generative-priority ruling and
`Design Plates/Surface Halauric Bridge - Plate 1.md`. This document preserves candidate regional history,
corpus tests, and the protected **Xion** transmission route. Its sound laws and ordinary cognates have no
authority unless recovered through an approved design plate.

## Period and scope

Early Surface Halauric is an author-facing reconstruction. It covers the human community languages that
emerged during the later open period and continued after Hauren sealed roughly two thousand years before
Book 1. It is not one afternoon on which everyone began speaking “Proto-Balishan.” Language shift occurred
in several settlements, occupations, and households across centuries.

The reconstruction uses three broad phases:

| Phase | Approximate position | Social condition |
|---|---|---|
| **Nativizing Surface Halauric** | final centuries of regular contact | Children acquire contact Halauri in bilingual human households; substrate habits become native patterns rather than learner variation |
| **Early Surface Halauric** | around the sealing and first post-contact centuries | Direct correction from metropolitan speakers ends; regional community norms become self-sustaining |
| **Heartland divergence** | well before the Balishan Empire | Coastal Marresonnian and western Vartonnian varieties remain related but regularize different outcomes |

No date in this table implies a clean border. Older speakers, formal schools, trade networks, and copied
texts preserve competing forms. The future Imperial Balishan koine inherits that variation rather than
descending from a perfectly uniform proto-language.

## Notation

- `*form` marks a reconstructed or schematic form, not an attested word.
- `>` means “develops into.”
- `/ /` encloses phonemes; `[ ]` encloses a surface pronunciation.
- `V` means vowel, `C` consonant, `#` a word boundary, and `ə` a reduced vowel.
- Schematic examples such as `*katena` have no assigned meaning. They demonstrate sound shape only and
  must not enter the lexicon by accident.

## The common changes

These changes define the shared human branch. Their outputs may vary by region, but the pressures and
relative order are common.

### SL-1 — lexical stress moves toward the first root syllable

Late Open public speech defaults to penultimate stress. Ancestral Continental favors initial root stress.
In nativizing households, children regularize the human pattern:

```text
Late Open       *ka.TE.na
Early Surface   *KA.te.na
```

Prefixes can remain unstressed, and later compound roots receive secondary stress. The important change
is not mechanically “stress the first written letter”; it is that speakers identify a lexical stem and
anchor stress near its beginning.

Learned, ritual, and repeatedly corrected names can resist SL-1. This creates a durable contrast between
ordinary inheritance and Hieratic recitation.

### SL-2 — unstressed vowels reduce

After the stress shift, post-tonic vowels centralize. Unstressed bound prefixes also reduce where the
lexical root retains stress:

```text
/a e o/ > [ə] in weak post-tonic syllables
/i u/   > [ɪ ʊ] in the same position
e       > [ə] in a weak pretonic prefix
```

Reduction is initially phonetic. It becomes phonemic only after later loss and analogy make the original
vowel unrecoverable. Public names, contracts, and copied formulas retain fuller pronunciations longer than
ordinary speech.

```text
*KAt ena > *KAtəna
*MIr osu > *MIrəsʊ
*re-HUtan > *rə-HUtən
```

### SL-3 — the glottal contrast weakens in vernacular speech

Ancestral Continental does not share phonemic /ʔ/. In ordinary inherited vocabulary:

```text
/VʔV/ > /V.V/
```

The resulting hiatus is then repaired by SL-4. The glottal stop remains visible in copied spelling and is
restored in careful titles, ritual phrases, and Hieratic lessons. Consequently a modern speaker can lose
/ʔ/ in inherited words while pronouncing **Kaha'an** carefully when the capital-title is at stake.

### SL-4 — hiatus is repaired

Once /ʔ/ is lost, and wherever Late Open already permitted adjacent vowels, human varieties select among
three repairs:

```text
front vowel + V  > /jV/ or a fronted diphthong
back vowel + V   > /wV/ or a backed diphthong
like vowels      > one vowel, often phonetically long
```

Stress and register matter. Unstressed sequences contract early. Stressed sequences survive longer and
can create long vowels in surface traditions even though vowel length was not phonemic in the Late Open
public register.

The capital-title supplies the canonical living example. Careful Hieratic transmission preserves the
three syllables and glottal stop, while ordinary speech can pass through the regular vernacular chain:

```text
/ka.ˈha.ʔan/  >  /ka.ˈha.an/  >  /ka.ˈhaːn/  >  /kaˈhan/
Kaha'an           glottal loss     contraction       kah-HAN
```

The last shortening becomes the native Kaha'ani outcome rather than a merely optional casualism. People
who must name their city constantly regularize **kah-HAN**, /kaˈhan/, as the unmarked form. The full title
survives through instruction, fixed ceremony, and foreign reading traditions; using it in ordinary local
conversation can therefore identify someone who learned the written name before hearing residents say it.

### SL-5 — unfamiliar Halauri consonants merge

The substrate lacks shared /θ/ and /ɕ/. Nativizing communities do not preserve them uniformly:

| Late Open | Coastal tendency | Western tendency |
|---|---|---|
| /θ/ **th** | /s/ | /t/ |
| /ɕ/ **x** | /ʃ/ or /z/ in learned-name tradition | /s/ |

These are early isoglosses, not mistakes later imposed on one proto-language. They help identify where a
word entered the continuum. A later form with **th** may be a Hieratic reborrowing even when an ordinary
cognate has **s** or **t**.

### SL-6 — reduced medial vowels delete where the resulting cluster is legal

After reduction, [ə] is vulnerable between consonants:

```text
*KAtəna > *KAtna
*MARəta > *MARta
```

Deletion does not occur if it would create a cluster absent from the receiving community. Different
substrate varieties therefore produce different surface forms from the same contact input. Once children
acquire the cluster directly, it is a native phonotactic pattern.

SL-6 is the main route by which a contact register favoring simple onsets gives rise to the **dr-, br-,
vr-, sk-** and internal clusters later common in the heartland corpus.

### SL-7 — final weak vowels shorten, centralize, and may disappear

Final unstressed **-a, -e, -o** first become [ə]. Western varieties often delete them; coastal varieties
retain them longer in public and mercantile speech.

```text
*KArena > *KArənə > western *KArn
                     coastal *KArən
```

Final high vowels resist deletion somewhat longer. Grammatical analogy can also restore a vowel where a
productive paradigm makes it useful.

### SL-8 — boundary consonants assimilate

When syncope joins consonants across an old morpheme boundary, place or voicing may assimilate:

```text
/n + n/ > /nː/
/t + n/ > /tn/ or /nː/ by community
/m + v/ remains /mv/ where speakers perceive a compound boundary
/r + h/ > /r̥/ after prefix-vowel loss; later /r/ in ordinary heartland speech
```

Later human spelling can represent historical fortis or assimilated consonants with doubling. A written
**-nn-** therefore need not prove that Late Open Halauri had a geminate in the source word.

The first lexical test supplies one case where the doubled consonant does have an older morphological
source: LOL *on-na* /ˈon.na/ “established settlement” becomes coastal *onne* and western *onn*. The
Marresonnian spelling **-onne** preserves the boundary and is later generalized to the toponymic class.
Full derivations are in `Halauric Core Lexicon.md`.

The imperial-cluster test supplies the first coalesced boundary: LOL *re-hutan* /re.ˈhu.tan/ loses the
weak collective-prefix vowel, and /r + h/ becomes a voiceless rhotic /r̥/. Historical spelling writes
**rh**, even after ordinary Imperial Balishan voices it to /r/. This is a regular result of an old boundary,
not a new Late Open onset.

## The early dialect split

### Marresonnian: the coastal contact corridor

Marresonne remains closest to the Great Shaft interface, copied textual traditions, surface interpreters,
and international trade. Its language changes natively but is repeatedly refreshed by public and later
Hieratic forms.

Characteristic tendencies:

- inherited /θ/ usually becomes /s/;
- inherited /ɕ/ usually becomes /ʃ/;
- a learned-name convention maps rare written **x** before **i** to /z/;
- final [ə] survives longer than in the west;
- hiatus commonly produces glides or long vowels rather than immediate deletion;
- full vowels are restored readily in formal and technical vocabulary;
- speakers tolerate inherited and learned doublets without treating one as an error.

This is not “more correct Halauri.” Coastal speech has its own innovations. Its access to textual forms
simply means that an older-looking pronunciation can repeatedly enter beside an inherited one.

### Vartonnian: the western river and market communities

Vartonnian develops farther from daily metropolitan correction and in communities whose compact native
root shapes remain socially strong.

Characteristic tendencies:

- inherited /θ/ usually becomes /t/;
- inherited /ɕ/ becomes /s/;
- medial syncope applies more aggressively;
- final weak vowels disappear earlier;
- new clusters and final stops are preserved rather than repaired;
- compound boundaries remain audible even after the linking vowel disappears;
- old local names and landscape vocabulary retain more obvious substrate material.

This branch supplies a plausible structural base for the river-and-market speech later broadened into
Early Imperial Balishan. Marresonnian merchants and scribes subsequently feed coastal and Hieratic forms
into that koine.

## How the future imperial koine can inherit both

Valan I's coalition does not choose a language from a menu. It brings speakers into repeated institutions:
army camps, river transport, markets, courts, contracts, and record offices. The resulting koine can take:

- stress, cluster tolerance, and much ordinary morphology from Vartonnian speech;
- commercial spelling and documentary vocabulary from Marresonnian practice;
- throne, law, ritual, and learned terms directly from Hieratic Old Halauri;
- military vocabulary from Terinoki contact;
- household, agricultural, and landscape vocabulary from surviving substrate traditions.

That mixture explains why later Balishan can contain open aristocratic names, compact common names, opaque
house compounds, and conservative ceremonial spellings without being four unrelated naming systems.

## Xion: the first complete transmission chain

The author's ruling is **ZY-on**, /ˈzaɪ.ən/. It does not require changing Late Open **x** /ɕ/ or inventing
a longer name.

The most economical route is a learned name carried through the Marresonnian–Hieratic tradition:

| Stage | Form | Development |
|---|---|---|
| Late Open contact/learned | /ˈɕi.on/ | Regular Late Open **x**, two audible vowels |
| Early surface learned reading | /ˈzi.on/ | Coastal proper-name convention maps rare /ɕ/ before /i/ to /z/ |
| Hieratic recitation | [ˈziː.on] | Stress lengthens /i/ before retained hiatus; length becomes conventional in the name |
| Late Imperial/Kaha'ani | /ˈziː.ən/ | Unstressed /o/ centralizes while the school spelling remains unchanged |
| Modern Balishan | /ˈzaɪ.ən/ | Stressed /iː/ diphthongizes to /aɪ/ in this learned-name class |

This is a **traditional learned pronunciation**, not an irregular sound change demanded of every word.
Comparable names can form a small historical class if the books ever need them. The spelling **Xion** is
conservative because it descends through the Hieratic written tradition; the pronunciation is fully
human and modern.

The result makes the old “clipped from a longer name” theory unnecessary. Xion's name is Balishan in the
same way a modern name of classical origin is English, French, or Spanish: inherited by a culture that has
used it for centuries, regardless of the language in which its spelling began.

### What remains deliberately un-derived

- The **-onne** test is complete in `Halauric Core Lexicon.md`; no additional name may be assigned to the

- **Balisha, Hauren, Elara, Valanar, Kemvimore,** and **Hadris** still lack proven segmentation.
- No Bloodsworn, Gunastran, Erulian, Silarian, or Far Eastern form is pulled into this family merely for
  convenience.

## Doublets predicted by the model

The history predicts three-way contrasts from one Late Open source:

```text
ordinary Marresonnian inheritance: th > s, reduced vowels
ordinary Vartonnian inheritance:    th > t, stronger syncope
later Hieratic borrowing:           th restored, full vowels
```

Urathan can then add a fourth form by coining anew from the Hieratic model. Terminal Halauri may carry
a fifth collateral cognate changed inside the sealed city.

No actual lexical doublet is coined here. The prediction will be tested once roots and morphology exist.

## Constraints satisfied by the grammar foundation

`Grammar Foundations.md` preserves this sound history and provides:

- linking vowels capable of reducing under SL-2 and disappearing under SL-6;
- the settlement construction that regularly produces **-onne** from LOL *onna*;
- compounds whose boundaries explain forms like **Kemvimore** without requiring every sequence to be

  real community language rather than a phrasebook.
